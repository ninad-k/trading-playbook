# Split Ratings and the Pricing of Credit Risk: the trader's summary

*A two-stage study finds that averaging Moody’s and S&P ratings generally predicts new-issue corporate-bond spreads more accurately than using either rating alone.*

**Richard Cantor, Frank Packer, and Kevin Cole** · 1997 · Investing, Value & Market History · advanced

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-30. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Cantor, Packer, and Cole study 4,399 U.S. dollar straight-bond offerings issued from 1983 through 1993. Moody’s and Standard & Poor’s assign the same notch rating to 45.3% of the sample; 42.1% differ by one notch, and larger splits are much less common (pp. 8-9, 23). The authors estimate a spread model on 1,994 identically rated issues, then use six rating rules to predict Treasury spreads for 2,405 split-rated bonds: Moody’s, S&P, the higher rating, the lower rating, an equal average, and Bloomberg’s convention (pp. 3-7, 26-27).

Using Moody’s or S&P alone is approximately unbiased but inefficient. Choosing only the higher or lower rating adds material bias. The equal-average rule has the lowest full-sample root mean squared error, 56.8 basis points, versus 59.4 for Bloomberg and 61.1-62.8 for the single-agency, high, or low rules (pp. 12-14, 27). The advantage is concentrated below investment grade: the average rule’s RMSE is 98.9 basis points, compared with 106.3-114.8 for the alternatives (pp. 16-18, 29). In investment grade, average, low-rating, and Bloomberg rules have very similar forecast precision (pp. 15-16, 29).

## Key points

- The design separates model estimation on identically rated bonds from out-of-sample-style prediction for split-rated bonds (pp. 3-7).
- Controls include issuance conditions, maturity, size, callability, underwriting, shelf registration, and industry (pp. 8-12).
- Full-sample high-rating forecasts average 13.4 basis points too low; low-rating forecasts average 12.3 basis points too high (pp. 12-13, 27).
- The average-rating rule remains best across split sizes, although tiny three- and four-notch samples limit precision (pp. 14, 28).

## Actionable rules

1. In empirical bond-pricing models, retain both agency ratings rather than silently selecting one (pp. 4-7, 17).
2. Use the equal-average rating as the baseline for split-rated issues, especially below investment grade; compare lower-rating or Bloomberg conventions for investment-grade work (pp. 15-18).
3. Evaluate rating rules on both bias and forecast error, because an unbiased rule can still be much less precise (pp. 6-7, 12-13).

## Caveats

The paper studies primary offerings from 1983-1993 and excludes several issue types and very small offerings. It predicts spreads rather than returns or a tradable strategy. The working paper is marked preliminary, and the smallest split-size subsamples contain few bonds.

## Who it is for

Credit researchers, bond-model builders, and analysts deciding how to encode disagreements between rating agencies.
