# Understanding the Relationship Between Risk, Reward and Margin/Equity Ratio in Managed Futures: the trader's summary

*AIMA Newsletter article by Winton Capital's founders arguing that a managed futures fund's perceived 'riskiness' is a business choice of margin/equity ratio, not a property of the underlying strategy.*

**David Harding and Georgia Nakou** · 2002 · Money Management & Position Sizing · intermediate

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-5. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A 2002 AIMA (Alternative Investment Management Association) newsletter article by David Harding and Georgia Nakou of Winton Capital Management. It argues that managed futures funds differ from traditional stock/bond portfolios because futures positions require only a small margin deposit (often 1-2%) rather than full capital outlay, which lets managers dial leverage up or down independently of the underlying trading strategy's return/risk ratio. The perceived "riskiness" of a given fund is therefore a business/marketing decision about how much margin-to-equity to run, not an inherent property of the strategy, and the authors argue investors should judge managers by risk-adjusted return ratios (Sharpe/Sortino) rather than raw volatility.

## Key points

- Typical managed futures funds employ 5-30% of assets as trading margin, versus a theoretical maximum of ~100%.
- Because margin/equity is a discretionary choice, two managers running the identical underlying strategy at different leverage levels will show very different volatility while having the same reward-to-risk ratio.
- The **Kelly Criterion** is cited as the theoretically optimal leverage level when an investor's utility is unbiased (equal aversion to a 1% loss as attraction to a 1% gain) and the objective is maximizing long-term compound growth.
- Reward-to-risk ratios (Sharpe, Sortino, or similar) are argued to be the correct metric for comparing managers, since they stay roughly constant across different leverage levels while dispersion measures (like standard deviation) do not.
- Major stock indices can decline 50-75% peak-to-trough over the long run while delivering only 3-7% excess return over the risk-free rate — used as a benchmark to argue well-managed futures programs offer a comparatively attractive return/risk ratio.
- Fee structure interacts with margin level: higher margin/equity accounts tend to pay proportionally more in performance fees, lower margin/equity accounts more in management fees.
- A "Capital Market Line" analogy is used: an investor can combine a fixed optimal-strategy portfolio with risk-free bonds to reach any desired leverage/risk level without changing the underlying reward-to-risk ratio.

## Actionable rules

None given as trade-level entry/exit rules — this is an allocator-level and manager-comparison paper about leverage and fee structure, not a trading system. Its practical takeaway for money management: when comparing two systems or funds, normalize by reward-to-risk ratio (e.g., Sharpe/Sortino) before comparing raw returns or drawdowns, since raw volatility is often just a leverage choice layered on top of an otherwise-comparable strategy.

## Caveats

Written by principals of Winton Capital Management to promote managed futures as an asset class, using Winton's own simulated (not live/audited) trading results as the illustrative example — the authors explicitly disclose the return data is hypothetical/simulated per AIMA editorial policy. Comparative statistics (stock index drawdowns, managed futures margin ranges) reflect data available as of 2002 and the specific 20-year lookback period used.

## Who it is for

Allocators, fund analysts, or systematic traders wanting a clear conceptual framework for separating a strategy's inherent return/risk quality from the leverage decision layered on top of it.
