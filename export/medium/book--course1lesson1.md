# Course #1, Lesson #1: Background of Futures Trading: the trader's summary

*The opening lesson of Jake Bernstein's online futures trading course, covering futures market history, participant roles, and the psychology of getting started.*

**Jake Bernstein** · 2000 · Market Structure & Price Action · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 5 and the introductory sections checked against the note's participant and capital rules. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Lesson 1 of Jake Bernstein's "Basic Training/Getting Started" course (MBH Commodity Advisors, 2000). It is foundational education, not a trading system: it dismantles five common myths about futures (that it's a pure gamble, rigged, insider-driven, economically pointless, or only for short-term trading), explains hedging's origin and function via a grain-farmer example, and defines the three participant categories. It closes with practical starting-out guidance on capital, partnering, and time horizon, framed as decisions to make before opening an account.

## Key points

- Myths addressed: futures losses exceeding your investment is true but reflects speculation, not investment; futures is no more a "gamble" than stocks; regulators (CFTC, NFA, USDA) limit position sizes to curb manipulation; futures stabilize prices rather than serving no purpose; position traders successfully hold intermediate/long-term futures.
- Hedging explained via a grain farmer locking in a sale price through a forward contract or by selling futures.
- Three participant categories: Producers (lock in a sale price), End-Users (lock in a purchase price), and Speculators (the largest group; provide liquidity — the text notes perhaps only 1–3% of contracts settle by delivery).
- Roles can switch: an overstocked end-user may sell; a supply-short producer may buy.
- The Chicago Board of Trade (organized 1848, trading from ~1859) developed to help Midwest grain producers and exporters manage price risk.
- Decision points before starting: trade alone or with a partner (downsides listed: shared profits/research, slower decisions, unclear responsibility); choose a time horizon based on trading system, time availability, commissions, personality, health, and data access.
- Two named but unresolved schools on per-trade risk: a "money management school" (fixed predetermined dollar risk) versus a "systems approach" (risk set by each trade's own support/resistance); the author says he prefers picking one extreme over a middle path, without stating which.

## Actionable rules

1. Do not borrow trading capital — use only disposable capital you will not miss if lost.
2. Day trading requires accurate, tick-by-tick real-time data; without it, don't day trade.
3. Choose a time horizon deliberately based on time available, commissions, personality, and health.
4. Decide in writing whether your approach is technical, fundamental, or a hybrid before trading live.
5. Decide up front whether per-trade risk will follow a fixed money-management rule or each trade's own chart-based support/resistance.

## Caveats

An introductory, non-technical lesson for complete beginners — no chart-based entry/exit system, no specific risk percentages, and only one illustrative hedging example. It ends with a 10-question quiz rather than more content, confirming this is course material rather than a standalone strategy guide.

## Who it is for

Complete beginners to futures trading needing conceptual groundwork before moving to technical or fundamental methods.
