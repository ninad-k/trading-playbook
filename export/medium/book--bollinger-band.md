# Bollinger Bands: Using Volatility: the trader's summary

*Uses Bollinger Band Width to distinguish volatility contraction from expansion and add context to breakouts and oscillator signals.*

**Matthew Claassen** · 2004 · Indicators · intermediate

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-5; all rendered pages inspected visually. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

This July/August 2004 Technical Analyst article treats volatility as an additional dimension of chart analysis. Claassen uses Bollinger Band Width to interpret whether volatility is contracting or expanding, then combines that information with price breakouts and momentum oscillators. S&P 500, TASER and Alcoa charts illustrate the argument; they are selected examples rather than a systematic performance test. References below use PDF pages; printed pages are 17-21.

## Key points

- **Band construction:** the illustrated default is a 20-period moving average with upper and lower bands two standard deviations away (PDF p. 2).
- **Band Width:** wider bands indicate greater recent price dispersion; narrowing bands indicate less. This describes volatility, not whether the next move will be upward or downward (pp. 2, 4).
- **Persistence and reversion:** the author argues that volatility clusters over short intervals while extremes eventually reverse. These are contextual tendencies, not timed entry guarantees (pp. 1-2).
- **Expansion filter:** a five-period exponential moving average of Band Width helps visualize its direction in the TASER example (p. 3).
- **Different information:** combine price, volume and volatility observations instead of treating several similar price oscillators as independent confirmation (p. 4).
- **Squeeze:** the article adopts Band Width at its lowest level in six months as a concrete definition of unusually low volatility (p. 5).

## Actionable rules

1. In the author's approach, first identify an otherwise attractive stock or price setup, then examine its volatility context. A breakout after contraction is distinguished from chasing a move when volatility is already unusually high (pp. 2-3).
2. Track whether Band Width is starting to expand from a low level, using the five-period EMA as a visual aid. The article does not define a complete crossover-based execution rule (p. 3).
3. Flag a six-month Band Width low as a squeeze, then evaluate the subsequent price move. A squeeze alone does not establish the direction of a trade (pp. 4-5).

## Caveats

The article supplies no complete stop, exit, position-sizing or transaction-cost model. Its chart-based claims of better timing and its comparison with the VIX are historical illustrations, not evidence of reliable future returns. Do not read a volatility contraction as a bullish signal by itself. All five scanned pages were inspected visually; figures are described rather than reproduced.

## Who it is for

Readers who understand basic bands and want to use volatility as context for breakouts and oscillator signals, before specifying and testing a complete strategy.
