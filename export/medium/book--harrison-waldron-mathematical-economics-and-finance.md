# Mathematical Economics and Finance: the trader's summary

*Trinity College Dublin lecture notes building math finance from linear algebra and optimization through mean-variance portfolio theory and the CAPM.*

**Michael Harrison, Patrick Waldron** · 1998 · Quant, Microstructure & Academic Research · advanced

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: the derivation chapters checked against the note; the notes are algebraic and state no trading parameters. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Course notes for MA381/EC3080 at Trinity College Dublin, for advanced undergraduates crossing between mathematics and economics. Part I covers the toolkit: linear algebra, vector calculus, and convexity/optimization (unconstrained, Lagrange, Kuhn-Tucker, duality). Part II applies it: choice under certainty/uncertainty, portfolio theory, and investment analysis. The portfolio chapter derives the mean-variance efficient frontier (with and without a riskfree asset) from first principles up to the CAPM. A final chapter sketches derivative arbitrage pricing (binomial, Black-Scholes) but is left largely a stub.

## Key points

- Frames finance as a subset of microeconomics: allocation across goods (prices), time (interest-rate term structure), and states of nature (risky returns).
- Builds the portfolio frontier by minimizing variance subject to a target return, in both weight space and mean-standard-deviation space.
- With a riskfree asset, the frontier becomes a straight capital market line from the riskfree rate through the tangency portfolio; positions above tangency imply borrowing.
- Derives the CAPM beta relationship from the tangency condition between an inner and outer frontier.
- CAPM's core assumption: every investor has mean-variance preferences, via quadratic utility or normally distributed returns.
- Notes that as securities are added to an equally weighted portfolio, variance converges toward the average covariance — the diversification floor.
- The arbitrage-pricing chapter (binomial and Black-Scholes option pricing) is outlined in the table of contents but not developed in the text.

## Actionable rules

None given — a theoretical reference, not a system. The practical takeaway: portfolio risk cannot be diversified below the average covariance among held assets, and CAPM beta pricing rests on the often-unrealistic assumption of mean-variance preferences.

## Caveats

An unfinished course draft, not a polished textbook: sections are marked "[To be written]" or "Need more waffle here," and figures are referenced but not included. The option-pricing chapter is essentially a stub. Assumes graduate-level comfort with proofs and matrix calculus.

## Who it is for

Mathematically trained readers wanting mean-variance portfolio theory and CAPM derived from optimization first principles, not practitioners seeking trading rules.
