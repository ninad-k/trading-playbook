# My Fairy God Trader: the trader's summary

*A fable about the Pip Auction Game teaches traders to stop escalating losses, review records, and return to demo trading after drawdowns.*

**Rob Booker** · 2004 · Trading Psychology & Discipline · beginner

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-29. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Booker uses a fictional “Pip Auction Game” to explain how traders turn a planned small loss into a large one by defending sunk costs. A trader seeking 30 pips may tolerate a 100-pip loss because exiting would admit a mistake; the story frames this as overbidding for pips (pp. 14–20). The remedy is explicit planning, fast loss recognition, record review, and a temporary return to simulation after severe account damage. The text is a behavioral lesson rather than a market method.

## Key points

- The core failure sequence is lack of planning, poor execution, escalation and loss, then negative emotion (pp. 24–26).
- The source’s example contrasts a 30-pip goal with a tolerated 100-pip loss, illustrating poor reward-to-risk discipline (pp. 15–18).
- If an account loses more than 9% over any period, the author says to stop live trading (p. 21).
- The suggested recovery exercise is one week of demo trading for every 2% of account loss (p. 21).
- Weekly review should take two hours away from the trading desk and examine account history and mistakes (pp. 21–23).
- The author warns against adding to losing trades and against changing systems to avoid admitting poor execution (pp. 18–27).
- The fictional trader concludes that rebuilding confidence may require six months or more without live trading (p. 28).

## Actionable rules

1. Define the intended profit and maximum loss before entry; close a stagnant trade or one entered for emotional reasons rather than letting losses escalate (pp. 18–21).
2. Stop live trading after a drawdown exceeding 9%; use one demo week per 2% lost before resuming (p. 21).
3. Spend two hours each week reviewing the account history away from the trading screen and document recurring errors (pp. 21–23).
4. Do not average down or remove a stop to preserve hope; when in doubt, take no new live trades (pp. 22–27).

## Caveats

The document is fictionalized and offers no quantified evidence that its thresholds fit all markets or accounts. Treating every losing trade as a mistake can confuse poor process with a normal loss in a valid system (p. 23). Its psychological rules are useful prompts, not a complete risk model.

## Who it is for

New or repeatedly overtrading FX participants who need a memorable explanation of sunk-cost behavior and a recovery routine.
