# Trade Like Jesse Livermore: the trader's summary

*Reconstructs Jesse Livermore's technical system — Pivotal Point timing, industry-group top-down analysis, and five money-management rules — from his heirs' interviews and his own 1940 market-key notebooks.*

**Richard Smitten** · 2005 · Trend Following & Mechanical Systems · intermediate

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 6, 9, 12, 23, 69, 96-97, 101, 104 (Top-Down Trading checklist, the probe system with its 20/20/20/40 sizing, and the Livermore Market Key chapters). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Overview

Smitten, who had already written a full Livermore biography (2001) based on interviews with Livermore's son Paul and daughter-in-law Patricia, uses this follow-up to isolate the mechanics rather than the life story. The book organizes everything Livermore is known to have used under three headings — Timing, Money Management, and Emotional Control — and closes with a verbatim reprint of Livermore's own 1940 "Market Key" notebooks (a hand-ruled, six-column price-recording ledger he used to formalize trend changes). Charts are used throughout to illustrate concepts Livermore himself tracked with chalkboards, ticker tape, and hand calculation, since he traded decades before charting software existed. The book explicitly aims to answer what Livermore's earlier vehicle, "Reminiscences of a Stock Operator," left out: not just what he did, but the specific technical rules behind how he did it.

## Core thesis

Markets are driven by recurring human emotional patterns (greed, fear, ignorance, hope), not by logic, so price and volume action — not news, opinions, or fundamentals — is the only reliable signal a speculator should act on. Livermore's method is to wait in cash until every available factor (overall market direction, the stock's industry group, a "sister stock" in the same group, and the individual stock's own price/volume pattern) lines up, enter only after the market has confirmed the move rather than anticipating it, size in gradually to test the thesis with real money, and then let a correct position run while cutting an incorrect one almost immediately. Smitten frames the entire system as procedural discipline compensating for a market that is inherently emotional and repetitive rather than rational.

## Key concepts

- **Line of least resistance** — Livermore's term for overall market/stock direction, deliberately avoiding "bull" or "bear" because those labels lock a trader into an anticipatory mindset.
- **Pivotal Point** — the specific price level marking the "perfect psychological moment" to enter; divided into Reversal Pivotal Points (a change in basic trend) and Continuation Pivotal Points (a pause/consolidation confirming the existing trend is intact).
- **Top-Down Trading** — a four-step checklist: (1) confirm overall market direction, (2) confirm the stock's industry group is moving the same way, (3) confirm a "sister stock" (tandem trading) in the same group shows the identical pattern, (4) final due-diligence check on the individual stock before entry.
- **Tandem trading** — tracking two leading stocks in the same industry group together (e.g., Morgan Stanley/Merrill Lynch, GM/Ford) because Livermore held that stocks in a group move together and a single stock's signal is unreliable alone.
- **Probe system** — building a position in tranches (Livermore used roughly 20%/20%/20%/40%) so that each additional purchase is made only after the prior tranche is already showing a profit, at a progressively higher (or, for shorts, lower) price.
- **One-Day Reversal** — a session where the day's high exceeds the prior day's high, but the close is below the prior close, on higher volume than the prior day; treated as a strong danger/exit signal even in an otherwise intact uptrend.
- **Consolidating base / saucer pattern** — a slow, rounded basing formation that lets earnings/sales catch up to price before the next leg; treated similarly to a long Continuation Pivotal Point.
- **New-high breakout** — Livermore's contrarian-for-his-era belief that stocks breaking out to new highs (clearing "overhanging" supply from trapped sellers) tend to keep advancing, and symmetrically for new lows on the short side.
- **Involuntary investor** — Livermore's term for a speculator who refuses to sell a losing position and unconsciously reclassifies it as a "long-term investment" to avoid realizing the loss.
- **Livermore Market Key** — his own 1940 six-column hand-ledger method (Secondary Rally / Natural Rally / Upward Trend / Downward Trend / Natural Reaction / Secondary Reaction) for formalizing Pivotal Points using fixed point thresholds (see system page).

## Rules and setups

1. **Direction filter first.** Before considering any individual stock, establish the line of least resistance for the overall market (Dow, Nasdaq, S&P, or Amex depending on the stock's listing) — trade only in that direction.
2. **Group and tandem confirmation.** Confirm the stock's industry group is moving the same direction, and that at least one "sister stock" (the group's other leader) shows the same pattern; the exception is when a single stock (e.g., a dominant name) represents over 50% of group volume, in which case that stock alone can be tracked.
3. **Trade only group leaders.** Restrict trading to the leading stocks in the leading industry groups; avoid cheap, laggard, or "bargain" stocks in weak groups.
4. **Entry timing — Pivotal Points.** Enter at a Reversal Pivotal Point (marks a change in trend, typically accompanied by a volume surge of 50%–500% over average) or a Continuation Pivotal Point (a pause/consolidation within an established trend). Do not buy more than 5%–10% above the Reversal Pivotal Point — beyond that, the edge is considered lost.
5. **Position building (the probe).** Do not establish a full position at once. Example ratio: 20% on the first purchase, 20% on the second, 20% on the third, 40% on the final purchase — each successive purchase only after the prior one is showing a profit, and each at a higher price (never average down).
6. **Stop-loss / risk cap.** Never lose more than 10% of invested capital on a single position ("the Bucket Shop rule"); set the stop before entering, typically anchored at the Pivotal Point itself. Never meet a margin call — close the position instead.
7. **One-Day Reversal exit signal.** If, during an uptrend, a session prints a higher high than the prior day but a lower close than the prior day's close on higher volume, treat it as a strong warning and consider selling.
8. **Concentration limit.** Hold no more than about 10 positions at once, and preferably 5 or fewer, to keep each one closely watched.
9. **Profit-taking discipline.** Let winners run without a fixed profit target as long as the stock "acts right" and no valid sell signal appears; after a large ("windfall") profit, bank roughly 50% of it out of the trading account.
10. **Livermore Market Key thresholds (see system page for full mechanics).** A stock trading above roughly $30 needs an approximate 6-point move to register a Natural Rally/Reaction; a combined ("Key Price") signal across two group stocks needs roughly 12 points; a trend resumption past a marked Pivotal Point requires a further 3-point (or 6-point for the Key Price) push beyond it to be considered confirmed.

## Risk and money management

The book states five explicit money-management rules attributed to Livermore: (1) use the probe system rather than buying a full position at once; (2) never lose more than 10% of invested capital on any trade; (3) always keep a cash reserve; (4) require a specific reason to both buy and sell — never sell purely because a position shows a profit; (5) after a windfall profit, move roughly half of it out of the trading account into cash/safekeeping. A reproduced "Percentage Loss Table" makes the asymmetry of losses explicit: an 8% loss needs an 8.7% gain to recover, but a 50% loss needs a 100% gain — the arithmetic justification for the hard 10% stop. Additional stated rules: never avoid a margin call by adding funds (close instead), never average down into a losing position, evaluate every trade's risk/reward ratio before entry (the book gives an example where a $200 stock needing a 10% move for a $20,000 gain on $200,000 committed is judged an unattractive ratio), and treat commissions, interest, and losses themselves as normal costs of running speculation as a business.

## Psychology and discipline

Livermore is portrayed as someone who studied psychology formally (night courses) to understand why traders — including himself — repeatedly broke their own rules under the pull of fear, greed, hope, and ignorance. The book's central psychological instruction is not to anticipate: wait for the tape/market to confirm a view rather than acting on an opinion, because "the market is never wrong — opinions often are." It repeatedly stresses that cutting losses quickly is an act of will against basic human instinct (the impulse to average down and wait for a loser to come back), while letting winners run requires resisting the equally strong urge to grab a small, certain profit. Other repeated behavioral rules: never take or give stock tips, trade alone and keep positions secret (Livermore used up to 50 brokers to hide his own activity), avoid the words "bullish"/"bearish" to prevent a fixed directional mindset, and treat a good run of profits as no guarantee against a future blowup — Livermore himself went bankrupt four times and, by his own account to his sons, "only lost money when I did not follow my own rules."

## Strengths and caveats

The book's strength is specificity: unlike "Reminiscences of a Stock Operator," which narrates Livermore's career without disclosing mechanics, this book states concrete thresholds (10% stop, 20/20/20/40 probe ratios, 5%–10% Pivotal Point entry window, 6-point/12-point/3-point Market Key thresholds) that a reader could attempt to code. The major caveat is source reliability: the material is reconstructed by a biographer decades after Livermore's 1940 death, from family interviews and a reprint of Livermore's own notebook, not from an independently verified or backtested rule set, and no historical performance statistics or backtests accompany any of the rules — the evidence offered is anecdotal (the 1907 and 1929 trades) rather than systematic. The point-based thresholds (6-point reactions, $30 minimum stock price) were calibrated to 1930s–40s stock prices and volatility and do not translate directly to modern percentage-based or decimalized markets without rescaling. The "top-down" industry-group framework assumes stable, identifiable sector leadership, which is a reasonable assumption in equities but requires the reader's own judgment to apply, since the book gives no formal quantitative test for what qualifies as a "leading" stock or group beyond relative size and price action.

## Who should read it

Best suited to a discretionary swing or position trader interested in trend-following, sector-relative-strength analysis, and disciplined position sizing, particularly someone who has already read "Reminiscences of a Stock Operator" and wants the underlying mechanics behind Livermore's narrative. Less useful for a trader seeking a backtested or quantitatively validated system, or for very short-term/intraday trading, since the examples and point thresholds are calibrated to swing-to-position-length moves.
