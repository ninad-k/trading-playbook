# Speculative Investor Behavior in a Stock Market with Heterogeneous Expectations: the trader's summary

*A theoretical model shows how disagreement and short-sale constraints can put a resale premium above every investor's dividend valuation.*

**J. Michael Harrison and David M. Kreps** · 1978 · Quant, Microstructure & Academic Research · advanced

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-15. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Harrison and Kreps define speculation as paying more for a stock because it can later be resold than if it had to be held forever. Their partial-equilibrium model gives investors the same information but different subjective probabilities. With short sales prohibited, anticipated resale to a differently optimistic class can lift price above every class’s present value of dividends (PDF pp. 2–7). This is a theoretical mechanism, not an empirical study.

## Key points

- Speculative behavior requires a resale opportunity; it disappears in a one-period setting, with identical investors, or in complete and perfect contingent-claims markets (PDF pp. 2–3).
- The core model assumes finitely many belief classes, risk neutrality, constant discounting, effectively unlimited class wealth, common substantive information, and no short selling (PDF pp. 3–6).
- In the numerical example, class 2 values perpetual dividends at 1.45 in state zero and 1.91 in state one. Class 1 will pay at least 1.75 in state zero because it expects to resell to class 2 after a dividend arrives (PDF pp. 6–7).
- The minimal consistent prices are 24/13 in state zero and 27/13 in state one; ownership switches with the state. The paper prints 27/13 as 2.04, although the fraction is about 2.08 (PDF p. 11).
- A minimal consistent price is at least every class’s expected present value of dividends and is typically strictly higher under heterogeneous beliefs. With one investor class, it equals dividend value (PDF pp. 10–11).
- Nonminimal consistent prices add a bubble or Ponzi component; that component requires an infinite horizon in this model (PDF p. 10).
- The authors derive active portfolio management inside the model, then acknowledge that risk aversion and transaction costs would move its implications toward buy-and-hold efficiency (PDF pp. 13–15).

## Actionable rules

1. Separate a security’s dividend value from the resale value created by other investors’ beliefs (PDF pp. 2–3, 6–7).
2. When analyzing a disagreement premium, identify which constraints prevent pessimistic investors from expressing their view; the paper’s result relies heavily on prohibited short sales (PDF pp. 3, 12–13).
3. Stress-test any resale-premium thesis after adding risk aversion, finite wealth, costs, and imperfect forecasts (PDF pp. 8, 12–14).
4. Do not treat the model’s active-management result as a trading prescription; it follows from restrictive assumptions rather than observed returns (PDF pp. 14–15).

## Caveats

The model assumes perfect contingent foresight about future price formation and abstracts from firm control, endogenous dividends, and general equilibrium. Its authors call the foresight assumption heroic and emphasize the short-sale restriction (PDF pp. 3–5, 8, 12–14).

## Who it is for

Advanced readers studying belief disagreement, speculative premiums, and theoretical limits of fundamental valuation.
