# 25 Rules of Millionaire Traders: the trader's summary

*Futures Magazine booklet of 25 short discipline and risk rules distilled from elite futures traders, covering position sizing, stops, pyramiding, and emotional control.*

**Futures Magazine editorial staff** · 2006 · Trading Psychology & Discipline · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 1-3 (the numbered rules). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A short promotional booklet from Futures Magazine (2006), presenting 25 numbered "rules of trading" that the editors attribute to a small group of consistently profitable futures traders (noting only 15-25% of futures traders succeed long-term). Each rule is a short paragraph of practical advice covering discipline, position sizing, stop placement, order types, and emotional control, aimed at a beginner-to-intermediate futures trader. There is no data, backtesting, or named-trader attribution — it reads as a condensed checklist of trading-floor folk wisdom rather than a systematic study.

## Key points

- Discipline is called the hardest and most important rule; without it a trader constantly reacts to the market instead of controlling their own plan.
- Fund trading accounts only with money that would not cause financial hardship if lost; never risk money needed for essentials.
- Progress gradually: paper trade first, then trade single-contract lots, before scaling to larger size or more volatile markets.
- Don't place more than about one-third of account equity in any single position (implying roughly 3x margin coverage per position).
- Decide entry, exit, and profit objective before placing a trade rather than reacting to intraday price swings or news.
- Stay focused on a small number of markets mastered in depth rather than spreading attention across many markets ("information overload").
- Be wary when a position becomes popular with the retail/small-lot crowd — by the time the public "discovers" a move, it is often over; institutional crowding is treated differently.
- Never add to a losing position ("averaging down" is framed as compounding a mistake, not a valid technique); cut losses while they are still small.
- Let profitable trades run until a technical or fundamental signal indicates it's time to exit, rather than closing simply because profit exists.
- Avoid trading based on intraday news reactions; back away and reassess before re-entering on a news-driven move.
- Avoid trading in a futures contract's delivery month due to elevated volatility, unless prepared to make/take physical delivery.

## Actionable rules

1. Cap any single position at roughly one-third of total account equity (equivalently, keep about 3x the position's required margin in the account).
2. Place a protective stop-loss at the same time as the entry order, not too close to current price (to avoid premature stop-outs) but always present.
3. Never add to a losing position; only add to winners, using a pyramid pattern where each added lot is smaller than the prior one (e.g., 5 contracts, then 4, then 3, then 2, then 1, before liquidating).
4. Avoid "at the market" orders except when a position must be liquidated immediately; otherwise use a specific limit price.
5. Avoid holding positions into a contract's delivery month.
6. Exit near-momentum reversals rather than trying to pick the exact top or bottom; ignore the last few ticks of a move.
7. Take periodic multi-day breaks from trading (weekends/holidays don't count) to avoid judgment fatigue from continuous market exposure.
8. Look for "shopped odds": markets trading near historical extremes (e.g., historical lows) where risk is lower and profit potential is higher than the average setup.

## Caveats

Promotional booklet (Futures Magazine premium content, 2006) with no named sources, no backtested statistics, and no specific instrument, timeframe, or numeric stop/target values beyond the one-third-equity and pyramid-sizing guidelines — rules are qualitative folk wisdom rather than a codified system. Some rules are in tension with each other in practice (e.g., "let profits run" vs. "don't try to pick tops/bottoms") and require the trader's own judgment to apply. Content is futures-market-specific (contract delivery months, margin) but the discipline and risk principles generalize to any instrument.

## Who it is for

Beginner-to-intermediate futures traders who want a compact discipline and risk-management checklist rather than a technical or quantitative system; a good companion primer alongside a specific entry/exit methodology.
