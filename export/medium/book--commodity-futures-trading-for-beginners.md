# Commodity Futures Trading for Beginners: the trader's summary

*Bruce Babcock's free beginner primer on futures trading, built around four cardinal rules: trade with the trend, cut losses short, let profits run, manage risk.*

**Bruce Babcock** · 1999 · Trend Following & Mechanical Systems · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 6, 15, 21 (the market list, capital minimum and the order-mechanics examples). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A free introductory guide by veteran system trader and newsletter publisher Bruce Babcock, aimed at readers with no futures experience. It explains what a futures contract is, why leverage makes futures both more profitable and riskier than stocks, why speculators are economically necessary to hedgers, and why most traders lose. The core of the book is a four-part "successful trading plan" framework and a closing list of psychological pitfalls.

## Key points

- Futures trading is a zero-sum bet on price direction; no ownership of the underlying is taken, and for every winner there is a loser.
- Leverage is the main attraction: a $10,000 futures account can control exposure equivalent to $350,000 of stock (roughly 35:1), amplifying both gains and losses.
- Commissions are much lower relative to position size than for stocks (roughly $30–50 on a $20,000 futures gain vs. up to 1% each way on stocks).
- About 40 futures markets have sufficient liquidity for prudent speculation, spanning currencies, rates, energy, metals, food, and agriculturals.
- The four cardinal principles of a trading plan: trade with the trend, cut losses short, let profits run, manage risk.
- A minimum of about $10,000 in true risk capital is recommended before starting; less than that makes proper risk management impossible.
- The best trending markets by sector: currencies (Swiss Franc, German Mark, Japanese Yen, British Pound), interest rates (T-Bonds, Eurodollars), energy (Crude Oil, Heating Oil, Natural Gas), food (Coffee, Orange Juice, Sugar), metals (Gold, Silver, Copper), agriculturals (Corn, Oats, Soybeans, Cotton).
- Options are discouraged as a "safer" alternative to futures; author argues selling options (where the edge lies) is harder and riskier than trading futures directly.
- Professional money managers are typically satisfied with ~20% annual returns; a good diversified mechanical system can realistically target 25–50% annually.

## Actionable rules

1. Define trend on a time frame of at least four weeks; only take trades in that direction. Example entry rule given: buy when today's close is above the close 25 market days ago, sell when it is below.
2. Cut losses at about 1% of account equity per trade (up to 5% max for very small accounts); in dollar terms, risk roughly $500–$1,000 per trade in most markets, using a broker-held stop-loss order.
3. Let winners run using a trailing stop that protects a set percentage of accumulated profit rather than a fixed price target.
4. Diversify across a basket of the more trend-prone markets (see sector list above) rather than concentrating in one.
5. Test any strategy on precise, objective rules using historical data before trading it live; non-computerized testing is considered unreliable.
6. Size positions and choose markets (e.g., Mid-America mini contracts) so that risk stays in proportion to account capital; avoid large, volatile contracts (e.g., S&P 500 futures) with small accounts.

## Caveats

Written in 1999 by the author of a well-known trading-system newsletter (Bruce Babcock, Reality Based Trading Company); commission and margin figures ($10,000 minimum account, ~$30–50 commissions) are dated and should be treated as illustrative, not current. The tax discussion (60/40 long/short-term capital-gains split) reflects pre-2000s U.S. tax rules and may no longer be accurate. No specific entry/exit indicator beyond the simple 25-day breakout example is given; the book is a framework, not a complete system.

## Who it is for

Complete beginners to futures trading who need the vocabulary, risk mechanics, and a simple four-rule mental framework before building or buying an actual trading system.
