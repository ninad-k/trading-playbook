# Asset Pricing with Speculative Trading: the trader's summary

*Academic model showing speculative price premiums arise from persistent, rational-but-diverse investor beliefs, formalizing Keynes's 'beauty contest' intuition.*

**Ho-Mou Wu and Wen-Chung Guo** · Quant, Microstructure & Academic Research · advanced

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: the model and implications sections checked against the note; the thesis is analytical rather than numeric. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

An academic finance paper asking: can persistent disagreement among rational investors, rather than private information or noise trading, explain speculative price premiums in asset markets? The authors build a formal model using Kurz's "rational beliefs" framework — beliefs that are individually rational (consistent with observed data) yet can remain diverse and non-stationary across agents even after all agents have learned the data's true long-run statistical distribution. Using a Markovian model of dividends and beliefs, they derive analytically how the speculative premium in an asset's price depends on the degree of belief heterogeneity among traders, and show this framework can generate "endogenous uncertainty" (extra volatility not explained by the underlying dividend process).

## Key points

- Formalizes Keynes's "beauty contest" idea — investors price an asset based on what they expect others to think it is worth, not just its fundamental dividend stream.
- Even with complete learning (agents converge on the same long-run/stationary distribution of the data), their period-by-period beliefs can still diverge and remain non-stationary, sustaining speculative trading indefinitely.
- The size of the speculative premium in equilibrium prices is shown analytically to increase with the degree of heterogeneity of investor beliefs.
- Equilibrium prices in the model are generally higher than fundamental (dividend-based) value, and speculative premiums are typically positive.
- The model produces "endogenous uncertainty" — additional price volatility generated by the trading process itself rather than by news about fundamentals.
- Positioned against private-information/noise-trader models (e.g., DeLong, Shleifer, Summers & Waldmann) as an alternative explanation for excess trading and pricing anomalies, building instead on Harrison & Kreps (1978) and Morris (1996).
- Results are derived in a simplified Markovian setting but the authors argue the core conclusions are robust in more general environments.

## Actionable rules

None given — this is a theoretical asset-pricing model with no trading signals, entries, exits, or parameter values. Its practical takeaway for traders is conceptual:

1. Persistent price premiums above fundamental value can be a rational consequence of investor belief dispersion, not necessarily mispricing that will correct quickly.
2. Elevated trading volume and volatility can arise purely from disagreement among informed, rational participants — a reason to be cautious about assuming volatility spikes always signal new information.
3. Belief heterogeneity (e.g., diverging analyst/investor consensus) is a variable worth tracking as a proxy for how much speculative premium might be embedded in current prices.
