# What's in Your Head? Becoming a Trader: the trader's summary

*Applies deliberate-practice and expertise research (Ericsson, Simonton) to explain why becoming a competent trader takes structured, mentored practice over months to years, not shortcuts.*

**Brett N. Steenbarger and Doug Foster** · 2005 · Trading Psychology & Discipline · beginner

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-8. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

This SFO Magazine article (July 2005) by trading psychologist Brett Steenbarger and Doug Foster describes the "internship/residency" style training program the authors ran for professional traders at a proprietary trading firm, and connects it to the psychology-of-expertise research literature (K. Anders Ericsson on deliberate practice, Dean Keith Simonton on eminence, Arthur Reber on implicit pattern learning). The core argument: trading skill develops the same way expertise develops in medicine, chess, and sports — through structured, goal-directed, mentored practice with rapid feedback, sustained over many months, not through passively watching markets or reading books.

## Key points

- Expertise research (Ericsson) finds skill acquisition is a function of "deliberative practice" — structured, goal-oriented, with feedback — not raw hours of exposure; roughly a decade of such immersion precedes mastery (Simonton).
- The firm's training used a medical-school model: "see one, do one, teach one," moving from observation to supervised practice to independent trading.
- Mentorship matters for three reasons: preparing traders for rare "fat tail" events, creating a safe environment for voicing doubt, and providing the peer support screen-based trading has displaced compared to pit trading.
- Learning is enhanced by decomposing trading into components (pattern recognition, order execution, trade management) and drilling each separately rather than only "playing full games" from day one.
- Simulation and mental rehearsal (guided imagery of a trading session) are effective bridges between paper trading and live trading, per sports-psychology research.
- Position size should scale only with demonstrated recent results ("you must earn the right to trade two lots before abandoning a one-lot default").
- A trading journal logging market conditions alongside each trade reveals behavioral progress before it shows up in P/L; oscillation in trade size/duration often reflects unresolved stubbornness about exiting positions.
- Hours of serious, high-effort study/practice — not just time in the market — is the best predictor of skill (Janet Starkes' research on chess, figure skating, music).

## Actionable rules

1. Segment practice into components (pattern recognition, execution, trade management) and drill each in isolation before combining them in live trading.
2. Start with a fixed minimum position size (e.g., a one-lot default) and only increase size after demonstrated recent profitability in simulation or live trading.
3. Use a trading journal that records market conditions (trending/non-trending, volatility, time of day) alongside every trade, not just P/L.
4. Seek a mentor or structured feedback loop rather than learning solely through unsupervised live trading.
5. Expect a multi-month learning curve before consistent profitability; budget capital and time accordingly rather than expecting quick results.

## Caveats

The article is descriptive and motivational rather than prescriptive; it gives no specific market, indicator, or risk-per-trade numbers. Findings are generalized from sports/chess/medical-education research applied by analogy to trading, and from the authors' own unnamed proprietary firm's program, so the claims are not trading-specific empirical results.

## Who it is for

Aspiring and early-stage discretionary traders who want a realistic, psychology-grounded view of how long skill development actually takes, and firms or mentors designing a trader-training curriculum.
