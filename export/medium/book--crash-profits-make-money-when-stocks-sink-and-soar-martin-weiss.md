# Crash Profits: Make Money When Stocks Sink and Soar: the trader's summary

*A dramatized 2002-era guide to protecting capital and profiting from a stock/bond/real-estate bust via safe-haven moves, put options, and reverse index funds.*

**Martin D. Weiss** · 2003 · Money Management & Position Sizing · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 3, 5-8, 116 (the rally-mode rule, the staged re-entry plan and the historical panic chapters). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Overview

Written in late 2002 in the wake of the dot-com bust and the Enron/WorldCom accounting scandals, this book is framed as a novel: a fictional family (broker's client Linda Dedini, her CEO father, and a fee-only financial adviser) works through the 2000-2002 bear market while a parallel cast of characters (a whistle-blowing Wall Street analyst, a corporate-reform committee) exposes analyst conflicts of interest and government deficit accounting. Embedded in the story are the author's actual instructional set pieces: how to rate and sell vulnerable stocks and bonds, how to defend a 401(k), how put options work and how to size them, and how to identify a market bottom and redeploy capital. Weiss's father survived and profited from the 1929-1932 crash by shorting stocks and later buying blue chips near the bottom; that family history frames the book's two-sided thesis: protect capital on the way down, then buy aggressively once a recognizable bottoming process completes.

## Core thesis

Bull markets built on hype, leverage, and accounting manipulation must eventually reverse, and most investors are unprepared because Wall Street's incentive structure (commissions, investment-banking fees, analyst compensation tied to sales) systematically discourages honest sell advice. An individual investor's edge is not prediction but preparation: rate and exit vulnerable holdings before a crisis is obvious, hold safety in Treasuries/money markets, and only then use small, strictly sized allocations to instruments that gain in a decline (put options, reverse index funds, short selling) to convert the crash itself into profit. On the other side of the cycle, a repeatable "three-phase" pattern in interest rates (sharp decline, an unexpected spike, then a second decline) historically marks the transition from panic to recovery and is the cue to redeploy capital into stocks and long bonds in stages.

## Key concepts

- **Broker conflict of interest** — brokerage "strong buy" ratings are frequently tied to investment-banking fees from the rated company, documented via the 2002 Spitzer/Merrill Lynch and Salomon Smith Barney e-mail disclosures cited in the text.
- **Fee-only adviser** — an adviser paid only a stated hourly or asset-based fee, with no commission or product-sale incentive, positioned as structurally more trustworthy than a broker.
- **"Is the Market in Rally Mode?"** — the book's one explicit technical rule: compare the S&P 500 to its 20-day moving average; index above the average = rally mode, below = not.
- **Put option** — the right to sell a stock at a fixed strike price before expiration; used as the book's main lower-risk vehicle for profiting from a decline (max loss = premium paid).
- **Reverse (inverse) index fund** — a mutual fund designed to rise in value as its benchmark index falls; used as the larger allocation vehicle for crash profits because it lacks an option's time decay.
- **Short selling** — borrowing and selling shares to profit from a price decline, explained but not recommended as the book's primary tool because of unlimited theoretical loss.
- **Bond quality ladder** — Treasury > single-A corporate > triple-B corporate > junk (double-B or lower); the book's rule of thumb for which bonds to hold versus sell first in a credit crisis.
- **Three-phase interest-rate pattern** — (1) rates decline as the Fed eases and deflation sets in, (2) rates spike unexpectedly as a credit panic hits, (3) rates decline again as the panic passes; phase 2's end / phase 3's start is presented as the historically best entry point for long bonds.
- **Staged re-entry** — redeploying capital from safety back into stocks/bonds in tranches (e.g., 10%, then 20%, and so on) rather than all at once, once a bottom is suspected but not confirmed.
- **"Selling intelligently"** — a triage sequence (most vulnerable stocks first, then unrated holdings checked against independent rating services, then less-urgent categories) rather than an indiscriminate liquidation.

## Rules and setups

This is not a chart-pattern trading book; its "setups" are money-management and instrument-selection rules stated as explicit numbered steps.

1. **Vulnerable-stock triage** (Ch. 6): sell any stock on an independent low-safety-rating list immediately; for unlisted holdings, check an independent rating agency and sell if the rating is low; avoid thinly traded/small-cap names without broker assistance on execution.
2. **Bond safety ladder** (Ch. 14): most urgent — sell all bonds rated double-B or lower (junk); urgent — sell all corporate bonds below single-A, including triple-B; less urgent — sell remaining corporate bonds with 5+ years to maturity. Favor the highest quality and shortest maturity available.
3. **401(k) defense** (Ch. 14): do not withdraw from the plan; within it, prefer (safest to least-safe) a government-only money market fund, any money market fund, a high-grade/short-maturity bond fund, then a high-dividend income fund only as a last resort; petition the plan administrator under ERISA §404(c) for a genuine fixed-income option if none exists.
4. **Rally-mode filter**: before selling into strength or before buying/selling puts, check whether the S&P 500 is above (rally mode) or below (not) its 20-day moving average; the book's only concrete technical trigger.
5. **Put option system** — sized allocation (max 5% of the portfolio), staged entry, and 15 numbered execution rules; detailed in the companion system page [The 15-Rule Put Option Crash Strategy](https://ninad-k.github.io/trading-playbook/books/crash-profits-make-money-when-stocks-sink-and-soar-martin-weiss--put-option-crash-strategy.html).
6. **Bottom-recognition and re-entry** (Ch. 23): watch for the three-phase interest-rate pattern; once phase 2 (rate spike) ends and phase 3 (renewed rate decline) begins, begin staged buying — the book's own example allocates roughly 10% to common stocks and 20% to convertible bonds as an initial tranche, adding more "one step at a time" as conviction grows.

## Risk and money management

Money management is the book's real subject even though it is not organized under that heading. The core discipline is capital sequencing: get to safety first (Treasuries/money markets), then risk only a small, capped slice on instruments that profit from further decline. For options specifically the book is explicit: no more than 5% of a portfolio in options, spread over roughly a year rather than deployed at once (e.g., $10,000 planned becomes $2,500/quarter), and no single option contract costing more than about $500 (or less than $50, where commissions dominate). Stop-loss orders are explicitly discouraged for the options sleeve — sizing and staging are used instead — but stops are used elsewhere (401(k) triage, bond triage) as hard sell triggers. On the re-entry side, risk is managed by scaling in over tranches rather than a single all-in purchase, and by favoring convertible bonds (yield cushion plus some upside participation) alongside common stock when a bottom is still unconfirmed.

## Psychology and discipline

The book repeatedly frames emotional discipline as the actual product being sold: investors are warned against "guru-itis" (subservience to market forecasters, illustrated by the author's own costly 1987 OEX-options episode), against holding a position out of sunk-cost attachment to a paid-off family debt of gratitude, and against treating a broker's enthusiasm as due diligence. The recurring instruction is to act mechanically on rules (sell when a rating is low, buy in tranches once phase 3 begins) rather than on hope, prediction, or a professional's "free" advice, since commissioned salespeople are structurally motivated to keep clients invested regardless of risk.

## Strengths and caveats

The put-option sizing rules (Ch. 14) and the bond/401(k) triage steps (Ch. 14) are genuinely concrete and usable independent of the book's macro narrative. However, the book is a 2002-2003 period piece: its worst-case deflation scenario, its specific bond-market and real-estate-bubble calls, and its dramatized "grand-supercycle" macro predictions did not play out as the fictionalized narrative implies, and none of the market-timing claims are backtested — the three-phase interest-rate pattern is asserted from a single historical analogy (the 1930s) rather than demonstrated statistically. The novel framing (invented characters, dialogue-driven exposition) means genuine technical content is diluted across many pages of narrative, and the political/deficit-accounting material is opinion, not trading methodology. Readers should treat the specific percentage caps (5% options, 95% safety) as reasonable defaults rather than optimized figures.

## Who should read it

Beginner-to-intermediate investors who want a plain-language walkthrough of what a stock/bond bear market does to a normal portfolio and a simple, capped-risk way to hedge or speculate on further decline (via puts or reverse funds) without learning short selling mechanics. Less useful for traders wanting technical entries/exits — the only chart-based rule in the book is the 20-day moving average rally filter.
