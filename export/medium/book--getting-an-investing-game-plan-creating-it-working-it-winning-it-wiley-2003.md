# Getting an Investing Game Plan: Creating It, Working It, Winning It: the trader's summary

*A certified financial planner's 10-step framework for building, allocating, monitoring, and writing up a personal mutual-fund investing plan.*

**Vern C. Hayden with Maura Webber and Jamie Heller** · 2003 · Investing, Value & Market History · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 5, 15 (contents and the allocation chapters carrying the Brinson finding and the plan-level rules). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Overview

Written by CFP Vern Hayden with journalists Maura Webber and Jamie Heller, this is a long-term personal-investing guide, not a trading book: it targets buy-and-hold investors building a mutual-fund portfolio rather than active traders. Hayden frames the whole process as a football-style "game plan" built in 10 sequential steps — mindset, risk tolerance, goals, fund selection, asset allocation, manager selection, monitoring, and finally writing the plan down — interleaved with short profiles of well-known 1990s-2000s fund managers (Bill Gross, Bob Rodriguez, Jean-Marie Eveillard, and others) and worked numeric examples drawn heavily from the 2000-2002 bear market.

## Core thesis

Investing success is driven far more by having and sticking to a written, properly allocated plan than by picking winning securities: Hayden cites Gary Brinson's research finding that up to 90% of a portfolio's returns come from asset allocation decisions, not security selection or timing. The book's throughline is "commitment, consistency, and courage" — commit to a plan sized to your real risk tolerance, consistently hold to its allocation, and have the courage to both stay the course in downturns and to act when a genuine strategic review calls for change.

## Key concepts

- **Offense and defense** — Hayden's central metaphor: stocks are "offense" (higher expected return, higher risk), bonds and cash are "defense" (capital preservation, stability); every portfolio needs both regardless of age.
- **Three C's** — Commitment (to a written plan), Consistency (sticking to it through volatility), Courage (both to hold steady and to act when a real review calls for change).
- **Intellectual vs. emotional risk tolerance** — the gap between how much risk investors believe they can handle in the abstract and how they actually react once real money is falling; Hayden argues emotion usually wins, which is why he starts most clients conservatively.
- **Gap analysis** — comparing your current portfolio's actual risk profile against your measured risk tolerance to find and close the mismatch.
- **Static vs. active asset allocation** — static allocation fixes target percentages and rebalances back to them regardless of market view; active allocation allows deliberate, limited tilts toward opportunity, distinct from outright market timing.
- **Three levels of allocation** — (1) stocks/bonds/cash mix, (2) fund style mix within each asset class (large/mid/small-cap, value/growth/blend), (3) picking the specific funds/managers to fill each style slot.
- **Special teams** — a smaller, opportunistic sleeve of sector or thematic funds (e.g., healthcare, technology) layered on top of the core offense/defense allocation.
- **Real return** — investment return minus the inflation rate; the only meaningful measure of whether wealth is actually growing.
- **The "bunker" portfolio** — a fourth, more defensive allocation reserved specifically for extended bear markets, distinct from the standard conservative/moderate/aggressive trio.
- **Tactical vs. strategic review** — short-interval fund-level monitoring (tactical) versus an annual, big-picture check of the whole plan and personal circumstances (strategic).

## Rules and setups

This book has no trade entries/exits; its "rules" are portfolio construction and review rules for a buy-and-hold mutual-fund investor.

1. **The five-year rule.** Do not put at risk any money you may need within the next five years; keep it out of stocks and bonds entirely, since a broadly diversified stock/bond portfolio has historically produced positive returns over the large majority of five-year holding periods (per Ibbotson Associates data cited in the book), but not reliably over shorter windows.
2. **Reasonable risk sizing.** Take only the amount of risk needed to hit your target return for your goal and time horizon — no more. If an 8% annual return over five years meets the goal, a riskier portfolio aimed at a higher return is "needless" risk.
3. **Speculation kitty cap.** If you can't resist chasing hot tips or trends, wall off no more than 5% of total portfolio value for that purpose, kept fully separate from the core game plan.
4. **Four model portfolios (equities / fixed income / cash):**

   | Portfolio | Equities | Fixed income | Cash | Target return | Time horizon |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Conservative | 50% | 40% | 10% | 5-6%/yr | 4-5 years |
   | Moderate | 65% | 25% | 10% | 7-8%/yr | 5-7 years |
   | Aggressive | 80% | 15% | 5% | 9%+/yr | 7+ years |
   | Bunker | 30% | 55% | 15% | 3-6%/yr | bear-market only |

   Hayden starts roughly 80% of new clients in the Moderate portfolio by default, on the view that it is very hard to gauge true risk tolerance up front and moderate is the easiest starting point to adjust from in either direction.
5. **Risk Quiz mapping.** A 15-point risk-tolerance quiz (Chapter 2) maps to portfolio choice: score 5-7 → Conservative (or Bunker in a bear market), 8-12 → Moderate, 13-15 → Aggressive.
6. **Fund monitoring cadence.** Track individual fund performance no more often than twice a month (more frequent DIY monitoring tends to provoke overtrading); run a full portfolio performance report quarterly; conduct a full strategic review of allocation and goals at least once a year (Hayden suggests tax season).
7. **Fund sell triggers.** (a) Psychological: if a fund's volatility is visibly affecting your mood/sleep, sell it regardless of the numbers. (b) Quantitative: a fund underperforming the best funds in its style category by 10 percentage points year-to-date is a "red flag" for closer scrutiny; underperformance that persists or widens to 15-20 percentage points below the category's best funds is Hayden's general sell signal — applied only after 6-12 months of observation, not on a single bad quarter.
8. **Rebalancing trigger.** When a single fund's weight drifts materially from its target allocation (Hayden's example: a planned 5% position that grows to 15% of the portfolio), trim it back toward its target weight even if your fundamental view of that category is unchanged.
9. **Recovery math (why avoiding large losses matters more than chasing large gains):** a 20% loss requires a 25% gain to recover; a 50% loss requires a 100% gain; a 90% loss requires a 900% gain. Losses compound asymmetrically against you.
10. **Active-fund selection bar.** Because Hayden found that historically studied value managers outperformed their benchmark only about 60-70% of the time (not every year), judge a manager on rolling multi-year outperformance and process, not on any single year's relative return.

## Risk and money management

Risk is treated at the portfolio level, not the trade level: the book's core risk-management tool is the offense/defense asset-allocation mix scaled to a measured risk-tolerance score, reinforced by the five-year rule (excluding near-term-need money from the risk portfolio entirely) and a hard cap (5%) on any speculative "kitty." Diversification is applied at three nested levels — asset class, fund style, and individual manager — on the logic (citing the Brinson study) that allocation decisions dominate portfolio outcomes far more than security selection. The book also flags inflation as an underweighted risk: over 1934-2001 cited examples show cumulative multi-thousand-percent inflation in prices like postage, cars, and hospital stays, and Hayden stresses evaluating "real return" (return minus inflation) rather than nominal return. Rebalancing back to target allocation — whether on a calendar schedule or when a position's weight drifts materially, as in the 5%-to-15% example — is presented as the main ongoing risk-control mechanism for a long-term portfolio.

## Psychology and discipline

Hayden's "three C's" (commitment, consistency, courage) are explicitly about closing the gap between an investor's stated, intellectual risk tolerance and their actual emotional behavior under stress — he argues that when the two conflict, emotion nearly always wins, citing 1973-74, October 1987, and September 2001 as case studies of investors abandoning otherwise sound plans at the worst possible time. He repeatedly warns against performance-chasing (the physician client who nearly bought into a fund up 251% for the year, which then lost 65% and 51% in the next two years) and against using an aggressive portfolio to "make back" a prior large loss, which he calls a mistake — the right response to a big loss is to reset to a more conservative allocation, not double down. He distinguishes deliberate "active allocation" tilts from market timing, warning that the former can slide into the latter if short-term reactions are allowed to override the long-term plan.

- Ch 2 — Step 2: Know Your Risk Tolerance — financial vs. psychological risk, the five-year rule, the Risk Quiz.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's numeric examples and manager profiles are dated to 2002-era markets and specific named funds (some later closed, merged, or changed managers), so the fund-level recommendations should be read as illustrative rather than current advice — the allocation framework and review process age much better than the specific fund picks. It is written by a practicing, fee-based financial planner with an evident preference for actively managed funds over index funds, and the case made against indexing (that only "5 percent of managers" beat certain periods, so it's worth searching for them) leans on a favorable stretch for active management and does not fully grapple with the larger academic literature on manager persistence. There is no discussion of tax-efficient investing, taxable-account mechanics, or trading costs/turnover beyond a brief mention. As a personal-finance and mutual-fund allocation book, it does not address individual stock selection, technical analysis, or trading mechanics at all — it is a companion to, not a substitute for, the trading- and system-focused books in this library.

## Who should read it

Individual investors and beginning financial planning clients building a first long-term, mutual-fund-based investment plan, who want a structured process for setting goals, sizing risk, allocating a portfolio, and reviewing it on a schedule — not active or short-term traders.
