# Single Stock Futures: A Trader's Guide: the trader's summary

*Mechanics-focused guide to single stock futures: contract specs, margin economics, corporate-action adjustments, and how funds, hedgers, and pairs traders use SSFs versus cash stock, CFDs, and options.*

**Patrick L. Young and Charles Sidey** · 2003 · Options, Futures & Derivatives · intermediate

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 7, 13, 20, 71, 89 (the contract mechanics, margin comparison and the worked pricing examples). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A practitioner's guide to Single Stock Futures (SSFs), written just after their 2002 launch on LIFFE/Euronext, NQLX, and OneChicago. Structured as Q&A plus explanatory chapters, it covers the product's history (dormant since 17th-century Amsterdam options/futures, revived after the Shad–Johnson Accord was eased), how SSFs are built, traded, margined, cleared, and settled, how corporate actions (splits, spin-offs, mergers) force contract-size and strike adjustments, who uses them (hedge funds, pairs traders, tax-driven investors, index arbitrageurs), and how they compare to buying stock on margin, CFDs, and equity swaps.

## Key points

- SSFs are exchange-traded futures on a single underlying stock, typically one contract = 100 shares (1,000 shares for UK Universal Stock Futures), settled physically or in cash depending on the exchange.
- US SSFs (NQLX, OneChicago) launched under SEC/CFTC joint oversight with a mandated 20% initial margin; European SSFs (LIFFE Universal Stock Futures) traded with much lower margins, often 7–10%.
- Contrasts SSF margin (20% in the US) with the standard 50% margin required to buy stock on a cash margin account (150% to short), making SSFs a materially cheaper way to get leveraged equity exposure.
- Corporate actions (stock splits, spin-offs, special dividends) trigger contract-size and reference-price adjustments by the clearing house; the book walks through worked examples (e.g., a reference-price and lot-size change after a special distribution).
- Variation margin is marked to market daily (sometimes intraday in volatile conditions), with the clearing house able to call additional margin as risk changes.
- Key user groups: hedge funds and pairs traders (SSFs reduce margin further on offsetting spread positions), fund managers doing currency-efficient cross-border investing, tax-driven investors, and index arbitrageurs.
- SSFs are compared against margin-traded cash stock, Contracts for Difference (CFDs, which can offer even lower margin to qualified/institutional players), and equity swaps as alternative leveraged-equity vehicles.
- A worked example: buying $5,555 of stock outright versus a single SSF contract at 20% initial margin ($1,112.20) shows a materially higher percentage return on capital for the same dollar move, at the cost of leverage risk.

## Actionable rules

1. US SSF initial margin is set at a minimum 20% of underlying value for both longs and shorts (vs. 50%/150% for cash stock margin accounts) — capital efficiency is the core mechanical advantage over buying stock on margin.
2. European SSF margins were commonly in the 7–10% range at retail level (with some individual stocks trading much wider, e.g., double-digit percentages, based on volatility) — margin is set stock-by-stock based on risk, not fixed market-wide.
3. Spread/pairs positions (long one SSF, short a related one) receive reduced margin versus two outright directional positions, reflecting the lower net risk.
4. After a stock split or special corporate action, expect the contract's lot size and reference/strike price to be adjusted by a ratio calculation rather than the position being closed out — check the exchange's adjustment notice rather than assuming the original 100-share equivalence holds.
5. No specific entry/exit trading rules are given — this is a market-structure and product-mechanics reference, not a system.

## Caveats

Published in 2003, shortly after SSFs launched in the US; several exchanges and products discussed (NQLX, some LIFFE Universal Stock Futures listings) were later discontinued, so the competitive landscape described (which exchanges list which names, whose margins are lowest) is dated. Margin percentages, contract sizes, and specific corporate-action worked examples are anchored to 2002–2003 market conditions and specific stocks (e.g., Exxon Mobil, France Telecom, Swisscom) rather than being general formulas.

## Who it is for

Traders and fund managers evaluating single stock futures as a leverage or hedging tool against cash stock, options, CFDs, and equity swaps, who want the mechanics of margin, settlement, and corporate-action handling rather than a directional trading system.
