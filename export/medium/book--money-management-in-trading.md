# Money Management: the trader's summary

*A Joe Ross chapter cataloguing common money-management mistakes — chasing opinions, overtrading, sunk-cost thinking, margin-inflation surprises — with a fix for each.*

**Joe Ross** · Money Management & Position Sizing · beginner

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-8. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A short chapter by veteran trader Joe Ross (distributed as a free Forex-ebook excerpt) framing money management as fundamentally a psychological discipline rather than a formula. Through a series of short narrative case studies (a trader named Kim who abandons a good short on media noise; Pete, who "over-diversifies" into three trades at once and ends up worse off in all three; a farmer named Tim who over-trusts localized market knowledge; a couple who moves based on one favorable opinion out of dozens), Ross catalogs specific behavioral mistakes traders make with money and position management, each followed by a concrete "what should be done" fix. It closes with a list of additional common errors (confusing trading with investing, copying other traders, ignoring the downside, expecting the "one big trade," and taking too much risk).

## Key points

- Listening to outside opinion (news, "experts," brokers, friends) after already entering a trade tends to shake confidence and cause traders to exit near their own stop for no valid reason — the fix is to set your own guidelines and trade only what you see on the chart.
- "Taking too big a bite": entering multiple diversified positions at once, driven by greed rather than a defined objective, tends to turn a single good trade into a tangled, higher-risk mess.
- Overconfidence from limited or localized "special knowledge" (the farmer example) is a specific trap distinct from general overconfidence — local information does not necessarily generalize to the broader market.
- Preferential bias: once a trader forms a view, they tend to selectively interpret new information (or poll people until they get an answer they like) to confirm it rather than updating on the market's actual behavior.
- Sunk-cost fallacy in trading: rationalizing giving a losing trade "more room" because of commissions, research time, or the wait for the setup already spent — this typically converts a small loss into a much larger one.
- Exaggerated-loss syndrome: weighting the pain of a loss two to three times heavier than the pleasure of an equivalent win, which causes hesitation ("brain lock") on the next trade.
- Traders treat money won on trades as less "real" than invested capital and take bigger risks with it — Ross calls this valuing money differently depending on its source, which has no rational basis.
- "Money illusion" / margin inflation: unrealized paper profits are not usable margin for adding to a position; brokers can and will refuse to let a trader pyramid using open profits, especially as volatility (and margin requirements) rise.
- Additional errors listed: confusing trading with investing, copying other traders' strategies without matching their capital/risk tolerance, ignoring the downside case for every trade, expecting each trade to be "the big one," not reviewing trading goals before entering, trading because "it feels right" without a plan, and simply taking on too much risk (stops too wide, too many positions, too little capital).

## Actionable rules

1. Never adjust or exit a position based on outside opinion (news commentary, broker calls, friends) once your own analysis and stop are already in place.
2. Set a specific profit objective and risk limit for each trade before entering, and achieve or resolve that goal before adding any new position.
3. Do not add to a position using unrealized (paper) profits as margin — treat only realized, settled equity as usable capital.
4. When a trade moves against you, do not "give it more room" to rationalize prior costs (commission, research time, waiting); exit at the pre-planned stop.
5. Before every trade, explicitly answer: what do I stand to gain, what do I stand to lose, and do I have an exit plan if it goes wrong?
6. Treat profits from trading exactly like invested capital — do not take larger risks with "house money."

## Caveats

This is a short, narrative/anecdotal chapter (all examples are illustrative case studies, not data) rather than a quantitative money-management framework — it contains no position-sizing formulas, percent-risk-per-trade guidance, or backtested rules. ## Who it is for

Traders who already have a system but keep sabotaging it through emotional or undisciplined money-management decisions — this is a psychology-of-money-management primer, not a sizing or portfolio-construction reference.
