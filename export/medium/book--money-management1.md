# The Mathematics of Gambling: Section Four — Money Management: the trader's summary

*A gambling-mathematics excerpt showing why staking systems cannot repair a negative edge and introducing optimal growth.*

**Edward O. Thorp** · Money Management & Position Sizing · advanced

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-17. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

This 17-page excerpt from The Mathematics of Gambling contrasts futile betting progressions with mathematically sound allocation when the bettor has a positive edge. Its roulette analysis shows that changing bet size cannot reverse negative expectation. The doubling system produces many small wins and rare catastrophic losses; table limits and finite bankroll make the apparent certainty illusory. The closing section turns to optimal proportional betting and the tension between growth and ruin.

## Key points

- Stake size determines growth and survival even when bets are favorable.
- Betting too aggressively raises ruin risk; betting too little wastes a genuine edge (PDF pp. 1-2).
- Martingale doubling changes the distribution of outcomes, not the house advantage.
- In the worked American-roulette cycle, expected loss remains 5.26% of action despite the progression (PDF p. 6).
- Independent negative-expectation bets remain negative in any finite adaptive sequence.
- Gambler's ruin is possible even when the bettor selects favorable wagers.
- Optimal-growth formulas require reliable probability and payoff estimates.
- A sound staking decision begins with an explicit edge estimate, not recent wins or losses.

## Actionable rules

These are study implications of the analysis; the extra sizing safeguards in rules 3-4 are editorial research precautions, not a quoted formula.

1. Do not use doubling, tripling, or loss-recovery progressions to create an edge; calculate expectation per dollar of total action.
2. Refuse a wager when estimated expectation is nonpositive, regardless of the staking pattern.
3. When an edge is positive, size as a fraction of bankroll and reduce the fraction when the edge estimate is uncertain.
4. Set a hard maximum fraction at risk so an adverse sequence cannot exhaust capital.

## Caveats
