# The Global Money Markets: the trader's summary

*Institutional reference on money-market instruments: T-bills, agency paper, CP, repo, floaters, MBS/ABS, futures/FRAs, swaps, and bank regulatory capital.*

**Frank J. Fabozzi, Steven V. Mann & Moorad Choudhry** · 2002 · Options, Futures & Derivatives · advanced

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: the repo, bank-capital and instrument chapters checked against the note. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

This is a technical, textbook-style reference for fixed-income and money-market professionals rather than a trading strategy book. It walks through the mechanics, pricing conventions, and calculations for the full range of short-term debt instruments used in institutional markets: Treasury bills, agency discount notes, commercial paper, medium-term notes, floating-rate notes, repurchase agreements, short-term mortgage- and asset-backed securities, and the derivatives (futures, forward rate agreements, swaps, caps/floors) used to hedge them. A closing section covers bank asset/liability management and Basel-era regulatory capital rules.

## Key points

- Money market yield conventions differ from bond-equivalent yields; the book works through discount-rate-to-yield conversions for T-bills and commercial paper.
- Floating-rate note valuation depends on quoted margin, index duration, and spread duration — not just coupon level.
- Repo pricing centers on the repo rate, haircut (margin), and delivery requirements; "special" collateral trades at a lower (richer) repo rate than general collateral.
- Repo rates for a specific security in high demand ("on special") can trade far below the general collateral rate, sometimes near zero.
- Overnight repo rates tracked closely with the Fed funds rate in the sample studied, averaging about 8 basis points below it.
- Regulatory (Basel-style) bank capital is split into Tier 1 (core, loss-absorbing) and Tier 2 (supplementary) capital, each with limits relative to risk-weighted assets.
- Collateral posted against secured financing is subject to a haircut reflecting price volatility and liquidity of the underlying security.
- Asset/liability management uses maturity-gap and duration-gap analysis to manage a bank's interest-rate exposure.

## Actionable rules

None given as trader entry/exit rules — this is a reference on instrument mechanics and institutional risk management, not a discretionary or systematic trading system. Practical takeaways for traders: (1) understand that repo "specialness" reflects short-covering demand for specific collateral and can be a signal of crowded short positions; (2) treat quoted margin and duration, not just coupon, when comparing floating-rate securities; (3) apply a haircut/margin buffer when using any security as financing collateral.

## Caveats

Reading was limited to the table of contents, the floating-rate and repo chapters, and the bank capital chapter given the book's length (337 pages) and Tier B budget; other chapters (agency instruments, ABS structuring, swaps/caps mechanics in detail) were not reviewed in depth. The regulatory capital material reflects the Basel I/early Basel II era (published 2002) and predates Basel III; capital ratios and Tier 1/Tier 2 definitions have since changed materially.

## Who it is for

Fixed-income professionals, bank treasury and ALM staff, and serious macro/rates traders who need the mechanics of money-market instruments and short-term financing; not intended for retail directional traders looking for entry/exit systems.
