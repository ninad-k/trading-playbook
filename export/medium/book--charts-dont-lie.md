# Charts Don't Lie: A Complete Beginner's Guide to Candlesticks, Chart Patterns, and Disciplined Trading: the trader's summary

*A beginner-oriented synthesis of five introductory guides into 55 illustrated candlestick and chart patterns, each with a full formation/signal/entry/stop/target box, plus volume, Fibonacci, a short indicator toolkit, a confluence checklist and four fully worked example trades.*

**Ninad K** · Candlesticks & Chart Patterns · beginner

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-48 (full read cover to back cover, including the appendices). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Overview

An "Expanded Edition" self-described as a rewritten synthesis of five introductory trading guides into one beginner's course, illustrated throughout and built around a consistent formula: every one of 55 named patterns gets a short plain-language explanation of *why* it means what it means, followed by a compact box of five fields — Formation, What It Signals, Entry, Stop-Loss, and Exit/Target. The book is organized as a deliberate learning path: mindset and market structure first (Ch1-3), then single-candle signals (Ch4), two- and three-candle patterns (Ch5), reversal chart patterns (Ch6), continuation chart patterns (Ch7), volume (Ch8), Fibonacci retracement (Ch9), a short indicator toolkit (Ch10), a confluence/checklist chapter (Ch11), and a full chapter on risk, money management and discipline (Ch12) — closing with four fully worked example trades, a pattern cheat-sheet, a glossary, and a 90-day study plan. Despite covering essentially the same pattern catalogue found in dozens of other titles in this library, it is unusually disciplined about pairing every pattern with a concrete entry/stop/target rule and about sequencing psychology and risk as the frame the patterns sit inside, rather than an afterthought — which is why it earns Tier A treatment as a beginner's reference despite its short length.

## Core thesis

A market is "just a giant, ongoing auction" and a chart is a record of repeating human behavior, not a crystal ball — patterns work because people react to similar situations similarly, not because the future is predictable. The book's stated core failure mode for beginners is not pattern ignorance but a lack of discipline: trading without a plan, without a stop-loss, on emotion, too often, or too large. Its organizing method is **confluence**: no single tool (a candle, a pattern, an indicator, a Fibonacci level) is trusted alone — a trade is only taken when trend direction, a meaningful support/resistance or Fibonacci level, a completed and closed pattern, and at least one additional confirming tool (volume, an indicator, or a second timeframe) all agree, formalized as an eight-item pre-trade checklist in Chapter 11.

## Key concepts

- **The three-questions habit** — for any single candle, ask who closed in control (green/red), how convincing the move was (body size), and who got rejected (wick length); every named single-candle pattern is a specific answer to these three questions.
- **Support, resistance, and the flip** — a floor/ceiling built from prior turning points; once broken, a level frequently swaps roles (old resistance becomes new support and vice versa).
- **Multi-timeframe habit** — use a higher timeframe to set directional bias, a lower one to time entry; the longer the timeframe, the more reliable its signal.
- **Volume as "lie detector"** — the four price/volume combinations (rising/rising = healthy bullish, rising/falling = bull-trap caution, falling/rising = real bearish, falling/falling = neutral/no interest), read against a roughly 10-day average volume line.
- **Fibonacci retracement/extension** — 23.6/38.2/50/61.8/78.6% retracement levels plotted from a clear swing low to swing high (or reverse); 127.2/161.8/200% extensions used as targets; never traded alone.
- **The indicator toolkit** — Moving Averages (Golden/Death Cross), MACD, RSI (70/30), Bollinger Bands (squeeze), ADX (25 threshold for trend strength), Stochastic (80/20), Supertrend, OBV, plus brief entries on CCI, MFI and Parabolic SAR — explicitly framed as a "second opinion," never the whole decision.
- **Confluence** — stacking independent witnesses (trend + level + pattern + volume/indicator/second-timeframe) before entering; the book's central risk-reduction mechanism, formalized as an eight-item pre-trade checklist.
- **R:R and position sizing** — risking a small, fixed fraction of the account (the book cites roughly 1-2%) per trade, sized so the stop-to-entry distance equals that fraction, with a stated target of at least about 2:1 reward-to-risk.

## Rules and setups

1. **Universal chart-pattern recipe** (Ch6-7): identify the shape after a clear trend, find the trigger line (neckline or S/R edge), wait for a close beyond it ideally on rising volume, measure the target as the pattern's height projected from the breakout, and place the stop on the pattern's opposite side.
2. **Single-candle reversals** (Ch4): e.g., Hammer — small body near the high, long lower wick (~2x body) after a downtrend; enter above the Hammer's high, stop below its wick's low, target the next resistance or per the risk-reward plan. The Doji family, Marubozu, Pin Bar and Spinning Top each get the identical formation/signal/entry/stop/target treatment.
3. **Two/three-candle patterns** (Ch5): e.g., Bullish Engulfing — a small red candle fully engulfed by a larger green one after a downtrend; enter above the engulfing candle's high, stop below its low. Harami, Piercing/Dark Cloud Cover, Tweezer Tops/Bottoms, Kickers, Inside Bars, Morning/Evening Star and the "Three ___" confirmation patterns (Three White Soldiers, Three Inside Up, Three Outside Up, etc.) follow the same box format.
4. **Reversal chart patterns** (Ch6): Double/Triple Top and Bottom, Head and Shoulders (and inverse), Rounding Bottom, Diamond Top/Bottom — each entered on a neckline/boundary break, stopped beyond the pattern's defining extreme, targeted at the pattern's own height projected from the break.
5. **Continuation chart patterns** (Ch7): Flags, Pennants, Rectangles, Ascending/Descending/Symmetrical Triangles, Rising/Falling Wedges, Cup and Handle (and inverted), Broadening Triangle — traded with the established trend, entered on the breakout candle's high/low, targeted at the prior pole's height (flags/pennants) or the pattern's own height (triangles/rectangles/wedges).
6. **Fibonacci entry** (Ch9): in an uptrend pullback, enter when price reaches a key level (commonly 50% or 61.8%) and shows a resuming signal (e.g., a candlestick reversal), stop just beyond the next level out (e.g., below 78.6% or the swing low), target via 127.2/161.8/200% extensions.
7. **Pre-trade checklist** (Ch11): before any entry, confirm (1) higher-timeframe trend direction, (2) price at a meaningful level, (3) a completed, closed pattern signal, (4) at least one more confirming tool, (5) an exact entry trigger, (6) an exact stop-loss, (7) reward-to-risk of roughly 2:1 or better, (8) a position size that keeps the stop-loss small.

## Risk and money management

Chapter 12 is explicit and non-negotiable on three points: every trade gets a stop-loss decided *before* entry, with no exceptions and no moving it once placed (the book calls moving a stop out of hope "the most expensive habit in trading"); position size is whatever makes the stop-to-entry distance equal a small, fixed fraction of the account (the book cites roughly 1-2%, without deriving the figure from any formula or backtest); and reward-to-risk should be at least about 2:1, so a trader can be wrong more often than right and still be profitable over time — the explicit "mathematical reason discipline beats prediction." A short discipline checklist reinforces this: follow a written plan, never enter without a stop, require confluence rather than a single signal, wait for the candle to close before acting, don't chase a missed move, don't overtrade, and treat a disciplined loss as "a good trade" while a reckless win is "a bad one that happened to pay."

## Psychology and discipline

The book opens (not closes) with the claim that chart knowledge is "the easy part" and that discipline, not pattern recognition, separates traders who last from those who blow up — a mindset argument placed deliberately in Chapter 1 before a single candle is introduced. Its four explicit asks of the reader are to finish learning before risking money, to paper-trade a demo account first, to keep real-money exposure tiny while learning, and to keep a trade journal recording both outcomes and feelings. It reframes the goal away from being "right": traders will lose a large fraction of individual trades even with a working method, so the measure of a good trade is whether the plan was followed, not whether that specific trade won. The closing "Next 90 Days" chapter operationalizes this into a three-stage plan — 30 days of pattern-spotting with no real trading, 30 days of disciplined paper trading run through the Chapter 11 checklist, and 30 days of journaling and review — before any real capital is deployed.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's real strength for a beginner is consistency and completeness: every one of 55 patterns gets the identical five-field box, so a reader can flip to any pattern and immediately see an actionable entry/stop/target rather than only a definition — a level of discipline many longer, more "authoritative" pattern encyclopedias in this library do not maintain throughout. The four worked example trades in Appendix A are genuinely useful teaching devices, walking a full trend/level/signal/confirmation/entry/stop/target/R:R sequence end to end. The caveats: this is, by its own admission, a synthesis rather than original research, and the pattern catalogue itself is the standard one found across dozens of other candlestick/chart-pattern books in this library (Engulfing, Harami, Head and Shoulders, the triangle family, etc.) — a reader who already owns one or two solid pattern references gains little new pattern *content* here, only a cleaner presentation and a stronger risk-management frame around it. No numeric win rates, backtests, or statistical support are given for any pattern's reliability (a sound and honestly-stated omission, since the book never claims otherwise), and the worked examples use round, illustrative prices rather than real historical charts. The reward-to-risk (~2:1) and position-sizing (~1-2%) figures are stated as reasonable defaults, not derived or sourced.

## Who should read it

An excellent true first book for someone who has never opened a price chart: it deliberately orders psychology and market structure before patterns, gives every pattern a complete, actionable rule rather than a vague description, and ends with risk management and a concrete 90-day practice plan rather than trailing off after the pattern catalogue. A trader who already knows standard candlestick and chart-pattern terminology from another Tier A or B source in this library (e.g., Nison, Bulkowski-style encyclopedias, or Connors' Street Smarts) will find the pattern content largely redundant and should read this mainly for its confluence checklist and risk-management framing.
