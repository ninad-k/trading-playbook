# Stock Trader's Almanac 2005: the trader's summary

*A 2005 calendar and databank of U.S. equity seasonality, political cycles, holiday effects, and record-keeping tools.*

**Yale Hirsch and Jeffrey A. Hirsch** · 2005 · Investing, Value & Market History · intermediate

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 1-18, 24, 39, 41-42, 48-53, 55, 59, 116, 138 (the strategy-summary front matter, the Best Six Months and MACD-timing pages, the January Barometer and the seasonal-pattern tables). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

The 38th Stock Trader's Almanac organizes U.S. market history around the calendar. It tabulates monthly, daily, holiday, option-expiration, presidential-cycle, and decennial tendencies, then supplies portfolio records and planning forms. Its signature claim is that Dow and S&P gains since 1950 were concentrated in November through April, while NASDAQ's favorable span ran November through June. The edition adds MACD-based timing to vary the exact seasonal entry and exit (PDF pp. 49-59).

## Key points

- November, December, January, and April ranked among the strongest months in the 1950–mid-2004 sample; September was the weakest (PDF p. 49).
- The fixed Dow “Best Six Months” held from November 1 through April 30.
- The almanac reports a $10,000 Dow illustration growing to $492,060 during favorable halves versus $9,682 during May–October, before costs and taxes (PDF p. 51).
- MACD timing begins looking for entry after October 1 and exit after April 1 (PDF p. 53).
- NASDAQ's historical favorable span was November through June, with July–October the weak interval.
- Political-cycle and January indicators are historical associations, not causal laws.
- The calendar also flags holiday and expiration-day behavior based on finite samples.

## Actionable rules

1. For the fixed seasonal test, hold the Dow proxy from November 1 through April 30 and fixed income from May through October (PDF p. 51).
2. For the timed version, accept the first bullish MACD signal after October 1 and the first bearish signal after April 1; define the MACD parameters before testing because this excerpt does not provide them (PDF p. 53).
3. For NASDAQ, test November–June against July–October separately rather than importing Dow dates (PDF pp. 55, 59).

## The Almanac's headline seasonal strategies

- **Best Six Months Switching Strategy** (discovered 1986, PDF pp. 51-52) — hold the Dow from 1 November to 30 April and switch to fixed income from 1 May to 31 October. Over the 54 years from 1950, the November-April half gained 10,599.68 Dow points while the May-October half lost 588.44; on the S&P the split was 1,089.23 points against 62.09. Compounding $10,000 through the November-April periods produced a $482,060 gain versus a $318 loss for May-October ($349,165 versus $7,102 on the S&P). Only two November-April periods lost double digits.
- **MACD timing on top of it** (PDF pp. 53, 59) — applying a simple MACD trigger to shift the entry and exit off the calendar dates is presented as roughly tripling the switching strategy's result, and the same treatment is applied to the NASDAQ's "Best Eight Months"; the Almanac notes the MACD-generated entry and exit points can lengthen or shorten the six-month window.
- **January Barometer** (devised 1972, PDF p. 24) — January's direction as a forecast for the year, reported as 90.7% accurate, with the Almanac attributing the mechanism to the Twentieth ("Lame Duck") Amendment moving the political calendar.
- **Santa Claus Rally** (PDF pp. 12, 116) — the late-December window, with the Almanac's warning framing ("if Santa Claus should fail to call").
- **Day-of-week effect** (PDF p. 8) — the 2005 edition reports Monday as then the most profitable day of the week, a reversal of the long-standing weak-Monday pattern.

## Caveats

All statistics end by mid-2004 and omit modern execution, taxes, and regime changes. Many calendar effects are tested, creating selection bias; performance figures are not a current validation. The edition's specific 2005 forecasts are historical artifacts.

## Who it is for

Researchers and tactical allocators studying historical calendar effects as testable hypotheses.
