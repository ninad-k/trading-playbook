# Letting Your Profits Run; When Not To Sell: the trader's summary

*Short essay arguing that staying in winning trades is a harder, less-discussed discipline than cutting losses, and offering mental techniques for holding profitable positions longer.*

**Market Wise Stock Trading School** · 1999 · Trading Psychology & Discipline · beginner

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-3. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A three-page essay from Market Wise Stock Trading School (1999) that argues most trading education over-emphasizes cutting losses and under-emphasizes the harder skill of staying in a winning trade long enough to capture its full potential. The piece is entirely qualitative — no indicators, setups, or numeric rules — and works through several mental frameworks for resisting the urge to exit winners too early, aimed at short-term stock traders.

## Key points

- Discipline is usually taught as "cut losses quickly," but the discipline to stay in a trade that is working is comparably important and less discussed.
- Historical studies cited (uncredited) suggest most traders are wrong more often than right, yet professionals profit because winning trades are pressed for maximum gain while losses are kept small.
- Scaling out (e.g., selling half a position, holding the rest) is offered as a practical way to bank some profit while staying exposed to further upside.
- Price level alone should not determine an exit; traders should assess how a stock is "acting" (order behavior, fills) rather than fixating on a specific price target.
- A suggested mental exercise: imagine having no position and ask whether you would enter fresh at the current price — if not, reconsider staying, but if the setup still looks valid, give the trade room.
- Another exercise: imagine the trader on the other side of the trade and assess whether their risk/reward looks attractive; if the counterparty's position looks weak, that favors staying in.
- Confidence in a predetermined exit point is described as a prerequisite for holding profitable trades without becoming "trigger happy" on minor retracements.
- Patience and acceptance that "there will always be another trade" are framed as necessary complements to staying in winners.

## Actionable rules

None given as numbered or quantified rules — this is a psychology/mindset essay, not a system. The closest actionable takeaways: (1) consider scaling out (e.g., 50%) of a winning position rather than exiting all at once; (2) set a predetermined exit point in advance and commit to it rather than second-guessing intraday; (3) evaluate exits by how the stock/order flow is behaving, not solely by price level.

## Caveats

No specific numbers, stop levels, or position-sizing guidance are given anywhere in the text; the "historical studies" and win-rate claims (e.g., professionals winning ~30% of trades) are asserted without citation. The essay predates decimalization-era commentary common in later stock literature but contains no dated technical content otherwise. Content is a promotional educational piece from a trading school rather than an independently verifiable study.

## Who it is for

Short-term stock traders who already cut losses reliably but tend to exit winning trades too early, and who want mental frameworks (not mechanical rules) for holding profitable positions longer.
