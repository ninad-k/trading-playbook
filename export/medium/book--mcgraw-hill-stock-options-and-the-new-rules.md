# Stock Options and the New Rules of Corporate Accountability: the trader's summary

*A governance-focused case for valuing and expensing executive options while redesigning incentives around performance.*

**Donald P. Delves** · 2004 · Options, Futures & Derivatives · intermediate

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 1-18, 80-110. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Donald Delves examines executive stock options as an accounting cost, incentive, and governance problem. His case is that large fixed-price grants rewarded broad market appreciation as much as managerial contribution, encouraged short-term stock-price focus, and escaped adequate board scrutiny. He argues for expensing options, recognizing their economic value at grant, and using a balanced portfolio of salary, annual incentives, performance-based long-term awards, and earned ownership.

## Key points

- A company granting a call option exchanges a valuable claim on future appreciation for employee services (PDF pp. 105-107).
- Executive options differ from traded calls through long terms, vesting, non-transferability, blackout periods, and early exercise.
- The option's cost to the company can exceed its subjective value to an undiversified employee (PDF pp. 108-109).
- Valuation models can incorporate expected life and exercise behavior; poor inputs do not make the claim valueless.
- Start-ups may rationally conserve cash with options, while mature companies have fewer reasons for mega-grants.
- Independent directors and leadership are necessary for credible compensation oversight (PDF p. 15).
- Performance vesting and relative measures can connect rewards more closely to contribution.

## Actionable rules

1. Record an option's grant-date economic cost using a defensible model and disclose all material assumptions.
2. Model expected term rather than contractual term when evidence shows exercise after vesting; Delves notes 10-year grants were often exercised after five to seven years (PDF pp. 107-109).
3. Compare company cost with employee-perceived value before choosing options over cash or stock.
4. Make substantial awards vest on long-term performance and require meaningful earned ownership.
5. Have independent directors test dilution, downside incentives, peer comparisons, and total realizable pay.

## Caveats

The accounting debate reflects the early 2000s and predates later standards. Examples are interviews and cases, not causal estimates of incentive effectiveness. This is a partial source review.

## Who it is for

Board members, compensation professionals, accountants, and investors evaluating equity-pay design.
