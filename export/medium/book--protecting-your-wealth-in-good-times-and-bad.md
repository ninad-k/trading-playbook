# Protecting Your Wealth in Good Times and Bad: the trader's summary

*Life-stage guide to saving, fees, and asset allocation, using the 2000-2002 bear market to show how herding and high costs wreck retirement accounts.*

**Richard A. Ferri** · 2003 · Investing, Value & Market History · beginner

*Source coverage: partial. PDF pages inspected: 9, 23, 52, 65-66 (the index-versus-active comparison tables and the turnover discussion). These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Written in the aftermath of the 2000-2002 bear market (S&P 500 down over 40%, Nasdaq over 80%), this is a personal-finance and retirement-planning book, not a trading manual. Ferri, an investment advisor, argues that most people's retirement shortfalls come from three behaviors: trying to time the market, chasing hot sectors after they've already run, and overpaying for advice and fund fees. The book is organized in three parts: mistakes investors make (Part One), the mechanics of accounts, stocks, bonds, and asset allocation (Part Two), and a life-stage playbook for Early Savers, Midlife Accumulators, Pre-Retirees/Retirees, and Experienced Retirees (Part Three).

## Key points

- **Herding** — investors pile into a hot sector after prices have already risen; DALBAR data cited shows the average equity-fund investor earned 8.5% (1991-2000) versus 15.6% for the average fund and 17.3% for the Vanguard 500 Index.
- **Turnover kills returns** — the average holding period for equity funds is 2-3 years; frequent traders and men (per Barber/Odean research) underperform buy-and-hold investors and women, respectively.
- **Fee drag** — among 97 Growth & Income funds with a 15-year record, the cheapest quartile (<0.5% expense ratio) returned 9.2% annualized versus 7.1% for the most expensive quartile; the pattern repeats in small-cap, foreign, and bond funds.
- **Broker incentives** — commissions, 12b-1 trailing fees, and sales contests bias recommendations toward high-cost products; the author discloses receiving sales prizes (a mountain bike, a trip) early in his own brokerage career.
- **Account vs. custodian vs. investment** — a retirement "account" (IRA, 401(k)) is just a tax wrapper; the custodian holds it; the investment (stocks, funds) is what's actually purchased inside it.
- **Pre-tax vs. Roth** — pre-tax contributions lower current taxable income but are taxed on withdrawal; Roth-type accounts are funded after-tax but withdraw tax-free.
- **Case study "Ed"** — a disciplined saver who abandoned a simple CD/stock-fund plan to day-trade tech stocks in 1999-2000, losing over 60% of a $700,000 nest egg.

## Actionable rules

1. Build a written asset-allocation plan and rebalance it on a schedule rather than reacting to market moves.
2. Favor no-load, low-expense-ratio funds (the book flags index funds and ETFs); ask any advisor exactly how much they earn from a recommendation.
3. Diversify across U.S./foreign, large/small, and growth/value equity styles rather than concentrating in one employer's stock or one hot sector.
4. Match account type to tax situation: use pre-tax accounts for immediate deduction, after-tax/Roth vehicles for tax-free withdrawals later.
5. Reassess allocation and withdrawal strategy at each life stage (Early Saver → Midlife Accumulator → Pre-Retiree/Retiree → Experienced Retiree).

## Caveats

Dollar figures, contribution limits, and fee benchmarks are 2003-era and outdated (401(k) caps, Social Security rules, and typical fund fees have all changed materially since). The book is aimed at long-horizon savers and retirees, not active traders — it argues directly against most short-term trading approaches found elsewhere in this library.

## Who it is for

Long-term savers, near-retirees, and anyone managing a 401(k)/IRA who wants a plain-language case for low-cost, diversified, buy-and-hold investing rather than active trading.
