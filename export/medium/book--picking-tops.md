# Picking Tops And Bottoms With The Tick Index: the trader's summary

*Stocks & Commodities article on using extreme NYSE uptick/downtick (TICK) readings and a double-top/double-bottom pattern to call short-term market turns.*

**Tim Ord** · 1991 · Indicators · intermediate

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-4. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

A short 1991 Technical Analysis of Stocks & Commodities article by Tim Ord describing a contrarian method for timing DJIA tops and bottoms using the NYSE tick index (upticks minus downticks). Reasoning from the premise that roughly 80% of traders lose money, Ord fades extreme one-sided tick readings, using a double-extreme pattern confirmed by volume to flag reversals one to ten trading days apart.

## Key points

- **Tick index** — intraday upticks minus downticks across NYSE issues; a real-time proxy for one-sided participation.
- **Contrarian logic** — extreme positive tick readings warn of a top; extreme negative readings warn of a bottom.
- **Double top/bottom trigger** — a sell signal is the second of two extreme positive tick readings forming a double top in the DJIA (same day up to 10 trading days apart); buy signal mirrors this with extreme downticks.
- **Volume confirmation** — higher volume on the extreme reading implies a more significant, reliable reversal.
- **Historical example** — downtick extremes of 890 (1/9/1991) and 880 (1/14/1991) near DJIA 2470 on heavy volume (191M, 121M shares) preceded a ~500-point rally.
- **Author's own caveat** — the tick index is "not always as accurate" and works best as a confirming tool, not standalone.

## Actionable rules

1. Watch for an extreme (unusually large positive or negative) intraday NYSE tick reading.
2. Sell signal: a double top in the DJIA where both highs align with extreme positive tick readings; short on the second extreme.
3. Buy signal: a double bottom where both lows align with extreme negative tick readings; buy on the second extreme.
4. The two tests can occur the same day or up to 10 trading days apart.
5. Require above-average volume on the extreme reading to treat it as significant.
6. No stop-loss, position sizing, or profit target is given — the article covers signal generation only.

## Caveats

No numeric threshold defines "extreme," no backtested win rate is given, and only one historical example (January 1991) is shown. It predates decimalization and today's much larger NYSE share count, both of which shift typical tick-index magnitudes, so any implied thresholds need re-calibration on modern data.

## Who it is for

Short-term index traders who already track breadth/tick data and want a simple contrarian breadth-extreme concept to combine with other confirming tools, not a complete standalone method.
