# Reverse Divergences and Momentum: the trader's summary

*Stocks & Commodities article defining 'reverse divergence' — where price, not the oscillator, leads a discrepancy at a turning point — as a distinct, normally reliable reversal signal.*

**Martin J. Pring** · 1997 · Indicators · intermediate

*Source coverage: full. PDF pages inspected: 1-4. These are study notes, not verified trading results.*

## Summary

Martin J. Pring's short technical-analysis article distinguishes "reverse divergence" from ordinary momentum divergence. In normal divergence, the oscillator fails to confirm a new price extreme. In a reverse divergence, the roles switch: price makes a lower high (or higher low) while the oscillator makes a new, more extreme high (or low) — price leads the momentum indicator into the turn. Pring argues this is normally reliable because price sums many overlapping cycles while a single oscillator captures only one component; when price turns ahead of the oscillator, it signals a shift in the dominant cycle.

## Key points

- Normal divergence: oscillator fails to confirm a new price extreme (classic textbook divergence).
- Reverse divergence at a top: price makes a lower high at point B while the oscillator makes a *higher* high at B.
- Reverse divergence at a bottom: mirror-image — price makes its final low first, oscillator bottoms afterward.
- Can also appear inside a trading range: price repeatedly tests the same level while the oscillator keeps making new extremes, typically resolving in a trend reversal.
- Should be confirmed by an independent trend-reversal signal (trendline break or MA crossover), since it can fail like any divergence.
- Longer-period oscillators imply reversals of a larger trend than short-period oscillators.
- Valid only on raw (unsmoothed) oscillator data — smoothing delays the turning point and can produce a false signal.
- Pring's preferred oscillator is rate of change (ROC); RSI, the Chande momentum oscillator, and the demand index also work.
- Worked example: British pound with 39-week ROC — the momentum low nearly coincided with the 1986 price low, and a multi-year oscillator low coincided with a 1989 double-bottom.

## Actionable rules

1. Identify a reverse divergence: price makes a new (lower high / higher low) extreme while the oscillator makes a *more* extreme reading than its prior swing.
2. Require two peaks (or troughs) in both price and the oscillator to validate the pattern.
3. Confirm with an independent trend-reversal signal (trendline break or MA crossover) before acting.
4. Use raw, unsmoothed oscillator values (e.g., ROC) rather than a smoothed one, since smoothing delays and can invalidate the signal.
5. Match oscillator time span to the size of trend being traded.

## Caveats

The technique is presented with only two worked chart examples (British pound and Luby's Cafeterias) and no systematic backtest or win-rate statistics; it is a discretionary pattern-recognition concept rather than a mechanically defined system.

## Who it is for

Technical traders already familiar with standard momentum-divergence analysis who want an additional, less commonly taught reversal signal to watch for at tops, bottoms, and trading-range boundaries.
