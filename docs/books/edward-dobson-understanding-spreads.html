Understanding Spreads

Summary

A short Traders Press booklet introducing futures spread trading — taking simultaneous long and short positions in two related contracts and profiting from the change in their price differential rather than from outright directional price movement. It covers why economically related markets maintain price relationships, catalogs the main spread types, weighs spreads’ advantages and disadvantages against outright positions, and closes with a section on seasonal spread tendencies and how to develop a seasonal approach.

Key points

  • A spread is a combined long/short position in two related futures contracts (same commodity different month, same commodity different exchange, or economically linked products); the “related” requirement rules out spurious computer-generated pairings.
  • Five fundamental relationship types: substitute relationships (e.g., corn/soybeans/cotton competing for acreage), product relationships (soybean crush, crude oil crack spreads), usage spreads (hog/corn ratio), carrying-charge spreads, and price-difference spreads (no cost of carry, e.g., interbank currencies).
  • Spreads generally require less margin than outright positions and are less volatile because they behave like hedged positions.
  • Spreads often trend even when outright markets are flat, letting traders apply seasonality studies, carrying-charge analysis, and standard technical indicators.
  • Order entry lets a trader “leg” in or out, offers real-time-data savings, avoids stop-hunting since exits are set on the differential rather than an absolute price, and keeps the position more confidential from floor/local traders.
  • Key risk noted: both legs of a spread can lose simultaneously, which magnifies risk rather than limiting it, despite the perception that spreads are automatically “safer.”
  • Spread trading requires a valid economic theory for why the relationship should hold — testing for a pattern first and then finding a theory to explain it is flagged as risky practice.
  • Seasonal categories: intra-seasonal (two contracts within the same crop year) vs. inter-seasonal (across crop years); long-term seasonal patterns (a month or two) are described as more reliable than short-term ones.

Actionable rules

None given as position sizes, entry triggers, or numeric stop levels — the book is conceptual/educational rather than a mechanical system. Its core actionable guidance: (1) only trade spreads with a genuine underlying economic relationship, not statistically coincidental correlations; (2) size positions with the awareness that both legs can lose together; (3) favor longer-duration seasonal patterns (weeks to months) over short-term ones for reliability; (4) quote and track spreads by listing the long leg first, per industry convention.

Caveats

This is an introductory booklet (42 pages) explicitly positioned as filling a literature gap, not a comprehensive spread-trading manual — it points readers to further resources (e.g., John Murphy’s intermarket work, Steve Moore’s mrci.com) rather than providing full technical detail itself. Published in 2007 discussing tax-code changes from the early 1980s that reduced favorable capital-gains treatment for certain spread trades; current tax treatment should be independently verified.

Who it is for

Futures traders who already understand outright directional trading and want an introduction to spread trading’s mechanics, relationship types, and seasonal edge; not a complete standalone system for a beginner.
