Zoom In on Personal Trading Behavior and Profit from It

Linda Bradford Raschke · 2003 · Tier B article intermediate

Raschke gives a practical routine for detecting personal error patterns, matching behavior to market conditions, and reducing unforced trades.

Summary

Raschke argues that traders should study recurring errors in their own behavior as carefully as they study market patterns. She gives examples: chasing a missed trade, becoming complacent after an equity high, letting a winner turn into a loser, averaging down, and overtrading when tired or frustrated (p. 3). Her method is observational rather than academic: identify the conditions that precede mistakes, notice physical signs of focus or anxiety, keep a daily plan and records, use routines, set small behavior goals, and separate a difficult market environment from self-created errors (pp. 3-5). The article says the initial trading process is to identify an edge, set an entry trigger and initial risk, then manage for confirmation or non-confirmation (p. 1).

Key points

  • The self-audit asks whether losses cluster by time of day, trade type, failure to follow a premarket plan, outside distraction, large one-day errors, or burnout (p. 3).
  • Raschke uses bodily state as feedback: feeling relaxed can accompany a trade working as planned, while shallow breathing and staring at the screen can warn that something feels wrong (p. 3).
  • She says markets often alternate between flat, choppy, and trending periods and estimates that traders encounter one or two dull periods a year (p. 4).
  • Recordkeeping can include trades, statistics, or market indicators; the purpose is to stay present and consistent with the plan (p. 4).
  • A trusted trading friend can provide accountability but should not supply market opinions or decide individual trades (pp. 4-5).

Actionable rules

  1. Before each session, define a trading plan appropriate to the expected market type; use it to screen out spontaneous marginal trades (p. 4).
  2. Track the specific times, setups, distractions, emotions, and physical cues that precede unforced errors, then stop trading when known burnout signs appear (pp. 3-4).
  3. Set a small daily behavior goal suited to the trader’s weakness, such as limiting trade count or following the plan for one day, and use a resting stop until enough experience exists to recognize a bad trade earlier (pp. 4-5).

Caveats

The article is based on Raschke’s experience and offers no controlled behavioral study or performance data. Physical feelings are subjective and can be misread. The examples of three trades per day and half-size entries are possible personal goals, not universal limits, and the article supplies no stop distance or position-size percentage.

Who it is for

Traders studying why emotionally consistent plans are difficult to execute.
