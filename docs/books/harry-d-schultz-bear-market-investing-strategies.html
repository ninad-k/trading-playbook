Harry D. Schultz · 2002 · Tier A — deep note book intermediate

Overview

Harry Schultz, a 38-year veteran investment-letter writer, wrote this book (Wiley Trading Series, 2002) after the dot-com crash to give investors a systematic way to recognize bear markets, survive them with capital intact, and where possible profit via short selling and defensive tactics. It blends classical Dow Theory concepts (the “50% principle,” secondary reactions, bear market “legs”), a survey of every major bear market from 1900-2001 with quantified declines and durations, a set of technical tools (moving averages, breadth and sentiment indicators), and explicit stage-based playbooks for the early, middle, and late stages of a bear market. It is a tactics book rather than a single mechanical system: value comes from accumulated numeric rules of thumb rather than one formula.

Core thesis

Bear markets are a recurring, statistically regular feature of markets (Schultz calculates the market has spent roughly 35% of history in bear-market conditions), and most investor losses come not from bear markets occurring but from failing to recognize which stage of the cycle one is in and acting against one’s own emotions accordingly. Because “buy and hold” behaves very differently across a full bull-bear cycle than within a single bull leg, Schultz argues investors need a stage-dependent playbook: sell into strength as the bull market’s late stage develops, navigate the deceptive “secondary reactions” (sharp counter-trend rallies) that dominate the bear market’s middle, and commit new capital only once late-stage, technically confirmed bottoming signs appear. Short selling, done with disciplined stops, is presented as a legitimate and often safer profit source than staying only in cash.

Key concepts

  • The 50% principle (Dow’s “Great Law of Action and Reaction”) — Dow’s 1900 observation that rallies and reactions tend to retrace roughly half the preceding primary move; holding above 50% favors the prior trend resuming, a break below signals reversal.
  • Secondary reaction — a sizable (often 30-60%, sometimes 66%+) counter-trend rally within a bear market’s middle stage; per Robert Rhea’s research, roughly 7% of reactions retrace 40-55%, 27% retrace 55-70%, 8% retrace 70-85%, 14% exceed 85%.
  • Bear market “legs” / stages — early, middle (secondary-reaction-dominated), and late, each requiring a different tactical response.
  • Contrary opinion — deliberately doubting consensus statements from officials, executives, and media, on the premise the majority is usually wrong at turning points.
  • “Taking a View” — an explicit, weekly-reviewed macro thesis about the market/economy’s stage, against which technical signals are interpreted.
  • Preservation of capital — the primary objective, ranked above profit-seeking; requires a worst-case risk assessment before any position is taken.
  • Odd-lot short-sale index — small-trader short-selling activity as a 10-day moving average; normal range roughly 7-13%, below 10% bullish, above bearish.
  • Breadth/internal indicators — percentage-of-advances index (normal range 30-60%; below 40% signals weakness), built as 10-day moving averages.
  • Moving-average trend tools — 5-day/10-day for short-term work; 30-week/40-week DJIA averages for the primary trend, with price penetration flagged as a trend-change alert.
  • Dividend-yield extremes — DJIA average yield as a valuation/fear gauge (example: 3.3% at the 1929 peak rose to 10.3% during the crash).

Rules and setups

This book does not present one entry/exit mechanical system; its “rules” are stage-dependent tactics and numeric thresholds.

1. Late-bull-market stage: sell common stock across the board even while still “rather sure” the averages have upside left; don’t try to catch the exact top. Use stop-loss orders 5-15% below current price, set relative to the short-term uptrend line on a daily chart.

2. Middle-bear-market stage (secondary reaction in progress) — five choices: 1. Sell in stages into the rally’s strength; a bear-market secondary reaction’s top is often the best price available for 1-5 years. 2. Optionally buy a small tranche (10-25% of a normal position, capped at 25-30% of total commitment) of chart-strong stocks with limited downside, for a tactical long during the reaction. 3. Sell short near the reaction’s top, where a stop-loss can sit close above the recent high, minimizing risk. 4. Sell out and stay out, rotating into Treasury bonds or other defensive paper, or (inflationary bear market) tangible assets like gold or real estate. 5. Never trade against the primary trend: short on down-legs, cover on secondary reactions, never go long against a confirmed bear.

3. Late-bear-market stage: begin accumulating; prefer low-priced or low-priced medium-quality stocks (these historically lead a new bull market’s first phase), with blue chips lagging; watch relative strength for the earliest leaders.

4. Short-selling rules (Ch. 13): protect every short with a stop-buy placed the same day, at 10-15% above the short-sale price (chart-based, not arbitrary); prefer heavily traded blue chips over thin issues, avoiding small-float or already-heavily-shorted stocks; short at the start of a decline, not after a sharp fall already occurred; then-current U.S. rules required an uptick before a short could execute.

5. Portfolio allocation: cites John Templeton’s practice of never committing more than 50% of a portfolio to long equities even at bull-market peaks, balance in bonds/defensive issues when the trend outlook was uncertain — a model more conservative investors can adapt (Templeton could afford more risk than most, given his wealth).

6. Bear-market diversification: in a highly unstable global environment, Schultz suggests 40-60% foreign vs. domestic exposure (loosened from an older 50/50 rule) to reduce single-market risk.

Risk and money management

Risk control centers on stop-loss discipline rather than a fixed percent-of-equity formula: stops are set the same day a position opens (longs exiting a bull top, or new shorts), typically 5-15% from entry based on the chart, and warned against being removed once emotions attach to a position. Position sizing during secondary reactions is explicitly capped (10-25% of a normal position, no more than 25-30% of total capital) to limit damage if a counter-trend rally is misread as a reversal. Schultz frames capital preservation as strictly senior to profit-seeking: determine the worst-case outcome before any trade. He also warns “buy and hold” investors not to assume they can safely ride out a bear decline, arguing tactics for long-, medium-, and short-term investors should converge during a confirmed bear market.

Psychology and discipline

Schultz treats emotional self-management as the deciding factor in whether an investor can execute the book’s tactics at all: the hardest part of selling into a bear-market rally is resisting media narrative, brokers’ bullishness, and one’s own bias, which combine to make investors rationalize away bearish signals. He advocates deliberate “contrary opinion” — treating consensus statements from officials as more likely wrong than right at turning points — and preparing emotionally for how difficult a bear phase will feel before entering it. He is candid that shorting is psychologically harder than buying for most investors, which is why he frames it as a source of edge for those willing to build the discipline gradually.

Strengths and caveats

The historical bear-market survey (Ch. 3) is a genuinely useful reference table of declines and durations, though Schultz sources some figures to his own career of observation rather than one citable dataset, and a few percentages are given as approximate or disputed. Some tools (the odd-lot short-sale index, the dividend-yield levels cited) are calibrated to a pre-decimalization, pre-electronic market structure and are harder to reproduce with modern data; the odd-lot indicator specifically has been widely noted elsewhere as having lost predictive value once odd-lot trading stopped proxying unsophisticated retail behavior. The short-uptick rule for shorts, current US law when written, was later eliminated by the SEC (2007). The book is a tactics compendium rather than a backtested system: none of the numeric thresholds carry formal statistical validation, leaning instead on decades of discretionary newsletter-writing experience.

Who should read it

Investors and traders who want a structured, historically grounded playbook for navigating bear markets rather than a single indicator or entry system — particularly those willing to consider short selling as part of their toolkit and who want explicit stage-based rules for when to sell, hedge, short, or sit in cash. Less useful for readers seeking a rigorously backtested mechanical system or exposure to modern derivatives-based hedging (options, VIX products) as the book’s defensive toolkit predates their widespread retail use.
