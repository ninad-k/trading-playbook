Overview

The Warren Buffett Way (2nd edition) reconstructs Warren Buffett’s investment philosophy by analyzing his actual purchase decisions at Berkshire Hathaway — Coca-Cola, The Washington Post, Wells Fargo, GEICO, American Express, and others — and extracting the recurring principles behind them. Hagstrom organizes those principles into twelve tenets across four categories (business, management, financial, value), traces their intellectual lineage back to Benjamin Graham, Philip Fisher, and John Burr Williams, and closes with Buffett’s concentrated “focus investing” approach to portfolio construction.

Core thesis

Buffett evaluates a stock purchase the way he would evaluate buying the entire business: by analyzing its economics, its managers, and its financial characteristics, then comparing a calculated intrinsic value to the market price. Price and value are treated as distinct things (“Price is what you pay. Value is what you get”), and a purchase is only justified when a sufficient discount — a margin of safety — exists between the two. This business-analyst mindset, applied with discipline and patience over long holding periods, is what separates Buffett’s approach from both short-term speculation and mechanical value screens.

Key concepts

  • Circle of competence — restricting investment to businesses whose economics the investor can genuinely understand and forecast; Buffett stresses defining the boundary of the circle precisely, not maximizing its size.
  • Margin of safety — Benjamin Graham’s concept, inherited by Buffett: buy only when price is meaningfully below calculated intrinsic value, creating a cushion against error and volatility.
  • Intrinsic value — the discounted present value of a business’s expected future “owner earnings,” per John Burr Williams’s dividend/cash-flow discount model, which Buffett treats as the single valid yardstick across asset types.
  • Owner earnings — net income plus depreciation/depletion/amortization and other noncash charges, minus the capital expenditures required to maintain the business’s competitive position and unit volume; Buffett’s preferred substitute for reported earnings or naive cash flow.
  • Institutional imperative — Buffett’s term for the tendency of corporate managers to imitate the actions of peer companies (acquisitions, capital allocation, strategy) regardless of rational business logic; a red flag when evaluating management.
  • Franchise vs. commodity business — a franchise sells a product/service that is needed or desired, has no close substitute, and is not price-regulated, giving it pricing power; a commodity business competes mainly on price and lacks durable advantage.
  • Return on equity (Buffett’s version) — operating earnings over shareholders’ equity, with marketable securities held at cost (not market value) and capital gains/extraordinary items excluded, used instead of earnings-per-share growth to judge management performance.
  • Focus investing — concentrating a portfolio in a small number (Buffett suggests ten to twenty, with the top ideas receiving the largest weights) of high-conviction, well-understood companies rather than broad diversification.
  • Turnover discipline — holding positions for years, not months; Hagstrom cites a 10–20% annual turnover rate (roughly a 5–10 year average holding period) as consistent with Buffett’s practice.

Rules and setups

The book is a checklist-based framework rather than a mechanical trading system; its “rules” are the twelve tenets, organized as a due-diligence sequence a value investor works through before buying:

  1. Business tenets (3): Is the business simple and understandable? Does it have a consistent operating history (avoid companies undergoing major strategic change or turnarounds)? Does it have favorable long-term prospects (a “franchise,” not a commodity business)?
  2. Management tenets (3): Is management rational (sound, shareholder-value-focused capital allocation, including honest decisions about dividends vs. buybacks vs. reinvestment)? Is management candid with shareholders (transparent, complete disclosure, including of mistakes)? Does management resist the institutional imperative (avoid imitating peers/industry fads)?
  3. Financial tenets (4): Focus on return on equity, not earnings-per-share growth. Calculate owner earnings rather than relying on reported earnings or raw cash flow. Look for high, sustained profit margins. Confirm the company has created at least one dollar of market value for every dollar of retained earnings (the “one-dollar premise”).
  4. Value tenets (2): Determine the intrinsic value of the business by discounting expected future owner earnings back to the present (Buffett historically used the long-term risk-free government bond rate as the discount rate, adjusting it upward — e.g., to 10% — when yields ran unusually low). Buy only when the current price is available at a significant discount to that intrinsic value (a margin of safety), not merely because a stock looks statistically cheap (low P/E, low P/B).
  5. Portfolio construction (focus investing): limit holdings to companies you can truly understand (roughly ten to twenty); concentrate the largest weights in the highest-conviction ideas (Buffett has argued for putting at least 10% of net worth behind a strong conviction); hold for the long term (five-plus years as a rule of thumb); accept price volatility without reacting to it, since focus portfolios are judged on business results, not short-term price action.

Risk and money management

Hagstrom’s book does not use position-sizing formulas, stop-losses, or percentage-risk rules in the trading-system sense — Buffett’s risk control operates entirely through the selection filter (circle of competence, consistent operating history, predictable earnings) and through the margin of safety embedded in the purchase price. Concentration itself is treated as risk-reducing rather than risk-increasing, on the logic that a small number of thoroughly understood businesses is safer than a large number of poorly understood ones; Hagstrom cites the “fifteen stocks gives ~85% of diversification’s benefit” result to justify capping the portfolio around ten to twenty names. The book also cites a comparison of market-beating concentrated mutual funds averaging about 30% annual turnover against roughly 110% for the average equity fund, framing low turnover itself as a risk-management practice (fewer forced decisions, more time for the underlying business thesis to play out).

Psychology and discipline

A full chapter (“The Psychology of Money”) addresses the behavioral gap between calculating intrinsic value correctly and actually holding a position through volatility. Buffett and Charlie Munger are presented as unusually able to “handle the bumps” psychologically — Munger is quoted calculating that, if he could tolerate the volatility, owning as few as three stocks would suffice. Hagstrom frames patience and the ability to ignore short-term price swings as trained behaviors, not innate traits, and repeatedly contrasts Buffett’s approach against the efficient-market-theory assumption that price already reflects all available information — Buffett’s whole framework depends on price and value diverging often enough, and long enough, for a patient investor to exploit the gap.

Strengths and caveats

The tenets are illustrated with real, detailed case studies (Coca-Cola, Washington Post, Wells Fargo, GEICO) that show the numbers Buffett was actually looking at, which makes the framework concrete rather than abstract. The value-tenet math (discounted owner earnings) depends on subjective inputs — the discount rate and the multi-year earnings forecast — that Hagstrom acknowledges are inherently imprecise; the book gives the method but not a mechanical formula that removes judgment. Because it analyzes decisions after the fact, survivorship bias is a risk: the case studies are Buffett’s most famous successes, and the book does not systematically discuss his mistakes or the base rate of the approach applied by less skilled investors. Some of the return-on-equity and market-value figures are dated to the 1980s–1990s and given in period dollars.

Who should read it

Long-horizon, fundamentals-driven equity investors who want a structured, checklist-style framework for business and stock selection — particularly those interested in concentrated (rather than broadly diversified) portfolios. Less useful for short-term or technically-driven traders, since the book contains no entry/exit timing rules, stops, or price-action analysis.
