Overview

A five-week email/web course written by Kevin Haggerty for TradingMarkets.com members on professional (direct-access, flat-by-the-close) equity day trading. Week 1 covers what day trading is, margin risk, and a “Traders Equation” money-management framework; Week 2 covers institutional stock selection (how fund managers build positions, and why day traders should ride the same momentum); Week 3 covers swing-point charting (S1/S2/S3), bar-pattern classification (BP1–BP4), and consolidation patterns (dynamite triangles, flags, Ledges); Week 4 covers specific named intraday setups (Opening Reversal, Trap Door, Slim Jim) with worked NYSE stock examples; Week 5 recaps money management, position-scaling rules, and a market-dynamics monitoring table. All examples use late-1990s NYSE/NASDAQ stocks (NEON, AOL, TXN, BMY, AXP, AMAT, HWP, MRK) and specialist-driven market structure.

Core thesis

Day-trading profitability is a direct function of a single equation — win rate, average win size, average loss size, and trade frequency — and the trader’s only real lever of control is keeping losses small and consistent; win rate and average win depend on stock selection and market dynamics that can’t be forced. Haggerty argues day traders should not try to out-analyze institutions but should instead identify which stocks institutions (“the Generals”) are already accumulating and use structural pullback/consolidation patterns to enter alongside that established momentum, always flat by the close to avoid overnight news risk (his recurring cautionary example is NEON gapping from 44 to 18⅜ overnight).

Key concepts

  • Traders Equation — Maximum % of profitable trades + Maximum profit per trade − Small losses × Multiple trades = successful day trader; the framework used to explain why controlling loss size is the trader’s highest-leverage decision.
  • Swing points (S1/S2/S3) — a swing high/low with one higher-low (or lower-high) bar on each side is S1; two on each side is S2; three or more is S3. Higher swing-point numbers carry more significance, and swing-point trendlines are drawn point-to-point, most recent first.
  • Bar Patterns (BP1–BP4) — a classification of daily bar relationships to the prior bar (BP1: higher high than prior day, opens above prior high, closes up on the day above both prior high and today’s open, today’s low above prior high — the strongest continuation signal) used to grade the strength of a breakout day.
  • Wide Range Bar (WRB) — an outsized-range bar on expanded volume marking a “thrust” move, often the start of a new leg or the breakout from a consolidation.
  • Dynamite triangle — a small, narrowing consolidation (visually a symmetrical/descending triangle) that frequently follows a WRB and resolves with another thrust in the trend’s direction.
  • Ledge — a short, roughly ten-bar-or-fewer sideways range with matched highs/lows connected by a ruler; entry is only on a genuine breakout through the level, never on a gap past it.
  • Relative Strength (RS) / “the Generals” / “Accelerators” — RS identifies stocks institutions are already overweighting; “Generals” are the institutional buyers driving the primary move, “Accelerators” are hedge funds, program traders, and market makers who amplify it once underway.
  • Travel range — the sum of all intraday high-to-low swings (not just the day’s net high-low range), used to identify stocks whose actual tradable movement is much larger than their nominal daily range.
  • ADX / +DMI / −DMI trend filter — Average Directional Index combined with directional movement lines to confirm trend strength and direction before screening a stock for entries; Haggerty screens starting from low ADX values upward to avoid missing early-stage trends.

Rules and setups

  1. Opening Reversal (OR) — trade set-up within the first 30 minutes: (a) stock opens and sells off no more than 25% (max 33%) of its typical daily range; (b) price makes a quick reversal and trades back through the opening price; (c) enter 1/8 point above the opening price (sells reversed for the short case). Best candidates are high-RS stocks already showing strength as the broad market opens lower.
  2. Trap Door — a contra-trend momentum reversal traded only in high-RS S&P 500 stocks with an average daily range of at least 1½–2 points, trading above their 50- and 200-day EMAs. Entry: (1) stock opens below the previous close and trades down at least 1 point from that close; (2) the second bar makes a lower high and lower low; (3) enter long when the third bar trades above the second bar’s high (earliest entry) — under normal conditions the entry typically comes on the 4th or 5th bar instead. About 90% of Trap Doors show a 3–5 bar reversal on the 5-minute chart. Sells are reversed for the short case.
  3. Slim Jim — a tight, narrowing intraday consolidation traded as a breakout of the day’s high/low or as a reversal off the day’s extreme; the longer and tighter the consolidation, the more powerful the eventual breakout. Best formed in the first hour of trading.
  4. Dynamite Triangle entry — (1) enter below the low, or above the high, of the closest inside bar within the triangle (does not need to be a textbook inside bar); (2) if the extended triangle trend line offers an earlier entry and the stock/market dynamics are still favorable, take that instead.
  5. Swing-point trendline breakout — draw a trendline connecting swing points of the same class (commonly S3-to-S3, the strongest combination) and enter on a confirmed break of that line in the trend’s direction, especially when combined with a BP1/BP2 bar pattern and rising volume.
  6. Pullback-and-continuation entry — after a stock pulls back 3, 5, 7, 8, or 13 days (Haggerty’s observed common pullback lengths, not a fixed rule) to a rising 50- or 200-day EMA while remaining in an established uptrend, enter 1/8 point above the high of the prior bar once the stock closes back in the top of its range.
  7. Stock-selection filter: restrict candidates to S&P 500 / high-liquidity institutional names showing strong relative strength versus the index, trading above their 20-, 50-, and 200-day EMAs during the advancing phase.
  8. Position-scaling rule (partial profit-taking): on a position showing an initial ½-point profit, either (a) sell part of the position and hold the stop where it is, (b) sell part and move the stop to breakeven on the remainder (Haggerty’s stated preference — locks in a small gain while letting the rest run), or (c) hold the full position if stock/market dynamics remain strong.
  9. Trade-management rule: once entered, if the market does not “prove the trade correct” by turning profitable within a short period, exit immediately rather than waiting for the stop-loss to be hit; re-enter later if the setup resumes.
  10. Order-execution rule: enter a protective stop-loss or stop-limit order immediately upon confirmation of entry rather than relying on a mental stop, to remove emotional decision-making from the exit.
  11. Example dollar-risk sizing (Week 1 illustration, not a universal rule): one trader profile kept losses to ¼–⅜ point per trade while targeting 1–1½ points profit per trade — a roughly 3:1 to 4:1 reward-to-risk ratio cited as the shape a day trader should aim for, not a fixed instruction.

Risk and money management

The course’s central risk framework is the Traders Equation (win rate, average win, average loss, trade frequency) with explicit instruction that loss size is the only variable fully within the trader’s control, so it should be managed most tightly. Concrete numeric guidance: keep individual losses to a fraction of a point (illustrated at ¼–⅜ point against a 1–1½ point target in one worked example) and select stocks with at least a 1½–2 point average daily range so that reward-to-risk math is favorable relative to typical intraday noise. Haggerty repeatedly stresses flat-by-the-close discipline as the primary risk control against overnight gap risk (NEON’s overnight 44-to-18⅜ collapse is the recurring cautionary example), and treats margin (Regulation T, roughly 2:1 for retail; higher for registered proprietary traders) as a tool for position sizing rather than for holding overnight risk. No fixed percent-of-account risk-per-trade rule is given in the sampled pages; sizing is instead described through dollar-loss examples (e.g., a $300 maximum loss on a given trade, translating to 0.30 on 1,000 shares or 1 point on 300 shares) tailored to the trader’s own capital and stock selection.

Psychology and discipline

Haggerty frames hesitation at the stop-loss level (“the trader freezes and is now in a position of hope”) as the central psychological failure day traders must eliminate, and prescribes immediately placing a hard stop-loss or stop-limit order on entry confirmation specifically to remove that judgment call. He also warns against the common mistake of taking profits prematurely out of nervousness during a normal pullback, only to watch the stock continue toward its real potential — arguing that “managing your trade to higher profit levels after normal pullbacks is harder than it seems” once money and emotion enter the decision. The course repeatedly instructs traders to react to observed market/stock dynamics (order flow, volume behavior, reaction to programs) rather than to a preconceived opinion of what a stock “should” do, and frames disciplined adherence to a small, boring rule set (flat by the close, small losses, immediate stops) as the real edge over more exciting but undisciplined approaches.

Chapter map

  • Week 1 — Day Trading Defined: professional vs. online day trading, margin risk (NEON example), the Traders Equation, small-loss discipline.
  • Week 2 — Stock Selection: how institutional fund managers build positions (“the Generals”), why day traders should trade alongside institutional momentum, worked AOL/TXN examples.
  • Week 3 — Market and Stock Dynamics: swing points (S1/S2/S3), Bar Patterns (BP1–BP4), Wide Range Bars, dynamite triangles/flags, ADX/+DMI/−DMI trend filtering, a market-dynamics monitoring table (TICK, PREM, advance-decline, up/down volume).
  • Week 4 — Intraday Patterns: Opening Reversal, Trap Door, Slim Jim, worked HWP/MRK/BGEN examples, swing-point trendline breakouts (worked BMY, GTW, AMAT examples).
  • Week 5 — Putting It Together: money-management recap, position-scaling rules, technology/communications requirements for direct-access trading, final consolidated rule list.

Strengths and caveats

These notes are drawn from a scanned book with OCR run only on a sample of pages; while a substantial share of Weeks 1, 3, and 4 was recovered with clear rule text, some worked-example pages and later Week 5 material were in the unsampled gaps, so some detail (particularly any additional named patterns beyond those captured here) may be missing. The named setups requested for this note — Trap Door and Opening Reversal — were both recoverable in full rule form; “1-2-3 higher bottoms” and “RST reversal” style setups by name were not found in the sampled pages (they may exist in the unsampled ~85% of the course) and are not included here to avoid inventing content. The course is explicitly written for late-1990s NYSE specialist-driven, phone/direct-access execution with Regulation T margin norms; the specific mechanics of specialist order handling described in the Trap Door setup are largely obsolete in modern all-electronic markets, though the underlying price-structure logic (swing points, pullback entries, stop discipline) remains applicable. No formal backtest, win rate, or aggregated track record is given for any of the named setups — all evidence is single worked chart examples.

Who should read it

Active or aspiring short-term equity traders who want a structured, rule-based framework for reading intraday price structure (swing points, bar patterns) and a small set of concrete, high-frequency setups, rather than a discussion of technical-analysis theory in the abstract. Best suited to traders already comfortable with basic charting who want Haggerty’s specific entry/exit mechanics and money-management discipline.
