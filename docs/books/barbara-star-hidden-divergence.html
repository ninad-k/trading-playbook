Hidden Divergence

Barbara Star · 1996 · Tier B — concise note article intermediate

Hidden divergence compares oscillator and price swings to identify trend-continuation entries and possible reversals.

Summary

Barbara Star distinguishes classic divergence, which warns of a possible trend change, from hidden divergence (HD), which usually signals continuation in the prevailing trend. Her examples use a 5–15 price oscillator: the difference between a five-period EMA and a 15-period EMA of closing price (p. 2). Bullish HD occurs during an uptrend when the indicator makes a lower low while price makes a higher low or double bottom; bearish HD occurs during a downtrend when the indicator makes a higher high while price makes a lower high (pp. 2–4). The pattern is intended for corrective pullbacks or retests, creating entry or re-entry points that keep the trader aligned with the trend. The article’s Micron, corn, cattle, Kansas City Southern, and General Nutrition examples show HDs followed by advances or declines, but also include false HDs and moves that were modest (pp. 2–5). Star introduces a second-point lookback when the immediately preceding indicator point appears to confirm price, but an older indicator swing still produces the hidden relationship (pp. 3–4). She notes that Williams’ %R and a 12-period momentum indicator can display similar patterns and that indicator choice changes signal locations (p. 5). A trendline break can confirm a broader reversal after classic or hidden divergence (p. 4).

Key points

  • HD is a continuation hypothesis whose meaning depends on prevailing trend and swing sequence (pp. 2–4).
  • Not every hidden divergence produces a large move; false patterns occur (pp. 4–5).
  • The examples use oscillator structure plus price levels and trendlines rather than oscillator shape alone.

Actionable rules

  1. In an uptrend, identify bullish hidden divergence when the oscillator makes a lower low while price makes a higher low or double bottom (pp. 2–3).
  2. In a downtrend, identify bearish hidden divergence when the oscillator makes a higher high while price makes a lower high (p. 4).
  3. Usually compare the latest two indicator swings; use the second-point lookback only when the immediately preceding swing confirms price but the older swing preserves the hidden relationship (pp. 3–4). The article supplies no stop or sizing rule.

Caveats

The article presents selected chart examples with no statistical sample, costs, stops, or sizing model. Swing identification and “significant” trend context are subjective, and the examples span stocks, commodities, and bonds.

Who it is for

Traders researching divergence as a trend-continuation filter.
