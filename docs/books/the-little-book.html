Overview

Written by hedge fund manager Joel Greenblatt (Gotham Capital) for a lay audience — the author repeatedly says a “gifted eleven-year-old” should be able to follow it — this short book builds up, from first principles and simple retail-business analogies, a two-factor stock-ranking system he calls the “Magic Formula”: rank a large universe of stocks by return on capital, rank them again by earnings yield, add the two ranks together, and buy the stocks with the best combined rank. The middle chapters present a 17-year backtest (1988–2004) of the formula across three market-cap universes, address the obvious objections (survivorship, small-cap illiquidity, luck, “why won’t everyone just use this”), and close with literal step-by-step instructions — including a companion website — for building and rolling a real portfolio.

Core thesis

You do not need to forecast the future to invest well; you only need two verifiable, backward-looking facts about a business — how much it currently earns relative to the price you’d pay for it (earnings yield) and how efficiently it turns capital into earnings (return on capital) — and a disciplined, mechanical rule for buying the best combination of both across a large, diversified basket of stocks, held long enough (Greenblatt argues a minimum three to five years) for Mr. Market to recognize the bargain. The formula works not because it is clever but because it is obvious and simple: it systematically buys good businesses only when Mr. Market is temporarily giving them away cheap, and it keeps working precisely because it underperforms for long stretches (sometimes years), which causes most investors and professional managers to abandon it before it pays off.

Key concepts

  • Mr. Market — Benjamin Graham’s personification of the market’s manic-depressive daily pricing, who is there to serve you (by occasionally offering silly prices), not to guide you.
  • Earnings yield (EBIT/Enterprise Value) — the book’s price/cheapness measure; EBIT (pre-tax operating earnings) divided by enterprise value (equity market value plus net interest-bearing debt), chosen over P/E or E/P specifically because it neutralizes differences in tax rate and capital structure across companies.
  • Return on capital (EBIT/[Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets]) — the book’s quality-of-business measure; pre-tax operating earnings divided by the tangible capital actually required to run the business, chosen over ROE or ROA because it excludes goodwill and normalizes for debt and tax-rate differences.
  • Magic Formula ranking — rank all eligible stocks 1-to-N by return on capital, rank them again 1-to-N by earnings yield, sum the two ranks per stock, and buy the lowest (best) combined-rank names.
  • Combined-rank logic — a stock ranked 232nd in return on capital and 153rd in earnings yield (combined rank 385) can beat a stock ranked 1st in return on capital but only 1,150th in earnings yield (combined rank 1,151); the formula rewards balance across both factors over dominance in either alone.
  • “Good business at a bargain price” — Greenblatt’s plain-English restatement of the formula’s entire logic; a high-return-on-capital business can profitably reinvest its own earnings at a high rate (compounding), and a high earnings yield means you are not overpaying for that quality.
  • Why the edge persists — because the formula underperforms the market in roughly 5 of every 12 months and in about 1 of every 4 full years (and sometimes 2–3 years running), most investors and even professional managers abandon it during these stretches, which is exactly why it keeps working for those who stick with it.
  • Rolling one-year holding period — each individual stock pick in the backtest was held exactly one year before being re-ranked and replaced.
  • Enterprise value — market value of equity (including preferred) plus net interest-bearing debt; used instead of just share-price market cap so that debt-financed and debt-free companies can be compared on an equal footing.

Rules and setups

The Magic Formula’s exact backtested rules (see the companion system page for the full step-by-step version):

  1. Universe: the largest ~3,500 U.S.-exchange-listed companies (minimum market value in the study ≈ $50 million); utilities, financial stocks (banks, insurers, mutual funds), and foreign ADRs are excluded.
  2. Rank every company 1-to-N by return on capital (EBIT / [Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets]), best = rank 1.
  3. Rank every company again 1-to-N by earnings yield (EBIT / Enterprise Value), best = rank 1.
  4. Sum the two ranks per company; the lowest combined-rank companies are the Magic Formula’s top picks.
  5. Hold a diversified portfolio of 20–30 stocks at a time, rebalancing 5–7 positions every two to three months so the full 20–30-stock portfolio is built up over roughly the first 9–10 months of investing.
  6. Hold each individual stock exactly one year, then sell and replace it with a new top-ranked name (taxable accounts: sell winners a few days after the one-year mark to get long-term capital-gains treatment, sell losers a few days before the one-year mark to get short-term-loss tax treatment).
  7. Commit to running the formula unmodified for a minimum of three to five years regardless of interim results — the book’s own backtest shows the formula underperformed the market in roughly 1 of every 4 one-year periods but beat it in 95% of rolling three-year periods (160 of 169 tested) and never lost money over any rolling three-year period (169 of 169) in the 1988–2004 sample.

17-year backtest results (1988–2004) by universe:

Universe (largest N companies) Magic Formula avg. annual return Market-average (equal-weight) return
3,500 (min. ~$50M market cap) 30.8% 12.3%
2,500 (min. ~$200M market cap) 23.7% 12.4%
1,000 (min. ~$1B market cap) 22.9% 11.7%

When the 2,500-stock universe was split into 10 equal rank-ordered decile groups of ~250 stocks each and each group’s annualized return calculated (1988–2004), returns declined monotonically from the top-ranked group to the bottom: Group 1 (best-ranked) returned 17.9%/year versus Group 10 (worst-ranked) at 2.5%/year, confirming the ranking orders returns rather than picking a few lucky winners.

Risk and money management

The book’s only explicit risk-management rule is diversification by count, not by position size: hold 20–30 stocks at once (Greenblatt notes an investor confident enough to do independent fundamental analysis could reasonably concentrate into 5–8 names instead, but recommends 20–30 for anyone using the formula mechanically without further individual analysis). There is no stop-loss, no percent-of-equity risk rule, and no volatility-based sizing — risk control comes entirely from the combination of (a) a large enough basket that a few bad picks don’t dominate results and (b) buying only statistically cheap, high-quality businesses on average. Greenblatt is explicit that the formula’s paper track record included real, multi-year periods of underperformance (up to three consecutive down years relative to the market in the sample), so the practical “risk” the book focuses on is behavioral — the risk of abandoning the strategy during a bad stretch — rather than a market/price risk to be hedged.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s central discipline argument, stated repeatedly, is that a mechanical formula only works for an investor who understands why it works well enough to hold it through years of underperformance; a good historical track record is explicitly framed as insufficient reason to follow a strategy, because belief without understanding will not survive real losing streaks. Greenblatt illustrates this with two real anecdotes: a well-known value-investing author who sold his own fund-management company after his formula underperformed for two of its first three years (only for the same fund to later become a top performer), and an unnamed “smartest money manager” friend who nearly lost his entire client base during a multi-year cold streak before his strategy’s long-run edge reasserted itself. The explicit lesson is that the formula’s edge is self-sustaining precisely because most market participants cannot tolerate the multi-year underperformance stretches required to earn it.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1–2 — Framing: why beating the market is hard, and why belief in a formula’s underlying logic (not just its track record) is what allows an investor to stick with it.
  • Ch 3–4 — Introduces Mr. Market and the difficulty of predicting the future; sets up the “Jason’s Gum Shops” retail-store analogy used throughout.
  • Ch 5 — Derives the two core concepts (earnings yield and return on capital) using the Jason’s Gum Shops / “Just Broccoli” comparison.
  • Ch 6 — Introduces the Magic Formula ranking mechanism and its first 17-year backtest results (Table 6.1).
  • Ch 7 — Stress-tests the formula: confirms it isn’t a small-cap-illiquidity artifact (works on the largest 1,000 stocks too) and isn’t luck (over 4,500 separate one-year stock selections tested; returns decline monotonically by decile rank).
  • Ch 8 — Addresses “why won’t this stop working once everyone knows it”: shows the formula underperforms the market in ~5 of 12 months and ~1 of 4 years, which keeps most investors from sticking with it.
  • Ch 9 — Explains fundamentally why high-return-on-capital businesses are special (they can profitably reinvest their own earnings, compounding growth).
  • Ch 10 — Transition chapter on individual stock-picking as an alternative to mechanical formula investing.
  • Ch 11 — Backtests alternative value ratios (price-to-book, price-to-sales, price-to-forward-earnings, ROE, sales-per-employee) against the magic formula’s own components.
  • Ch 12 — Surveys and dismisses standard retail alternatives (stockbrokers, actively managed mutual funds, hedge funds, star ratings) before recommending index funds as the fallback for investors unwilling to run the formula.
  • Ch 13 — Closing reflections, including a “Just Broccoli” analogy applied to school-choice/education policy.
  • Step-by-Step Instructions — the literal mechanical process (screening site, position sizing, rebalancing cadence, tax-lot timing).
  • Appendix — full technical definitions of EBIT/enterprise value and return-on-capital, and comparison to Benjamin Graham’s original “net-net” bargain-stock formula.

Strengths and caveats

The earnings-yield and return-on-capital definitions are unusually precise and fully disclosed (exact formulas, universe construction, exclusions), and the book is candid about the formula’s real interim drawdowns rather than presenting only the smoothed 17-year average — a rare honesty in popular investing books. The backtest period (1988–2004) coincides with a long secular bull market and included the late-1990s and 2000s value/growth rotations that may have specifically favored a value-plus-quality factor combination; results before 1988 are not shown (the underlying Compustat “Point in Time” database only went back 17 years at time of writing), so the strategy’s performance through earlier regimes (e.g., the 1970s) is untested in the book itself. The named implementation tool, magicformulainvesting.com, and several other named screening sites reflect mid-2000s web resources whose current availability and exact screening methodology cannot be verified from the book alone. The book does not address transaction costs, bid/ask spreads on less-liquid names, or the tax drag of annual full-portfolio turnover in a taxable account beyond the basic gain/loss timing rule given.

Who should read it

Beginning-to-intermediate investors who want a mechanical, rules-based, long-term equity strategy they can run themselves without needing to forecast earnings or read financial statements in depth, and who are prepared to commit to it through multi-year stretches of underperformance. Less useful for short-term traders, for investors seeking a live, currently-maintained implementation (the specific web tools named are now dated), or for readers wanting rigorous out-of-sample validation beyond the single 1988–2004 study presented.
