Overview

Phantom of the Pits is a dialogue-format book: an anonymous, self-described veteran floor and futures trader (“Phantom,” or POP) answers questions posed by Art Simpson (ALS), originally serialized for an online trading forum. The book’s entire content radiates outward from two core rules Phantom says he built his career on, plus a third rule added later at readers’ urging. Rather than presenting a trading system, it presents a position-management and behavior-modification layer meant to sit on top of whatever entry method a trader already uses.

Core thesis

Trading is fundamentally a losing game — most individual positions will not work out — so a trader’s job is not to be right more often but to control the asymmetry between being wrong (small, fast, mandatory losses) and being right (deliberately larger, pressed positions). Phantom argues traders instinctively do the opposite of both: they hold losing positions hoping to be proven right, and they take profits early on winners to “prove” they were correct, which is backwards from what a long-run positive-expectation approach requires. Behavior modification — actually changing habitual reactions, not just intellectually agreeing with the rules — is presented as harder than and equally necessary to knowing the rules.

Key concepts

  • Assume wrong until proven correct — Phantom’s reframing of position-holding: never keep a position because the market hasn’t yet proven it wrong; only keep a position because it has proven itself correct.
  • The surprise side — low-probability but possible adverse outcomes that a trade plan must account for even though they’re not the expected case, analogous to looking both ways at a crosswalk every time regardless of past experience.
  • Reverse image thinking — Phantom’s description of his own rules as the opposite of instinctive trader behavior (cutting winners short, holding losers long); framed as needing to consciously invert default human reactions.
  • Behavior modification — the practiced, repeated change of trading habits until correct responses become automatic (“second nature”), distinguished from merely knowing correct trading knowledge.
  • Washing a position — exiting a trade that hasn’t been disproven but also hasn’t confirmed itself, purely to preserve capital and stay in the long-run game.
  • Pressing correctly — Phantom’s qualification on Rule 2: adding size to a winning position only according to a predefined plan (not impulsively), scaled so later adds are smaller than the initial position.
  • Liquidity as a signal — Phantom’s Rule 3 concept that extreme volume (very high or very low) is itself information distinct from price, since it often precedes reversals or “special price level” events.
  • Trading in the long run — Phantom’s framing that any individual trade’s outcome is close to meaningless; only a large sample of trades run under consistent rules produces a reliable result.

Rules and setups

Rule 1 (cut losses without exception). In a losing game, assume every new position is wrong until the market proves it correct; remove a position that has not been proven correct rather than waiting for the market to prove it wrong. Practically: a position must show confirming price action (per the trader’s own plan — e.g., following through in the expected direction within an expected window) or it is closed, regardless of whether a stop-loss price has technically been hit. Phantom frames this as producing smaller, faster, more numerous small losses instead of fewer but larger ones, and as removing the psychological burden of “being wrong” since exits are systematic rather than admissions of a bad call.

Rule 2 (press proven winners without exception). Once a position is proven correct, add to it according to a predefined plan — never as an arbitrary, in-the-moment decision. Phantom’s own illustrative structure: scale adds down in size relative to the initial position (e.g., an initial 6 contracts, then a 4-contract add, then a 2-contract add — a 3:2:1 ratio), so the position grows without a top-heavy pyramid a minor reversal could unwind. Add criteria are plan-specific (a new buy/sell signal, a retracement, a breakout or breakaway gap); day traders are advised to place full intended size at entry and rely on Rule 1 alone, since short holding periods make scaling in impractical. Rule 1 stays active on the whole position at all times, including added units.

Rule 3 (question signals, and exit, at liquidity extremes) — added later at readers’ request. In illiquid/thin markets, treat price signals as unreliable and wait for confirmation before acting on them. At the opposite extreme, after a day of unusually high volume, exit half the existing position the next day and the remaining half within two additional days, then wait for a fresh signal before re-entering — using extreme volume as an early-warning flag for a likely reversal or exhaustion move rather than waiting for price itself to confirm the turn.

Phantom gives no market, timeframe, or indicator specifics beyond these three rules — he explicitly leaves entry-signal generation, exact stop levels, and add triggers to the reader’s own trade plan, positioning the rules as a layer that applies on top of any system.

Risk and money management

Risk control here is entirely behavioral and structural rather than numeric: Rule 1 is the loss-cutting mechanism (no specific percentage or dollar figure is prescribed; “small and fast” losses are the goal, sized by the trader’s own plan), and Rule 2’s declining-size pyramid (e.g., 3:2:1) is presented as risk control on the winning side — it grows exposure without over-concentrating a single reversal’s damage on the largest tranche. Rule 3 functions as portfolio-level risk control at market extremes: reducing or closing size specifically around unusually thin or unusually high-volume conditions, independent of what price itself is doing. The book gives no guidance on the percentage of equity to risk per trade or per position, leaving quantitative sizing entirely to the reader.

Psychology and discipline

Psychology is effectively the entire book. Phantom repeatedly distinguishes between “correct knowledge” and “behavior modification,” arguing traders fail not from not knowing what to do but from being unable to consistently do it under emotional pressure. Central examples include: the asymmetry between how it feels to hold a loser (hope, denial) versus a winner (fear of giving back profit, urge to take profit early to “prove” being right); the argument that avoiding decisions (letting a stop get hit) feels safer than making them (choosing to exit) even though the latter produces better long-run outcomes; and an extended discussion (with reader-submitted forum testimonials) of how subtly traders can believe they’re following Rule 1 while actually still waiting for disconfirmation rather than requiring confirmation. Phantom frames intuition, developed through repetition until correct responses are “second nature,” as ultimately more reliable in the moment than re-deriving logical justifications on every trade.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s strength is a clear, memorable, internally consistent behavioral framework that generalizes across markets and timeframes, reinforced by dialogue that anticipates common misunderstandings (the forum testimonials on subtly misapplying Rule 1 are a particularly useful worked example). Caveats: the anonymous-author, forum-transcript format means claims about the author’s track record cannot be independently verified; the rules are deliberately generic and give no market selection, entry-signal, or quantitative sizing guidance, so the book cannot function as a standalone system; and repetitive, discursive prose means the core content — three rules and their rationale — is stretched across the full book rather than concisely stated.

Who should read it

Discretionary and systematic traders alike who already have an entry method but struggle with exit discipline or with sizing into winning positions; less useful for a reader looking for a complete, numerically specified trading system.
