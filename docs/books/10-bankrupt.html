10% Of Traders Go Bankrupt

Mark McRae · Tier B — concise note article beginner

Short introductory lesson from the 'Traders Secret Library' arguing that lack of education, unrealistic expectations, and poor risk management — not low IQ — cause most traders to fail.

Summary

A short opening lesson from Mark McRae’s “Traders Secret Library” course, built around the claim that roughly 90% of traders lose their account and 10% go bankrupt. The article argues that failure stems not from low intelligence but from inadequate trading education — specifically in psychology, risk management, and entry/exit method — and lists common misconceptions that set new traders up to fail before introducing basic risk-management and trader-personality-matching ideas.

Key points

  • Cited statistic (uncredited, no source given): about 90% of traders who ever trade lose their account, and 10% go bankrupt.
  • Lists common new-trader misconceptions: expecting 80% win-rate consistency, expecting to turn $1,000 into $100,000 in six months, expecting to predict turning points to the minute, expecting a system that is 100% accurate, and expecting to quit a job after a few months of trading.
  • Blames marketing/propaganda (magazines, emails, vendor claims) for feeding these unrealistic expectations.
  • States trading is probabilistic, not deterministic — each trade is an independent event and the market has no memory of prior wins or losses.
  • Argues psychological approach to the market matters as much as the trading plan itself, and warns against tying self-worth to trading results.
  • Notes the “flaw” in human nature is the urge to influence outcomes we cannot control (the market), which produces emotional swings — euphoria on winning streaks, depression on losing streaks — that a trader must learn to neutralize into “impartiality.”
  • Cites (uncredited) a study of 925 CTA (Commodity Trading Advisor) programs from 1974–1995 finding that the most consistently profitable managers were those with the best risk-management systems.
  • Frames traders as generally falling into two main categories: trend traders (enter an established trend early, using tools like Fibonacci retracements, moving averages, or Gann) and breakout traders (enter on a valid break of a previously identified range, such as support/resistance or chart patterns like rectangles/triangles).

Actionable rules

None given as numbered, tradable rules — this is a psychology/framing lesson, not a system. The closest actionable takeaways: (1) size your account so no single trade feels like a “live or die” event; (2) match your trading method (trend-following vs. breakout) to your own personality rather than adopting one because it is popular; (3) treat entries as probabilistic bets governed by a fixed rule set, not predictions to be right about.

Caveats

Very short (4 pages) promotional lesson for a paid course/newsletter (“Traders Secret Library,” surefire-trading.com); the headline 90%/10% statistic and the “925 CTA programs 1974-1995” study are both referenced without citation and cannot be independently verified from the text. No specific numbers for risk-per-trade, stop placement, or position sizing are given — those are deferred to “future lessons” not included in this document.

Who it is for

Complete beginners who need a short, blunt reset on realistic expectations and the centrality of psychology and risk management before diving into technical methods.
