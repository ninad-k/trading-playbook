Summary

Lecture notes (from the “Global Financial Management” course by Brav, Harvey, Gray and Maug, 1999) on capital budgeting: how a firm should evaluate whether to undertake an investment project by analyzing its incremental cash flows. The notes build the net present value (NPV) rule from first principles, compare it against internal rate of return (IRR), payback period, and the profitability index, and work through the mechanics of computing incremental after-tax cash flows including depreciation tax shields.

Key points

  • Incremental cash flows — only cash flows that change as a direct result of the decision belong in the analysis; sunk costs and costs the firm would incur anyway are excluded.
  • NPV rule — accept a project if NPV > 0 (discount all future incremental cash flows at the project’s required return and subtract the initial outlay); reject if NPV < 0.
  • Why NPV is correct — a positive-NPV project lets shareholders borrow against future cash flows and consume the NPV amount today; a negative-NPV project forces a cut in consumption to finance it.
  • Different project lives — compare mutually exclusive projects with different useful lives using the annual equivalent (AE) cash flow, not raw NPV, when the asset will be replaced indefinitely.
  • IRR’s four problems — (1) ignores differing time horizons/reinvestment assumptions, (2) can produce multiple IRRs when cash flow signs change more than once, (3) ignores project scale, (4) sign convention flips between investing and financing cash-flow patterns.
  • Payback period flaws — ignores time value of money, ignores cash flows after the cutoff, and ignores investment scale; not a legitimate substitute for risk-adjusted discounting.
  • Profitability index (PI = NPV / initial investment) — useful for ranking projects under capital rationing only when projects are fully divisible; can select a suboptimal project set when investments are indivisible (“lumpy”).
  • Depreciation tax shield — cash flow = (1 − tax rate) × (revenue − expenses) + tax rate × depreciation − capital expenditure; depreciation itself is not a cash outflow, only its tax effect matters.

Actionable rules

  1. Accept a project if NPV > 0 using the project-appropriate discount rate; reject if NPV < 0; for mutually exclusive alternatives, take the highest positive NPV.
  2. For mutually exclusive projects with unequal lives (recurring/replaceable assets), compare annual equivalent cash flow AE = NPV × i / (1 − (1+i)^−n) and choose the higher AE.
  3. IRR decision rule: accept if IRR > required return for investment-type cash flows (outflow then inflows); accept if IRR < required return for financing-type cash flows (inflow then outflows) — but avoid IRR entirely when cash flows change sign more than once.
  4. Payback period rule (if used at all): accept only if payback < a pre-set maximum PP*, typically 2-4 years in practice; treat as a supplementary liquidity check, not a primary decision rule.
  5. Under capital rationing with divisible projects, rank by profitability index (PI = NPV / investment cost) and fund from the top until the budget is exhausted; with indivisible projects, PI ranking can be wrong and combinations must be checked directly.
  6. Worked example: a $62,000 machine (7-year life, $6,000 salvage, straight-line depreciation, 34% tax rate, 12% discount rate) generating $55,000/year net revenue produces a present value of $118,793 over its life.

Caveats

This is general corporate-finance/capital-budgeting theory (evaluating internal investment projects, equipment purchases, etc.), not a trading strategy or market-timing text — its numbers apply to project appraisal, not position sizing or trade risk. Tax and depreciation rules cited (U.S. Tax Reform Act of 1986 five/seven-year recovery classes) are dated and jurisdiction-specific; the core NPV/IRR/PI mechanics remain standard.

Who it is for

Traders or investors who want a rigorous grounding in discounted-cash-flow valuation and capital-budgeting decision rules, useful background for evaluating a company’s investment decisions in fundamental/value analysis.

Educational summaries only. Nothing here is financial advice. Test every rule before risking capital.
