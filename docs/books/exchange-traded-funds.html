Summary

A Knowledge@Wharton article (reprinted from 2005) explaining the mechanics behind the ETF boom of the early-to-mid 2000s. It covers how ETFs differ from mutual funds, how the creation/redemption process keeps ETF share prices tied to net asset value, and interviews Wharton finance professors and industry figures (Jeremy Siegel, Marshall Blume) on where the ETF market was headed.

Key points

  • ETFs are baskets of stocks tracking an index, but trade intraday like stocks rather than pricing once daily like mutual funds.
  • By August of the reported year there were 177 ETFs with $255 billion in net assets, up from 97 funds and $89 billion in 2002 — industry expectation was roughly 30% annual asset growth.
  • ETF annual expense ratios (0.4-0.5%) were far below stock mutual funds (~1.4%), though investors pay brokerage commissions on ETF trades that no-load mutual fund buyers avoid.
  • “Creation units” let market makers and specialists create or redeem large blocks of ETF shares against the underlying stocks, which is the arbitrage mechanism keeping ETF price close to NAV.
  • Because ETF trades don’t force the fund to sell holdings, ETFs generally avoid the year-end capital-gains distributions that trigger tax bills for mutual fund holders.
  • ETFs can be shorted and used with options, making them attractive to hedge funds and institutions for tactical sector bets and hedging.
  • The article flags a caution from Morningstar: a growing share of new ETFs were narrow, “gimmicky,” and risk being misused by investors trying to time the market.

Actionable rules

None given — this is a descriptive market-structure article, not a trading system.

Caveats

Dated (written in 2005): AUM figures, fund counts, and expense-ratio comparisons are all stale, and several named products/firms (PowerShares’ Intellidex line, specific ETF launches) reflect that era’s product landscape rather than the current one. It is journalism, not an academic or regulatory source, and offers no risk disclosures beyond the closing caution about narrow/gimmicky funds.

Who it is for

Readers wanting a plain-language explanation of why ETFs work mechanically and why they took share from mutual funds; not for traders looking for ETF trading strategies.
