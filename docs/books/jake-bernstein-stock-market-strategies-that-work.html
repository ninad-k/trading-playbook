Overview

Written by veteran commodity/stock trader Jake (Jacob) Bernstein with his son Elliott, this book targets the individual investor who wants a practical path from picking a broker and a stock to timing entries and exits with technical indicators. Using a recurring “market monkey” analogy (small individual investors as nimble chimps in a jungle dominated by “gorilla” institutions), the book moves from stock-picking hygiene (avoiding tips, TV gurus, low-liquidity “cheapies”) through an evaluative tour of standard indicators, into the authors’ own proprietary trend/support-resistance tool, the Moving Average Channel (MAC), and closes with discipline and a skeptical look at commercially sold trading systems. Its distinguishing feature versus a typical indicator primer is that each indicator is explicitly scored for assets, liabilities, and fixes, and the MAC chapter gives a complete, numerically specified method.

Core thesis

Most retail investors lose not because winning methods don’t exist, but because they use indicators/systems barely better than a coin flip, or possess a workable method but fail to apply it with discipline. The authors argue that timing matters more than price alone, that trading should combine an objective, rule-based method for defining trend and support/resistance with a self-imposed goal-setting process (profit targets, maximum loss, holding period), and that discipline — keeping a system current, implementing its signals, and enforcing its risk management — is a bigger determinant of outcomes than which specific system is used.

Key concepts

  • Timing indicator vs. trading system vs. trading method — an indicator is a raw signal; a system has fully specified, objective entry/exit/risk rules; a method combines signals with looser, subjective rules (the authors recommend systems over methods for beginners).
  • Leading, time-current, and lagging indicators — leading indicators (Stochastic, RSI) signal before a turn but risk early entries and whipsaws; lagging/trend-following indicators (moving averages, MACD) confirm a trend already underway but cost the first and last portion of the move.
  • Moving Average Channel (MAC) — the authors’ own tool: a 10-day MA of daily highs (resistance) with an 8-day MA of daily lows (support); a trend is confirmed once two or more consecutive bars close fully outside the channel, and five or more warns of a strong continuation move (full method in the system sub-page).
  • Channel surfing — a range-bound MAC variant: buying at the MA-of-lows and selling at the MA-of-highs (or the reverse for shorts) repeatedly while price oscillates within the channel.
  • MOM/MA (28-period momentum/moving-average) — a momentum oscillator crossed against its own moving average; buy above, sell below; a slower 42-period version with a 10-bar confirmation filter is used to gate dollar-cost-averaging (DCA) into mutual funds, producing a lower average cost per share than naive fixed-schedule DCA in the authors’ example.
  • Trading discipline (three levels) — preparation/planning, implementation (pulling the trigger), and risk management (honoring stops and profit rules); failing to close losers promptly and failing to hold winners long enough are the costliest discipline failures.
  • Institutional ownership / short interest / outstanding shares — screening factors for low-priced (“cheapie”) stocks: a low share count relative to peers amplifies price moves; institutional ownership signals liquidity but also downside risk if institutions exit; high short interest can fuel short-covering rallies.

Rules and setups

  1. MAC trend/support/resistance system (full detail in the [[jake-bernstein-stock-market-strategies-that-work–moving-average-channel-mac|MAC system sub-page]]): plot a 10-day MA of highs and an 8-day MA of lows; buy at the MAC low in a confirmed uptrend, sell short at the MAC high in a confirmed downtrend.
  2. MOM/MA crossover entries: a 28-period momentum crossed against its own 28-period moving average; buy above, sell below. For mutual-fund DCA, slow this to 42 periods and require the signal to hold 10 consecutive trading days before acting.
  3. Moving-average trend filters (general): price closing above its MA signals an uptrend, below signals a downtrend; with multiple MAs, a crossover of the shorter above/below the longer generates the signal instead.
  4. Parabolic stop-and-reverse: each day yields a “buy number” and “sell number”; a close through either flips the position — best paired with a non-price filter (volume/open interest) to avoid sideways-market whipsaws.
  5. DMI/ADX trend filter: go long when +DI crosses above −DI, short when it crosses below; use rising ADX to judge whether trend-following signals are worth taking at all.
  6. Cheap-stock (“under $10”) screen: favor adequate daily volume, a low share count relative to peers, meaningful institutional ownership, and a distinct business story; avoid issues trading only a few thousand shares a day regardless of the story.
  7. No universal stop-loss percentage outside the MAC chapter — pre-define a dollar risk and profit target and answer a seven-question checklist (share count, breakeven move, profit point, holding-period tolerance, target realism, maximum loss) before every trade.

Risk and money management

The book’s clearest numeric risk rule is embedded in the MAC system: a 10 percent closing-basis stop-loss from entry for conservative traders, or exiting on the opposite two-bar signal for aggressive traders (full spec in the system sub-page), plus a trailing stop below the lowest low (longs) or above the highest high (shorts) of the last three bars once a position shows a reasonable profit. Outside the MAC chapter, risk management is treated as a discipline problem: failure to honor a stop-loss and failure to let winners run are the two most damaging patterns, with workarounds including a good-until-canceled stop entered immediately, a trading partner to enforce the rules, and a trade diary logging entry/exit price, size, and reason to make discipline failures visible. Liquidity (average daily volume) is treated as a hard screen for stock selection, since an illiquid “cheapie” position can be difficult to exit regardless of the technical signal calling for it.

Psychology and discipline

An entire chapter is devoted to discipline, defined as “putting into action the actions generated by a trading system… and implementing the risk management rules that accompany [it].” The authors decompose discipline into three interdependent levels — preparation/planning, implementation, and risk management — and argue a mediocre system traded with full discipline will outperform an excellent system traded inconsistently. They identify two fears that undermine discipline: fear of losses (an “approach-avoidance” paralysis, likened to a lab-animal neurosis experiment) and fear of profit (self-sabotage from investors uncomfortable being winners, which they suggest may need professional treatment in severe cases). Countermeasures include a detailed trading diary, delegating trade execution to a detached partner or broker when self-execution repeatedly fails, taking small position sizes initially to build confidence, and ranking discipline above the trading system itself, since a system without discipline is “a ship without a rudder.”

Chapter map

  • Ch 1-2 — Beginnings; Playing the Game: risk-capital framing, why forecasters fail, and share/margin basics.
  • Ch 3-4 — Dos and Don’ts of Picking Winning Stocks; Picking a Stockbroker: avoiding tips/gurus, commission trade-offs.
  • Ch 5 — Trading the Cheapies: volume, shares outstanding, institutional ownership, short interest, penny-stock hazards for sub-$10 stocks.
  • Ch 6 — The Technical Approach: indicator taxonomy (leading/time-current/lagging) and an assets/liabilities/solutions review of moving averages, MACD, Stochastic/RSI, chart patterns, Parabolic, and DMI/ADX.
  • Ch 7 — Dollar Cost Averaging: mechanics of naive DCA.
  • Ch 8 — Support and Resistance: The MAC: the authors’ Moving Average Channel system in full (this book’s system page).
  • Ch 9-10 — Two Real-Time Examples; Implementing Market Strategies: worked live trades, MOM/MA example, and technical-gated vs. naive DCA comparison.
  • Ch 11 — The Importance of Discipline: the three levels of discipline, fear of losses/profit, and countermeasures.
  • Ch 12 — Computerized Trading Systems: critique of commercially sold systems and how to evaluate one before trusting it.

Strengths and caveats

The indicator-evaluation format (assets/liabilities/solutions for each indicator) is more candid about failure modes than most introductory technical-analysis books, stating plainly that overbought/oversold readings are “often misleading” and most timing indicators are “little better than guessing” without integration into a system. The worked MOM/MA trade example shows unusual honesty about the authors deviating from their own signals (buying late, selling early with no sell signal present) — instructive about real-world execution, but it undercuts the “trading system” framing elsewhere, since strict technicians would call this method rather than system discipline. Chart examples reference 2001-era stocks at dot-com-unwind volatility, so price levels are dated though the mechanics transfer directly. The book gives no aggregate backtested win rate, drawdown, or risk-adjusted return statistic for the MAC or MOM/MA methods; the examples are single illustrative cases, not systematic performance studies.

Who should read it

Beginning-to-intermediate stock investors who want one volume covering broker selection, stock screening, an honest tour of common indicators, a complete trend-following system (MAC) implementable without a computer, and a serious treatment of why discipline — not indicator choice — usually determines results. Less useful for traders already fluent in technical analysis wanting novel indicators, or anyone seeking a fully backtested, statistically validated system rather than a practitioner’s rule set illustrated with real trades.
