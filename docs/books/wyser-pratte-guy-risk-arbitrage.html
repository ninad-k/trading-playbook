Overview

Guy Wyser-Pratte, a longtime Wall Street arbitrageur, updates his own 1971/1982 monographs into this 2009 Wiley Investment Classics edition. The book is a working manual for risk (merger) arbitrage: buying the target (“Bride”) and shorting the acquirer (“Groom”) in a pending stock-for-stock merger to capture the spread between the current market price and the deal’s implied parity, while betting the deal closes. Roughly two-thirds of the book (Ch. 2-4) is the mechanics — calculating parity, estimating time to close, sizing the return, identifying deal risk — worked through dozens of real merger, cash-tender, exchange-offer, and freeze-in cases spanning the 1960s through the 2000s. The final chapters cover Wyser-Pratte’s own transition from passive arbitrage to shareholder activism.

Core thesis

Risk arbitrage is not a bet on the underlying stocks; it is a bet on whether a specific announced corporate transaction closes on its stated terms within a defensible timeframe. The arbitrageur’s job is to price that closing probability more accurately than the market-implied spread does, size a hedged long/short position accordingly, and require a return that compensates for the deal’s specific, enumerable risks (price risk, term changes, competing bids, antitrust, shareholder dissent, delay). Because spreads are set by the collective capital chasing them (“spread squeezing” when many arbitrageurs compete for few deals), a disciplined arbitrageur states a required return and declines a position when the market-implied spread doesn’t meet it, rather than reaching for return by taking on undisclosed risk.

Key concepts

  • Bride / Groom — the author’s shorthand for the target company being acquired (Bride) and the acquirer (Groom).
  • Parity — the total “work-out” value of the package of securities offered for the target, calculated from the exchange ratio and the current prices of the offered securities.
  • Spread (discount) — the gap between the target’s current market price and parity; the arbitrageur’s gross profit if the deal closes as announced.
  • Definitive agreement — the formal merger contract specifying exact terms and termination conditions, as distinct from the earlier, non-binding preliminary agreement.
  • “Short exempt” ruling — an NYSE designation, issued once a deal’s remaining risk is deemed negligible (shareholder approval obtained, favorable tax ruling received), that removes the uptick rule and reduced margin requirements on the short leg.
  • Freeze-in — the book’s term (Chapter 6) for a controlling shareholder using structural or governance mechanisms to squeeze out minority holders on unfavorable terms — a hazard distinct from ordinary deal risk.
  • Active arbitrage — Wyser-Pratte’s later career shift (Chapter 7 onward) from purely passive spread capture to using an arbitrage stake to actively push for a deal’s completion or improvement, i.e., shareholder activism.
  • Stub situation — a residual, thinly defined security or claim left over from a reorganization, traded as its own arbitrage opportunity.
  • Two-tiered offer — a tender structure (first seen in the McDermott/Pullman fight) paying more on the front end (cash) than the back end (securities), altering the economics and urgency of a position.
  • “Turning” a position — closing out early once most of the expected spread has been captured, rather than holding the full deal timeline for a smaller marginal return.
  • Hart-Scott-Rodino filing — the antitrust pre-notification requirement (from 1978) that starts the statutory waiting period the arbitrageur must factor into the time-to-close estimate.

Rules and setups

The core repeatable procedure — parity/spread calculation, annualized return, and the deal-risk checklist — is detailed exactly in Merger Arbitrage Spread Calculation and Deal-Risk Checklist. In summary:

  1. Confirm deal seriousness through paperwork milestones (SEC proxy filing, IRS tax-ruling application, antitrust filing) before committing capital, not on announcement alone.
  2. Calculate parity: sum each offered security’s (exchange ratio × current price).
  3. Estimate time to close by walking the standard sequence of approvals (definitive agreement, audit, tax ruling, SEC proxy clearance, shareholder vote, any regulatory approval, legal closing); a simple tax ruling took roughly 90-100 days, a complex one 100-120+ days, in the book’s period.
  4. Compute annualized return on investment: (parity − current price) ÷ current price × (12 ÷ months to close).
  5. Require a target return — the book cites a period “rule of thumb” of aiming for ~40% annualized, settling for ~30%, and passing on deals below what the specific risk profile justifies.
  6. Take the position long the target and short the acquirer in comparable dollar size (not necessarily matched share count), sequencing the two legs based on market direction and relative liquidity of each leg.
  7. Turn (close) the position early if a large fraction of the spread has already been captured well before the scheduled close, rather than holding for a smaller residual annualized gain.

Risk and money management

Position sizing is governed by five practical constraints, not a formal risk-per-trade formula: available capital, assessed deal risk, supply of the long-side stock, demand/liquidity for the short-side stock, and the ability to actually borrow the short-side shares for delivery. The book recommends small lots (300-500 shares as a period example, versus 3,000-5,000) to avoid moving the market against yourself when the offsetting leg isn’t immediately available. The short sale of the acquirer converts “risk on the market” into “risk on the deal only” — P&L should depend on whether the merger closes as announced, not on general market direction. The risk checklist sub-page has the full enumerated list of what can break a deal (price risk both ways, term changes, competing bids, shareholder dissent, financial-warranty breaches, tax-ruling risk, antitrust intervention, delay, personality clashes, freeze-in risk).

Psychology and discipline

Wyser-Pratte repeatedly frames arbitrage as an “investment in deals, not in securities” — a trader who stays long the acquirer’s stock after a deal breaks (rather than having hedged it away) has, in his framing, stopped being an arbitrageur and become an ordinary (and likely accidental) investor in that company. The book also documents the arbitrage community’s traditional secrecy and clubbishness, and is candid that when a firm suspects a position is going bad, “bad positions are often graciously turned over to one’s competitors” via intermediary brokers — a pointed acknowledgment of how adversarial and unsentimental professional arbitrage can be. Discipline shows up concretely in the requirement to walk away from a spread that doesn’t meet your required return rather than rationalizing a position into range, and in the tax-driven discipline of separating “Special Arbitrage Accounts” from ordinary investment accounts to avoid contaminating long-term capital gains treatment.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Introduction — history and derivation of risk arbitrage from riskless currency/security arbitrage; the arbitrage community’s evolution from the 1970s to 2000.
  • Ch 2 — Merger Arbitrage — gathering information, figuring parities, timing, return on investment, the risk checklist, average expected returns, taking and turning a position, consummation mechanics, tax strategy, antitrust considerations.
  • Ch 3 — Merger Arbitrage: Practical Applications — 12+ worked merger case studies from Scientific Data Systems/Xerox (pre-1971) through Utah International/General Electric, including option-based arbitrage.
  • Ch 4 — Cash Tender Offers — pre-1971 cases and the cash tender wave of the late 1970s/early 1980s, including Babcock & Wilcox/McDermott/United Technologies and several multi-party contested deals.
  • Ch 5 — Other Risk Arbitrage Situations — exchange offers, recapitalizations, spinoffs, stub situations, limited risk arbitrage.
  • Ch 6 — Corporate “Freeze-ins”: The Subterfuge Syndrome — notable examples of minority-squeeze-out structures.
  • Ch 7 — Active Arbitrage — the Bache/Pru-Bache years and the author’s own shift into shareholder activism from 1991 onward.
  • Appendices — additional parity calculations and the antitrust market-concentration index.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s greatest strength is its density of real, fully worked deal case studies spanning five decades, with actual prices, ratios, and realized (or lost) returns rather than abstract examples — a genuine practitioner’s field manual, not a textbook. It is also dated in specific, important ways: capital requirements, margin rules, the Hart-Scott-Rodino timeline, and “short exempt” mechanics reflect pre-2008 (and in places pre-1980s) market structure and regulation, all changed materially since; a modern arbitrageur must re-verify every procedural detail rather than take the book’s timelines at face value. The “rule of thumb” 40%/30% annualized return targets are explicitly tied to prevailing interest rates and deal supply, not universal benchmarks. The book has essentially no discussion of derivative-hedged stock deals beyond a brief options example, and gives no formal capital-allocation framework across concurrent positions.

Who should read it

Event-driven and merger-arbitrage practitioners, hedge fund analysts building deal-risk checklists, and traders who want to understand the mechanics (parity, spread, hedge construction, tax treatment) behind merger arbitrage as an asset class; not a source of general equity trading rules, and its regulatory/procedural specifics need updating against current rules before being applied to a live deal.
