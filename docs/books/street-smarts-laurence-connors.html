Overview

Written as a dialogue between two professional short-term traders, Laurence Connors and Linda Bradford Raschke, this book is a working manual of “surgical strike” setups rather than a technical-analysis primer. Each chapter presents one pattern with numbered entry/stop/exit rules, several annotated futures and equity chart examples from 1993-1996, and a closing conversation where the authors explain the market psychology behind the edge. The fifteen core setups are grouped into three families — tests of a prior high/low (Turtle Soup, Turtle Soup Plus One, 80-20’s, Momentum Pinball, 2-Period ROC), retracements in the direction of an established trend (the Anti, Holy Grail, ADX Gapper), and climax/exhaustion reversals (Whiplash, gap-fill patterns, Wolfe Waves, news reversals) — plus a final section on recognizing when a market has shifted from range-bound “swing trading” conditions into a trending “breakout mode” (Range Contraction / ID-NR4, Historical Volatility Meets Toby Crabel).

Core thesis

Markets alternate between range-bound oscillation and directional expansion, and most of a short-term trader’s edge comes from correctly identifying which regime is in force and reacting to well-defined, repeatable price patterns rather than predicting direction from fundamentals or opinion. The authors argue explicitly against mechanical, always-in-the-market systems (which large funds already exploit and which cannot use resting stop orders without slippage) and for a small trader’s structural advantage: nimbleness, tight stops, and the discipline to take only the setups that meet precise, pre-defined criteria. Every setup in the book shares one non-negotiable rule — enter the whole position at once, place a hard protective stop immediately at a defined risk point, and manage the trade defensively (scaling out, trailing stops) rather than letting losses run or profits round-trip.

Key concepts

  • Swing trading — actively trading support/resistance tests and retracements with tight stops, as distinct from trend-following (which tolerates large drawdowns for a big win) or fundamentals-based long-term investing.
  • Double stop point — a market test of a prior high/low, offering the tightest, lowest-risk entry because a single tick beyond the tested level invalidates the trade.
  • Single stop point — an entry on a pullback within an established trend (a higher low or lower high), where only one clear risk level exists because no prior test is required.
  • Climax/exhaustion pattern — a parabolic or range-expansion move signaling the last wave of participants entering; the book’s explicit signal to take profits on an entire position rather than add.
  • LBR/RSI — a 3-period RSI applied to a 1-period rate of change (net daily price change), used as the Momentum Pinball indicator to flag overbought/oversold exhaustion.
  • Taylor’s buy/sell-short day — George Douglass Taylor’s observation that markets rotate through a roughly two-to-three-day buy/sell/sell-short cycle, the conceptual basis for the 2-Period ROC and Momentum Pinball chapters.
  • ADX (Average Directional Index) — Welles Wilder’s trend-strength indicator, used as a filter (above 30 and rising) to confirm a market is trending strongly enough to trade retracements (Holy Grail, ADX Gapper).
  • NR4 / NR7 / inside day — Toby Crabel’s narrowest-range-of-N-days concepts, used as volatility-contraction signals that precede a breakout (“trend day”).
  • MOC (market on close) — entering or confirming a position at the closing price, used in Whiplash and several gap strategies to require same-day confirmation before committing capital.
  • “Anti” pattern — a stochastic (%K/%D) divergence-and-hook setup where a short-term retracement resolves back in the direction of the longer-term momentum trend.

Rules and setups

The book’s fifteen-plus setups fall into three families; the ones singled out as the core, most-traded methods each have a full companion system page with exact, codeable rules:

  1. Tests (of a prior 20-bar high/low or prior day’s range): Turtle Soup, Turtle Soup Plus One, 80-20's, Momentum Pinball, 2-Period ROC.
  2. Retracements (pullback entries in a defined trend): The Anti, The Holy Grail. A third retracement setup, the ADX Gapper, adds a +DI/-DI directional filter (12-period ADX > 30, +DI > -DI for buys) to a gap-fade-back-into-trend entry with a stop at the gap day’s low/high — not given its own page here but built on the same ADX-filter logic as the Holy Grail.
  3. Climax patterns: Whiplash and Wolfe Waves are the two given full pages. Related but not separately paged: Three-Day Unfilled Gap Reversals (buy/sell stop placed at the gap-day extreme, held open for three sessions, cancelled if unfilled) and Spike-and-Ledge / Three Little Indians (Chapter 14’s intraday exhaustion patterns — enter at-the-market on the reversal off a narrow-range “ledge” or a third symmetrical peak, stop at the recent swing extreme, moved to breakeven immediately).
  4. Breakout mode: [[street-smarts-laurence-connors–expansion-breakout]] covers both the ID/NR4 setup and the Historical-Volatility-Meets-Crabel variant, the book’s tools for recognizing when to stop swing-trading a range and instead straddle a volatility expansion with paired buy-stop/sell-stop orders.
  5. News reversals (Ch. 16-18, not separately paged): buy/sell a stock or future that fails to follow through on a shocking news event and instead recovers to (or through) its pre-news price, with the protective stop at the post-news extreme — illustrated with Intel’s 1994 Pentium-bug sell-off and the 1994 Orange County/MBIA municipal-bond scare.

Risk and money management

Chapter 3 states the book’s money-management rules apply identically to every setup: (1) enter the full intended position size at once — never scale into a winner; (2) place the initial protective stop immediately, one or two ticks beyond the most recent high/low that defines the setup’s risk point, before doing anything else; (3) scale out and trail the stop as the trade moves favorably rather than waiting for a single exit price. The authors are explicit that money management, not entry method, is “80 percent of the battle” for short-term traders, who — unlike trend-followers — rarely make a large profit on any single trade and so must cap losses tightly to survive. A recurring rule across setups is to tighten stops sharply (or exit entirely) the moment a position shows a parabolic or range-expansion move, on the premise that such moves mark the last wave of participants entering and therefore the highest-probability exit point.

Psychology and discipline

The authors are blunt that a trader’s own directional opinion — and any broker’s or guru’s opinion — is “your biggest enemy,” and repeatedly emphasize concentrating only on the pattern at hand (“the right-hand side of the chart”). Recommended habits include paper-trading a new setup until it is trusted, keeping a nightly trade journal and pre-market setup log (both authors describe decades of handwritten notebooks), never carrying a losing position home overnight, and treating a “windfall” profit as a signal to lock in gains rather than get greedy. They stress that professional results come from a skewed distribution — most trades are small wins or small losses, with the month’s profit concentrated in two or three large trades — so consistency in following rules matters more than any individual trade’s outcome, and the realistic first goal for a new trader should be to become breakeven, not to hit home runs.

  • Ch. 4-8 (Part One: Tests) — Turtle Soup, Turtle Soup Plus One, 80-20’s, Momentum Pinball, 2-Period ROC.
  • Ch. 9-11 (Part Two: Retracements) — The Anti, Holy Grail, ADX Gapper.
  • Ch. 12-15 (Part Three: Climax Patterns) — Whiplash, Three-Day Unfilled Gap Reversals, “A Picture’s Worth a Thousand Words” (Spike-and-Ledge, Three Little Indians), Wolfe Waves.
  • Ch. 16-18 — News, Morning News Reversals, Big Picture News Reversals — trading failed reactions to shocking news events.
  • Ch. 19-20 (Part Four: Breakout Mode) — Range Contraction (ID/NR4), Historical Volatility Meets Toby Crabel.
  • Ch. 21-25 (Part Five: Market Musings) — Smart Money Indicators, More Words on Trade Management, Be Prepared!, Final Thoughts, The Secrets of Successful Trading (a guest chapter on CTA longevity research).
  • Appendix — Moore Research Center statistical studies backing several setups, plus the historical-volatility formula.

Strengths and caveats

Every setup is stated as precise, numbered rules with real entry/stop prices from named contracts and dates, making this one of the more directly codeable short-term trading books of its era — a strength the authors reinforce by citing independent Moore Research Center back-testing in the appendix for several patterns. The caveats are equally explicit from the authors themselves: none of the setups is presented as a stand-alone mechanical system (they are discretionary patterns requiring the money-management chapter’s judgment layer), the 1993-1996 futures-pit examples use tick sizes, slippage assumptions, and market structure (open outcry, pre-decimalization) that have since changed, and the book’s own appendix caveats that its back-tests show “a market’s tendency,” not a validated trading system with quantified win rates. Some patterns (the Anti, Wolfe Waves) also require visual/subjective judgment (spotting a stochastic “hook” or a five-point wave count) despite an otherwise mechanical entry trigger.

Who should read it

Intermediate short-term and swing traders in futures or active equities who want fully specified, immediately testable setups rather than indicator theory — not a first technical-analysis book, since it assumes familiarity with stochastics, ADX, moving averages, and basic chart patterns throughout, and it is explicitly not a beginner’s introduction to reading price bars.
