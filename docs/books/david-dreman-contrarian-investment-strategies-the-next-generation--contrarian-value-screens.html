Dreman's Contrarian Value Screens

David Dreman · 1998 · Tier A — deep note system intermediate

Four statistically-backtested quintile screens — low P/E, low P/CF, low P/BV, high dividend yield — for buying out-of-favor, large-cap stocks and holding long-term.

What it is

Four related, purely valuation-based stock-selection screens Dreman backtested on the Compustat 1500 (large, established U.S. companies) over 1970–1996: low price-to-earnings (P/E), low price-to-cash-flow (P/CF), low price-to-book-value (P/BV), and high dividend yield (low price-to-dividend). Each screen independently selects the cheapest (or highest-yielding) quintile by that single ratio; all four beat both the market average and the corresponding “most expensive”/lowest-yield quintile over long holding periods. No forecasting of individual turnarounds is required — it is a systematic bet that the market persistently overreacts to a stock’s recent past and that reversion toward average valuations is itself the source of return.

Rules

Universe: large, established companies (Compustat 1500). Small-cap/Nasdaq stocks behave differently and are not assumed to follow the same statistics.

Screen construction (apply any or all, independently): 1. Low P/E: rank by trailing price-to-earnings; buy the cheapest quintile (bottom 20%). 2. Low P/CF: rank by price-to-cash-flow-per-share; buy the cheapest quintile. 3. Low P/BV: rank by price relative to industry- or market-relative book value (not raw historical book value); buy the cheapest quintile. 4. High yield / low P/D: rank by dividend yield; buy the highest-yielding quintile, favoring sustainable, growable payouts (a supporting quality check, not a separate ranking).

Preferred core: low P/E as the primary screen (most current, widely available, quarterly data) with the other three run opportunistically, since mispricings surface differently across the four measures at different times; the book recommends this eclectic approach over committing to a single ratio.

Position construction: diversify broadly — a well-diversified portfolio of roughly 16 stocks is cited as capturing about 85–90% of the return achievable from the full quintile.

Holding period: hold long-term. Odds of the low-P/E strategy beating the market rise with holding period — roughly 87 of 100 five-year periods, 94–95 of 100 ten-year periods, in Dreman’s sample; avoid quarterly changes, rebalance annually or longer.

Risk

Managed at the portfolio level, not per-position — no stop-loss or per-trade risk percentage. Controls: (a) diversification across ~16+ names so no single company’s disappointment dominates, and (b) patience through statistically-expected “cold streaks,” framed as expected variance around a favorable long-run edge (like a casino’s edge over many hands), not evidence the strategy has stopped working. Significance backing: low P/E and low P/CF each show about a 1-in-200 probability of their outperformance being chance; low P/BV about 1-in-100; the corresponding most-expensive/lowest-yield quintiles show weaker (~1-in-20, 95%-confidence) but generally significant underperformance.

Caveats

The backtest window (~1970–1996) predates the late-1990s valuation extremes and their unwind. The book states five “quality” indicators for stock selection alongside the valuation quintile; only Indicator 5 (sustainable, growing dividend yield) was recoverable from the sampled OCR pages — Indicators 1–4 are not included here and should not be assumed. Treat the four ratio screens above as necessary but, per the source, not sufficient alone. No explicit sell/exit rule or valuation-based rebalancing trigger is given beyond “hold long-term, rebalance infrequently.”
