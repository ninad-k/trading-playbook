Overview

Written by Thomas J. Dorsey and four analysts at Dorsey, Wright & Associates (DWA), the firm that has specialized in point-and-figure (P&F) research since the late 1980s, this book is organized as a “playbook” of roughly 70 short, standalone tips (“plays”) grouped into nine chapters, using a recurring football analogy (offense/defense, field position, game plan). Rather than one connected argument, it is a practitioner’s grab-bag: P&F chart construction and reading, market psychology, sector and market-timing tools (bullish percent indices), individual stock selection criteria, risk/reward and position-sizing mechanics, short-term trading tactics, portfolio management and sell discipline, options strategies for hedging and income, and miscellaneous tips (DRIPs, insider buying, dividend-reinvestment). The throughline across the plays is DWA’s core methodology: use point-and-figure charts (not bar or candlestick charts) to read supply/demand, and the bullish percent concept to time markets and sectors, layered under strict risk and money-management rules.

Core thesis

Stock selection answers “what to buy,” but only technical analysis (specifically point-and-figure charting) answers “when to buy and when to sell” — fundamentals identify candidates, but supply/demand as shown on a P&F chart determines timing. Markets and sectors move in a repeating football-like rhythm of offense (demand in control, columns of Xs, bullish percent rising) and defense (supply in control, columns of Os, bullish percent falling); a disciplined trader determines which phase the market/sector is in before selecting individual stocks, and applies the same rigor to selling (predetermined stops, partial profit-taking) as to buying. Consistency and process discipline — sticking to one methodology rather than switching systems after every losing trade — matter more than any single indicator’s precision.

Key concepts

  • Point-and-figure (P&F) chart — a time-independent chart plotting only price: columns of Xs (rising, demand) and Os (falling, supply), each column requiring a minimum of three boxes, with columns always alternating X/O.
  • Box size — the price increment per box; DWA’s standard scale is ¼ point ($0–$5), ½ point ($5–$20), 1 point ($20–$100), 2 points ($100–$200), 4 points (above $200) — non-percentage breakpoints that widen as price rises.
  • Three-box reversal — the standard rule requiring price to move at least three boxes in the opposite direction before a new column (and a change in chart direction) is recorded, filtering out minor noise.
  • Bullish percent — the percentage of stocks within a universe (an index, sector, or exchange) currently on a P&F buy signal; plotted on its own 0–100% grid (“football field”), with readings above 70% considered overbought/high-risk and below 30% oversold/low-risk; the best buy signals come from a reversal up from below 30%, the best sell signals from a reversal down through 70%.
  • Bullish support line / bearish resistance line — the two main P&F trendlines, each drawn at a 45-degree angle from the lowest O (support line) or highest X (resistance line) in the chart’s base/top; price holding above the support line signals an uptrend, and a break signals a trend change.
  • Relative strength (RS) — a stock’s or sector’s price divided by the S&P 500 (×100), plotted on its own P&F chart; a column of Xs on the RS chart means the issue is outperforming the market, independent of the issue’s own price trend.
  • Double/triple top and bottom, catapult, triangle, shakeout — the named P&F chart patterns: a double top is the simplest buy signal (an X column exceeding the prior X column); a catapult combines a triple top followed by a double top; a shakeout requires at least five columns of lower tops/higher bottoms resolved by a breakout; a triangle requires a minimum of seven columns.
  • High pole / low pole warning — a low pole warning occurs when a decline exceeds the prior bottom by three or more boxes then retraces more than 50% of that decline (bullish); a high pole warning is the mirror-image bearish signal on a rally.
  • Percent risk position sizing — sizing a trade so the dollar risk to the stop-loss equals a fixed percentage (DWA uses 1%) of current account equity, with share count = (% risk × equity) ÷ (entry price − stop price); an “anti-martingale” approach where position size grows only as equity grows.
  • Field position (offense/defense) — the book’s football framing for whether the bullish percent (market, sector, or stock universe) favors buying (offense, column of Xs, low field position) or defense (column of Os, high field position, wealth-preservation mode).

Rules and setups

  1. Four-step top-down process for new positions. (1) Market: check NYSE/OTC/options bullish percent and the High-Low Index to decide offense vs. defense; (2) Sector: favor sectors in a column of Xs on their own bullish percent chart, ideally still below 50%, and outperforming the S&P 500 on relative strength; (3) Fundamentals: build a fundamentally sound inventory; (4) Technicals: narrow to the names with the best P&F chart pattern, trend, and relative strength.
  2. Trend filter. Only buy stocks trading above their bullish support line (positive trend); avoid stocks below their bearish resistance line.
  3. Relative strength filter. Favor stocks whose own RS chart (vs. S&P 500) and peer-group RS chart are both in a column of Xs; for sector selection, favor sectors where at least three of the four DWA market-leadership indicators (sector RS trend, % of stocks with RS buy signals, % of stocks in RS Xs, % of stocks in positive trend) are positive.
  4. Risk/reward minimum. Require at least a 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio before entering: reward = P&F price objective (via vertical or horizontal count) minus current price; risk = current price minus the predetermined stop-loss level.
  5. Position sizing (percent risk method). Risk no more than 1% of current portfolio equity per trade; shares to buy = (1% × equity) ÷ (entry price − stop price); recompute equity (mark-to-market) before sizing each new trade.
  6. Bullish percent extremes. Best buy signals occur when a bullish percent index reverses up from below 30%; best sell/defense signals occur when it reverses down through 70%. A 6-percentage-point move (three boxes on a 2% scale) is required to register a reversal.
  7. Profit-taking (thirds rule). Sell one-third of a position once it is up 30%; sell another third once up 50%; hold the final third with a trailing technical stop (trendline violation or RS reversal) rather than a fixed target, to preserve exposure to long-term winners.
  8. Stop-loss discipline. Set a specific stop-loss price (typically a bullish-support-line violation or a double-bottom break) before entry and honor it without exception; treat a stock that hits its stop as “on death row” — allow at most a brief bounce window before closing if it fails to hold.
  9. Buy on rumor, sell on news. Write down the specific reason for a purchase before buying; when the anticipated catalyst (e.g., an earnings report) occurs, close the position regardless of the immediate price reaction, because the original thesis no longer applies.
  10. Short-term “long tail of Os” bounce trade. For aggressive traders: after a stock has fallen at least 18 boxes (Os) in a P&F column, buy on the first three-box reversal up, using the prior double-bottom level as the stop.
  11. Turnover discipline. Avoid switching between similar positions without a compelling technical reason; frequent trading (documented via an Odean/Barber study cited in the book) correlates with worse performance.
  12. Defense checklist (when bullish percent indicators turn bearish). Come off margin; stop initiating new (or only partial) positions; wait for pullbacks to support rather than chasing; rotate toward sectors already oversold and showing signs of bottoming; always attach a stop to any position opened during a defensive market phase.

Risk and money management

Position sizing is explicit and quantitative: DWA’s tracking portfolio risks exactly 1% of current account equity per position, with share count derived directly from the distance to the stop-loss (worked example: a $1,000,000 account risking 1% = $10,000; a stock bought at $49 with a $45 stop has $4/share risk, yielding a 2,500-share position). The book cites Van K. Tharp’s research showing this percent-risk method outsized equal-dollar and equal-share sizing by roughly 8x in a controlled backtest ($1.84M vs. ~$230K over the same trade signals), while a percent-volatility method did slightly better still. Loss tolerance is framed numerically: a 15–20% loss is called “manageable,” while a 50% loss is called “devastating” because it requires a 100% gain to recover. Profit management uses the fixed thirds rule (sell a third at +30%, another third at +50%, run the remainder on a trailing technical stop). At the portfolio level, defensive posture is dictated by the bullish percent reading crossing above 70% (reduce exposure, come off margin) or below 30% (favorable for initiating positions), reflecting the book’s central “offense vs. defense” framing of risk control.

Psychology and discipline

The book frames investing discipline through a sports-team lens throughout: consistency in following one methodology (rather than switching systems after a bad trade) is presented as the common trait among successful investors regardless of which system they use. Common investor mistakes are named explicitly: falling in love with a position, buying right but failing to sell right, having no game plan, and letting emotion override a predetermined stop or profit-taking rule. The “death row” framing for stopped-out positions (give a brief window for a bounce, then execute the stop without further debate) is offered as an emotional discipline device. A recurring theme is that markets go up roughly two-thirds of the time and down one-third, but decline roughly twice as fast as they rise, so a purely offensive (always-long, always-buying) posture underperforms over a full cycle; defensive skill (coming off margin, waiting for pullbacks, tightening entries) is treated as equally important as offense.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Building a Foundation: Point and Figure Basics: chart construction (Xs/Os, box size, three-box reversal), the standard bullish/bearish P&F chart pattern catalog, box-size adjustment for volatile stocks, and the bullish percent concept.
  • Ch 2 — Market Psychology: finding and sticking to a discipline, common investor mistakes, an adaptation of Bernard Baruch’s rules, and the value of working with a broker/money manager.
  • Ch 3 — Market and Sector Applications: the 80/20 phenomenon, sector vs. market timing, sector bullish percent charts and sector relative strength, market leadership indicators, and the Dow Jones 20 Bond Average as an interest-rate P&F indicator.
  • Ch 4 — Essentials of Stock Selection: the four-step top-down selection process, the stock evaluation checklist, bullish support/bearish resistance lines, relative strength (including peer relative strength), buying on pullbacks, and momentum.
  • Ch 5 — Risk/Reward Principles: the risk/reward-is-not-free-lunch principle, evaluating risk/reward with vertical/horizontal price-objective counts, using options to enter positions with defined risk, and protective puts as stock insurance.
  • Ch 6 — Short-Term Trading Techniques: tactics for short-term traders, the “long tail of Os” bounce technique, high short interest squeezes, the selling climax, trading spin-offs, the NYSE High-Low Index, and option-expiration strategy.
  • Ch 7 — Managing Your Investment: minimizing turnover, letting profits run, percent-risk position sizing, the sell-discipline checklist (profit-taking thirds, stop points, “death row”), buy-on-rumor/sell-on-news, and hedging a portfolio with index options.
  • Ch 8 — Understanding Options Strategies: option basics and delta, call/put guidelines, avoiding overleveraging, buying time (longer-dated options), puts as insurance, and covered-write selection.
  • Ch 9 — Other Investment Tips: the fundamental analyst’s role, an alternative to the Dogs of the Dow, DRIPs, and general market-sentiment indicators (magazine covers, cocktail-party indicator).

Strengths and caveats

The book’s strength is concrete, numerically anchored technique — box-size tables, the exact bullish percent formula and thresholds, worked position-sizing arithmetic, and a specific profit-taking schedule (30%/50% thirds) — delivered in short, self-contained sections that are easy to extract into rules. Its point-and-figure patterns and the bullish support/resistance line construction are described precisely enough to code directly. The main caveat is that it is a “tips” compendium rather than a unified system: individual plays occasionally sit in tension (e.g., “let profits run” alongside a fixed thirds-selling rule), and price-objective mechanics (the vertical/horizontal count) are referenced and used in examples without the underlying formula being fully spelled out in the text. Historical examples (Amgen, Qualcomm, Xerox, the 1999–2000 tech-stock references, the Dow Jones 20 Bond Average case study through 2000) are dated to the late-1990s bull market and its unwind, and the 2:1 risk/reward and 1% risk figures are presented as DWA house practice rather than backtested optima. Options-related margin and product references (SPDRs, QQQs, expiration mechanics circa 2001) should not be taken as current.

Who should read it

Best suited to a swing or position trader who wants a concrete introduction to point-and-figure charting and DWA’s bullish-percent sector-timing framework, along with practical position-sizing and sell-discipline rules, in a light, example-driven format. Less useful as a first exposure to fundamental analysis (it explicitly defers stock-picking fundamentals to other sources) or as a rigorously backtested quantitative system — treat it as a curated set of practitioner heuristics rather than a validated trading system.
