Summary

A step-by-step walkthrough of how a U.S. stock order gets filled, written by Georgetown professor James J. Angel for the Nasdaq Education Foundation. It follows a hypothetical 500-share order from click to settlement, explaining market vs. limit orders, how Nasdaq market makers and ECNs compete for order flow, how the NYSE specialist system worked, and the regulatory structure (SEC, NASD/SROs) policing execution quality. A glossary of market-structure terms closes the document.

Key points

  • Market order — fills immediately at the best available price (the ask when buying, the bid when selling); the bid-ask spread is the basic cost of immediacy.
  • Limit order — sets a maximum buy or minimum sell price; may never fill, but can get a better price than the current market, and must be displayed if it improves the best quote.
  • Market maker — a dealer posting continuous two-sided quotes in a stock, profiting from the bid-ask spread; the average Nasdaq stock had 10+ competing market makers, with over 300 market-making firms active market-wide.
  • ECN (electronic communications network) — an order-matching book (e.g., Instinet, Island) that lets subscribers trade directly with each other; roughly a third of Nasdaq volume passed through ECNs.
  • NYSE specialist — a single member firm per stock acting as both auctioneer and market maker on the physical trading floor, obligated to maintain a “fair and orderly market.”
  • Best execution — brokers are legally obligated to route orders for the best available terms (price, speed, fill quality), weighing multiple competing venues; “payment for order flow” arrangements exist between market makers and brokers.
  • Settlement (T+3) — trades are agreed at execution but not final until money and shares change hands, three business days later, processed through the Depository Trust and Clearing Corporation (DTCC).
  • SEC Rules 11Ac1-5 and 11Ac1-6 (adopted Nov. 2000) require market centers and broker-dealers to publicly disclose execution-quality statistics and order-routing practices.

Actionable rules

None given — this is a descriptive guide to market plumbing, not a trading system. Its practical value for a trader: understanding that a market order guarantees fill but not price, while a limit order guarantees price but not fill, and that “best execution” obligations mean brokers — not the trader — choose which venue an order is routed to unless a direct-access broker is used.

Caveats

Written circa 2001-2002 (Nasdaq trading-volume and market-maker tables reference 2001 data): specific market-share numbers, the described OTC/regional-exchange landscape, and firm names (e.g., named top Nasdaq market makers and NYSE specialist firms) are all dated and have since consolidated or disappeared. The NYSE floor-specialist model it describes was substantially replaced by electronic trading in the years after publication. Core order-type and market-maker/ECN concepts remain valid.

Who it is for

Beginners who want to understand what actually happens between clicking “buy” and owning shares — order types, who’s on the other side of the trade, and how execution quality is regulated.
