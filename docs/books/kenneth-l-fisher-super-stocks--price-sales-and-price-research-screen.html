The Price/Sales and Price/Research Screen

Kenneth L. Fisher · 1984 · Tier A system intermediate

A two-ratio screen — Price/Sales under 0.75 and, for tech names, Price/Research 5–10 — for buying growth companies priced like failures after an earnings glitch.

What it is

Fisher’s method for finding “Super Stocks” — companies that return 3–10x in three to five years. Because sales rarely disappear the way earnings can, he values companies on the Price/Sales Ratio (PSR = market value ÷ trailing 12-month sales) instead of P/E, then, for technology companies, cross-checks with the Price/Research Ratio (PRR = market value ÷ trailing 12-month R&D spend). The goal: surface Super Companies while priced like mediocre ones, typically just after an earnings “glitch” has driven away P/E-focused buyers.

Rules

Step 1 — qualitative filter (must pass first): confirm growth orientation, marketing excellence, a durable “unfair advantage” (usually lowest-cost producer or proprietary position), healthy personnel culture, and strong financial controls. A low PSR on a mediocre company is a value trap, not a Super Stock.

Step 2 — PSR entry: - Buy Super Companies at a PSR of 0.75 or less; avoid above 1.5; never buy above 3.0. - Separate bands apply outside the small-growth universe: basic-industry (“smokestack”) stocks — buy under 0.4; huge companies — buy near 0.4. Larger, more mature businesses structurally carry lower PSRs, so scale the threshold to size.

Step 3 — PRR cross-check (technology companies only): - Favor a PRR of 5–10; below 5 is rare. Reject above 15. - Use PRR to catch mismatches PSR misses: a marginally high PSR (~1.0) with a low PRR (~5) can mean cheap, underappreciated R&D — investigate the marketing behind it. A low PSR (~0.75) with a high PRR (25+) usually means starved R&D — a warning, not a buy signal. - Treat PRR as a rough (±20%) gauge, not a precision instrument — Fisher warns against treating 10.4 as meaningfully different from 11.8.

Step 4 — screening workflow: scan for low-PSR names, separately scan for money-losing companies with unfamiliar names (neglect is itself a signal), and separately collect qualitative “outstanding business” tips from other sources. Cross-reference all three against Step 1.

Step 5 — sizing and holding period: a concentrated, long-horizon (3–5 year) fundamental position, not a trade. No per-position sizing formula is given, but Fisher implicitly expects a handful of high-conviction names. Hold as long as the PSR stays low and the Super Company traits persist.

Risk

Risk control happens before and during the position, not through a stop-loss: - Before buying: require free working capital covering at least five years of worst-case losses and three years of negative cash flow; debt under 40% of assets; management stock ownership of at least 10x combined salary; prefer top-tier-audited financials; treat concentrated “controlling interest” ownership as a flag requiring extra scrutiny, since no board can remove an entrenched underperforming controlling owner. - While holding: monitor whether the Step 1 traits still hold; losing any of them is a reason to exit regardless of PSR.

Caveats

No stop-loss, position-sizing percentage, or diversification rule exists anywhere in the system — Fisher relies entirely on buying quality cheaply and holding. The numeric thresholds (0.75, 1.5, 3.0, 5–10, 15) are calibrated to early-1980s valuation levels and inflation; Fisher states they must scale with company size and inflation, so applying them unadjusted today requires judgment. The PRR leg applies only to R&D-intensive companies. The system depends on getting the qualitative Super Company assessment right first — the ratios alone, on a mediocre business, will not produce a Super Stock.
