The Flow of the Markets

Van K. Tharp · 2005 · Tier B — concise note article beginner

Short psychology essay using a river-flow metaphor to argue traders lose by fighting the market's direction and refusing to accept small losses.

Summary

A short psychological essay, not a trading system. Tharp uses the metaphor of being carried down a river: moving with the current is effortless, fighting it is exhausting and futile. He applies this to markets — trading with the prevailing direction (“flow”) works with far less struggle than trading against it, and the classic problem of traders holding losing positions too long stems from ego (refusing to admit being wrong) rather than from anything the market is doing. The core message is that market movement is neutral; the trader’s resistance to it is the actual source of losses and stress.

Key points

  • The “buy high, sell low” adage is reframed: buying when price is high means buying in the direction of the existing flow, which is not inherently irrational — fear of buying high is fear of going with the trend.
  • A trader cannot know in advance how long a trend (“the river”) will continue in its current direction; duration so far is not evidence of an imminent reversal.
  • Persistent directional bias (e.g., a trader who is a chronic bear and reads every wiggle as a top) causes repeated losing trades against the prevailing flow until the market eventually does turn.
  • The refusal to accept a loss is framed as an ego problem — accepting a loss feels like admitting the trader was wrong, so losses are allowed to grow larger rather than being cut small.
  • Tharp’s core reframe: “the problem has nothing to do with the market… the problem stems from you, the trader” — struggle is self-generated by resisting price direction, not caused by the market itself.
  • The piece implies (without stating specific numbers) that trend-following and prompt loss-cutting are the practical expressions of “going with the flow.”

Actionable rules

  1. Trade in the direction of the prevailing price movement rather than anticipating reversals based on how long a move has already lasted.
  2. Accept losses early and small rather than holding a losing position out of ego or the need to be proven right — the article’s implicit rule, though no specific stop-loss size or percentage is given.
  3. Treat market movement as neutral information, not a personal adversary, to reduce the emotional escalation that leads to outsized losses.
  4. “None given” for concrete numeric rules (entries, stops, position sizing) — this is a mindset piece, not a system.

Caveats

Extremely short (two pages) and purely conceptual/motivational; it offers no system, no numbers, and no testable rules — it is a psychological reframe meant to be paired with an actual trading method and risk-management plan. The trend-following stance (“go with the flow”) is asserted rather than backtested or qualified with exceptions (e.g., mean-reversion contexts where fighting a short-term extreme is the correct approach).

Who it is for

Traders struggling to cut losses or who fight the prevailing trend out of a need to be right; a short psychological reminder rather than a strategy resource.
