Summary

A Stocks & Commodities article (V13:5) by Tim Ord describing a method for timing short-term S&P 500 tops and bottoms by combining extreme readings on the NYSE tick index (the count of stocks trading on an uptick minus those on a downtick) with confirming candlestick reversal patterns. The article lays out specific tick thresholds for bullish and bearish setups, illustrates them with 1994 S&P 500 examples, and covers variations, limitations, and a closing-tick-based signal.

Key points

  • Tick index: number of NYSE stocks trading on an uptick minus number trading on a downtick, broadcast intraday by most data vendors.
  • Bullish setup: intraday tick reading below −800 (preferably beyond −1,000, “extreme” at −1,100+) near a double bottom on the S&P 500 daily chart, where the second bottom is no more than 2.5 S&P points below the first, confirmed by a bullish candlestick pattern (e.g., harami, hammer).
  • Bearish setup: intraday tick reading above +600 near a double top, where the second top is no more than 2.5 S&P points above the first, confirmed by a bearish candlestick pattern (e.g., dark cloud cover).
  • Signals are stronger when the two tops/bottoms occur within five business days of each other.
  • Variation: if the extreme reading occurs one day and the market gaps further the next day by more than 2.5 S&P points with a confirming candlestick, that next day can trigger the signal instead.
  • No reversal should be anticipated while the day’s open-to-close range (candlestick body) exceeds two S&P points, regardless of how extreme the tick reading is — the trend is still considered intact.
  • Closing tick readings above +750 are noted as evidence of a potential short-term top; readings of +1,000 or more “almost always” mark a short-term top.
  • Extreme readings do not always mark a reversal — sometimes they mark the start of a sideways consolidation instead of a clean top/bottom (examples given from February and November 1994).
  • 1994 illustrative case: three-day cumulative average tick readings around −1,450 to −1,460 preceded the April 4, 1994 low of the year.

Actionable rules

  1. Watch for an intraday tick reading beyond −800 (stronger below −1,000/−1,100) coinciding with a double bottom on the S&P 500 where the second low is within 2.5 points of the first.
  2. Watch for an intraday tick reading beyond +600 coinciding with a double top where the second high is within 2.5 points of the first.
  3. Require a confirming candlestick reversal pattern (e.g., harami, hammer for bottoms; dark cloud cover for tops) before acting.
  4. Prefer setups where the two extremes (first and second top/bottom) occur within five business days of each other.
  5. Do not expect a turn while the day’s candlestick body exceeds 2 S&P points, even at extreme tick readings; tighten stops and watch for confirmation once the body narrows below 2 points.
  6. Treat closing tick readings above +750 (and especially +1,000+) as a warning sign of a short-term top.

Caveats

The thresholds (±600/±800/±1,100, the 2.5-point spread, the 5-day window) are calibrated to the S&P 500 and NYSE tick data of the mid-1990s; absolute point thresholds likely need rescaling for a much higher-priced, differently-volatile modern index. The article states these reversal setups “do not occur often,” so it is a situational timing tool rather than a standalone trading system, and no win-rate or backtest statistics are provided.

Who it is for

Short-term S&P 500 traders who already track the NYSE tick index and want a concrete, rule-based way to combine it with candlestick confirmation for timing reversals.
