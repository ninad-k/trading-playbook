Summary

A May 2000 AAII Journal article by Wayne A. Thorp explaining J. Welles Wilder’s Relative Strength Index (RSI), introduced in Wilder’s 1978 “New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems.” The article walks through the RSI formula and standard 14-period default, its typical 30/70 oscillation range, several ways to trade crossovers of those levels, how divergence and “failure swings” generate reversal signals, and why the indicator loses usefulness in strongly trending markets.

Key points

  • RSI formula: RS = average price gain on up periods ÷ average price loss on down periods; RSI = 100 − [100 ÷ (1 + RS)], oscillating between 0 and 100.
  • Wilder’s default period is 14 (day/week/month); 9-period and 25-period RSIs are also common, with shorter periods producing more volatile, more frequent signals.
  • Classic overbought/oversold thresholds are 70/30; some traders use 80/20, especially with the more volatile 9-day RSI.
  • Three crossover trading approaches described: (1) buy below 30 / sell above 70 (risks entering before the trend exhausts); (2) sell when RSI crosses below 70 / buy when it crosses above 30 (a lagging, trend-confirming approach); (3) sell when RSI turns down from above 70 / buy when it turns up from below 30 (a more centrist approach, still prone to false signals in strong trends).
  • A backtested example: selling below-70 crosses and buying above-30 crosses on Microsoft (3/30/1998–3/28/2000) produced five round-trip trades and a cumulative 106.5% return.
  • Divergence: price makes a new low while RSI (below 30) makes a higher low (or double bottom) — an early warning of a possible upside reversal; the mirror pattern (price higher high, RSI lower high above 70) warns of a downside reversal.
  • “Failure swing” sell signal: RSI forms two peaks above 70, then breaks below the trough between them even while price is still rising — this is treated as a sell signal independent of price action (illustrated with Amgen, January 2000).
  • Key limitation: RSI performs poorly in sustained trending markets, where it can pin at extreme levels for weeks or months and generate no useful signal (illustrated with Ortel Corp., which rose ~480% after RSI gave a premature sell in October 1999).
  • The article recommends using RSI alongside other indicators (e.g., moving averages) rather than as a standalone system.

Actionable rules

  1. Default calculation period: 14 (day/week/month); use 9 for more sensitivity, 25 for smoother/less frequent signals.
  2. Standard overbought/oversold levels: 70/30 (or 80/20 for a more volatile short-period RSI).
  3. If price volatility is too low to push RSI into the 70/30 extremes, shorten the period to increase sensitivity; if RSI moves outside the extremes too often, lengthen the period.
  4. Treat a “failure swing” (RSI double top above 70 with a break below the intervening trough, or the inverse below 30) as a sell/buy signal even if price has not yet reversed.
  5. Do not trade RSI extremes in isolation during a strong trend — confirm with a second indicator (e.g., a moving average) before acting.

Caveats

Article is a general-audience primer (AAII Journal, May 2000) rather than a rigorous backtest; the single illustrative trading result (Microsoft, 106.5% over two years, five trades) is one example, not a statistically robust study, and no drawdown, sample size, or out-of-sample data is given. Figures/charts referenced in the original (MetaStock-produced) are not reproduced here.

Who it is for

Traders new to RSI who want a concise explanation of its calculation, standard trading approaches, divergence/failure-swing signals, and its known weakness in trending markets.
