Macroeconomic Implications of the Beliefs and Behavior of Foreign Exchange Traders

Yin-Wong Cheung and Menzie D. Chinn · 1999 · Tier B — concise note paper advanced

A 1997 survey of U.S. foreign-exchange traders documents rapid news adjustment, horizon-dependent fundamentals, and mixed views of speculation and intervention.

Summary

Cheung and Chinn report a mail survey of U.S. foreign-exchange traders conducted from October 1996 through November 1997. Of 1,752 deliverable questionnaires, 142 were returned, an 8.1% response rate (p. 5). Respondents describe 30% of current spot trading as technical, 25% as fundamental, 22% as customer-order driven, and 23% as short-horizon “jobbing”; technical trading had risen from 19% five years earlier (pp. 6-8, 27).

Dealers say exchange rates absorb unexpected unemployment, trade, inflation, GDP, and interest-rate news extremely quickly: for each, most adjustment occurs within one minute, and roughly one third report adjustment within ten seconds. Money-supply news adjusts more slowly (pp. 9-10, 28). The economic variable judged most important changes over time, while interest rates remain prominent (pp. 10-12, 29).

The survey draws a sharp horizon distinction. Only 1.4% say intraday exchange rates reflect fundamentals, compared with 58.7% within six months and 88.4% beyond six months (pp. 13-14, 30). At intraday horizons, respondents emphasize bandwagon effects, news overreaction, and speculation; at long horizons, 87.4% select economic fundamentals (pp. 14-15, 32). These are practitioner beliefs, not causal estimates or trading rules.

Key points

  • Eighty-four percent say speculation increases volatility, but 81% say it improves liquidity and 74% say it improves efficiency (p. 16, p. 33).
  • Views of intervention effectiveness are split, while 61% say intervention raises volatility (pp. 16-17, 34).
  • Sixty-three percent dismiss purchasing-power parity as academic jargon, and 81% would take no action on a PPP-indicated dollar overvaluation (pp. 17-19, 35-36).
  • Forty percent nevertheless believe PPP affects exchange rates beyond six months (pp. 18-19, 36).

Actionable rules

  1. Treat release effects as very high-frequency events; minute or daily data may miss the initial adjustment documented by the survey (pp. 9-10).
  2. Re-estimate which macro announcements matter rather than fixing their importance permanently, because dealer rankings changed across the two survey dates (pp. 10-12).
  3. Keep intraday microstructure explanations separate from long-horizon fundamental hypotheses when designing FX research (pp. 13-15, 19-20).

Caveats

The low response rate raises selection concerns, responses measure beliefs rather than realized behavior, and all observations are from the late-1990s U.S. dealer market. Percentages sometimes reflect incomplete or multiple responses.

Who it is for

FX researchers and macro traders studying how practitioners viewed news, fundamentals, speculation, intervention, and PPP across horizons.
