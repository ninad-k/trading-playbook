Overview

Steven E. Bolten, a finance professor (University of South Florida, Wharton-trained, ex-Merrill Lynch), presents a unifying explanation for stock market cycles built on the dividend discount model: a stock’s price is the present value of expected earnings/dividends discounted at a required rate of return, so price movement reduces to the relative rate of change in (a) expected earnings and (b) interest rates/risk. The book develops this “Equation (3) valuation framework” in Chapter 2, works out all nine combinations of earnings/risk changes in Chapter 1’s Table 1.1, and traces a repeating four-stage economic/stock-price cycle in Chapter 3. Later chapters apply the framework to asset allocation and sector rotation (Ch. 4), individual stock risk via a “build-up” discount-rate model (Ch. 5), the industry life cycle (Ch. 6), and P/E multiple behavior (Ch. 7). Chapter 8 reprints the author’s own peer-reviewed papers, including backtests of a quarterly asset-allocation rule and a market “bubble” indicator derived from the same framework.

Core thesis

Stock prices move because the relative rates of change in expected earnings and interest rates are almost never equal; whichever factor is changing faster dominates and sets market direction. This produces a repeatable four-stage cycle keyed to the business cycle: prices bottom before the economic trough (Stage I, earnings turning up while rates are still low/falling - the most bullish combination), keep rising as earnings growth outpaces rising rates (Stage II), top when rate increases exceed earnings growth (Stage III), and fall into recession until earnings’ rate of decline slows below rates’ rate of decline (Stage IV, the eventual low). Because interest rates fluctuate off a smaller base than total corporate earnings, rate changes tend to dominate briefly and sharply (bear transitions), while earnings-driven bull markets are more gradual and longer-lasting - why bull markets have historically outlasted bear markets. The same numerator/denominator logic explains sector rotation, the industry life cycle, and P/E expansion/contraction, and is empirically tested in Chapter 8’s reprinted papers.

Key concepts

  • Equation (3) valuation framework — P = Σ [E(1-λ)/(1+r)^t]: stock price is the present value of expected future earnings (E) net of the retention rate (λ), discounted at the required rate of return (r).
  • Fungibility — all financial assets compete for capital on an expected-return/risk basis, so a change in one asset’s yield (especially the long Treasury bond) forces re-pricing of all others.
  • Relative rate of change — the central diagnostic: whichever of expected earnings or interest rates is changing at the faster percentage rate dominates stock-price direction.
  • Table 1.1 combinations — a 9-row matrix of every earnings-change/risk-change pairing and its implied stock-price direction; used to classify where the market sits in its cycle.
  • Four-stage cycle — Stage I (post-trough, earnings turning up, rates flat/falling, fastest bull phase), Stage II (earnings still rising but rates accelerating, market tops), Stage III (rate increases exceed earnings growth, bear market begins), Stage IV (recession; market bottoms when earnings’ decline rate equals rates’ decline rate).
  • Build-up model — individual-stock discount rate: r = real rate + inflation premium + equity risk premium + size + business risk + financial/leverage risk + marketability risk + other risk.
  • Industry life cycle — venture capital → rapid growth → mature → stable/decline; each stage shifts the numerator (earnings growth) versus denominator (discount rate) balance.
  • P/E as 1/r — for a constant-earnings stock, P/E equals the reciprocal of the required rate of return; it expands when rates fall and contracts when rates rise, independent of earnings.
  • Time horizon duration (“n”) — years of assumed earnings growth needed to justify the gap between a stock’s/index’s price and its theoretical no-growth value; extreme readings flag bubble or trough conditions.
  • Total Change indicator — %ΔE (prior-quarter earnings change) minus %Δi (prior-quarter bond-yield change); a quarterly stock/bond reallocation signal (see system sub-page).
  • Bond/stock linkage — bond and stock prices are typically unlinked through most of Stages I, II and IV, and linked mainly in Stage III - contrary to the common assumption they always move together.

Rules and setups

This is a valuation/macro-cycle framework rather than a technical trading system - there are no chart patterns, entries, or stops. Its actionable content is diagnostic and asset-allocation oriented:

  1. Cycle diagnosis: each period, compare the percentage rate of change in consensus expected earnings against the percentage rate of change in the long Treasury yield; the larger rate of change determines market direction (Table 1.1).
  2. Identify the stage: use the earnings-direction/rate-direction combination to classify the market as Stage I-IV, implying early-bull, late-bull, early-bear, or late-bear/bottoming.
  3. Sector/asset positioning by stage: cyclical, high-business-risk stocks are most attractive when earnings acceleration dominates (Stage I/II); defensive, stable-earnings names become relatively more attractive as Stage III/IV nears.
  4. Bond-stock linkage check: expect stocks and bonds “unlinked” through most of Stages I, II and IV, and “linked” mainly during Stage III rate spikes.
  5. P/E interpretation: rising P/E with flat earnings signals falling required returns, not necessarily “expensive” stocks; falling P/E with flat earnings signals rising rates/risk, not necessarily “cheap” stocks.
  6. Bubble/trough screen: compute the time-horizon duration for the index or a stock (see system sub-page); extreme readings (n > ~20 preceded 1987’s crash, n = 46.6 preceded the 1973-74 bear market) flag elevated correction risk.
  7. Quarterly Total Change rule: reallocate between equities and long bonds each quarter by the sign/magnitude of (prior-quarter %ΔEarnings − %ΔBond yield); see system sub-page for the full backtested rule.

Risk and money management

The book does not address position sizing, stops, or trade-level risk management - its “risk” concept is macro: the required-rate-of-return components that make up an individual stock’s discount rate. Practically, this means recognizing which risk factors are diversifiable (size, business, financial leverage, marketability) versus non-diversifiable (real rates, inflation, the general equity risk premium). The one backtested strategy (Chapter 8’s Total Change allocation) reports its principal risk as short-term quarter-to-quarter whipsaw rather than traditional beta/volatility risk, and notes transaction costs (0.25%-0.50%/year via low-cost funds) are modest enough not to erode its historical outperformance.

Psychology and discipline

Psychology is addressed only indirectly, through the observation that investors are systematically slow to recognize turning points: “many investors miss this quick, upward turn… because their perspectives remain anchored in the tail end of the most recent prior stage” - staying bearish into the early Stage I recovery. The book also warns against naively treating stocks as an inflation hedge (they hedge only when inflation-induced earnings growth exceeds the inflation-induced rate increase) and against assuming P/E expansion or contraction always reflects changed growth expectations rather than a changed discount rate. Beyond these framing points, there is no dedicated discussion of trading discipline or behavioral bias in the sense found in trading-psychology literature.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Introduction — dividend discount model logic and Table 1.1’s nine earnings/risk combinations that drive stock prices.
  • Ch 2 — Causal Valuation Factors — the Equation (3) framework in detail: expected earnings (E), the risk/discount-rate factor (R), nominal interest-rate components, and Fed policy’s role.
  • Ch 3 — Causal Valuation Factors Interaction — the core four-stage economic/stock-price cycle, with a visual history across US bull/bear markets from the 1970s-1990s.
  • Ch 4 — Portfolio Asset Allocation Implications — bond/stock linkage across stages, Fed policy’s stage-dependent impact, and historical case studies (1970s oil shocks, 1987 crash, 1990s bull market).
  • Ch 5 — Individual Stock Price Implications — the “build-up” discount-rate model for pricing individual stocks versus the diversified market.
  • Ch 6 — Industry Life Cycle — venture capital, rapid growth, mature, and stable/declining stages and their earnings-growth-versus-discount-rate balance.
  • Ch 7 — The Price/Earnings Multiple — derives P/E = 1/r, explains distortions for growth/cyclical/deficit-earnings companies, introduces the time-horizon-duration bubble measure.
  • Ch 8 — Empirical Studies — the author’s reprinted papers: the cycle regression model (R² ≈ 57%), the Total Change asset-allocation backtest (1967-1987), the bubble indicator applied to 1972-1987 extremes, a study of liquidity/beta, and a note on management depth’s effect on valuation.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s strength is a rigorous, internally consistent theoretical spine - a single dividend-discount equation explaining bull/bear cycles, sector rotation, the industry life cycle, and P/E behavior - backed by real, peer-reviewed empirical work with disclosed methodology and data sources, unusually transparent for a practitioner-facing book. Caveats: the empirical studies use data no more recent than the late 1990s (the asset-allocation backtest covers only 1967-1987), so none reflect the low-rate, post-2008, or post-2020 macro regimes; the Total Change model’s reported outperformance is a single historical backtest without meaningful out-of-sample validation, and an R² near 50-57% in the cycle-regression paper leaves roughly half of market movement unexplained. The framework also assumes investors can reliably observe consensus earnings and their rate of change in real time, harder in practice than the clean quarterly data used in the studies. Given the book predates near-zero-rate regimes, its assumption that rate cycles dominate the denominator warrants added skepticism there.

Who should read it

Investors and analysts who want a rigorous, theory-first explanation for why bull and bear markets happen, rather than a pattern-recognition description of them, and who are comfortable with dividend-discount-model algebra. Particularly useful for asset allocators and macro-oriented portfolio managers thinking about stock/bond rotation and sector positioning across the business cycle. Less suited to short-term traders, since the framework operates on a quarterly cadence with no chart-based or intraday application.

  • Investment Valuation — the standard reference for the discounted-cash-flow and multiples valuation mechanics that underlie Bolten’s Equation (3) framework.
  • The Intelligent Investor — foundational value-investing perspective on the same earnings/discount-rate relationship, at the individual-stock rather than cycle level.
  • Market Timing — alternative market-timing approach to compare against Bolten’s earnings/interest-rate cycle-stage diagnosis.
  • All About Market Timing — a broader survey of market-timing methods, useful context for evaluating the Total Change asset-allocation model’s place among timing strategies.
