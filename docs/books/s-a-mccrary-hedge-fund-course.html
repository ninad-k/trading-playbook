Summary

A course-book (with end-of-chapter questions and answers) covering the hedge fund industry from the inside: fund types and strategies, investor classes, business structures, leverage mechanics, performance and risk measurement, regulation, accounting, taxation, marketing, and derivative wrappers on hedge funds. It is not a trading system book; it is aimed at students, industry employees, and professionals who need to understand how funds are built and measured rather than how to trade.

Key points

  • Defines a hedge fund loosely as a lightly regulated pool charging incentive fees, seeking returns not highly correlated to stocks/bonds — no single legal test separates it from private equity or venture funds.
  • Surveys major strategies: long/short equity, global macro, merger arbitrage, distressed/bankruptcy debt, divestiture/spin-off plays, equity market-neutral pairs trading, and index arbitrage.
  • Standard fee structure: management fee of 1–2% of assets annually plus an incentive fee of 15–25% of profits, gated by a high-water mark (no incentive fee until prior losses are recovered).
  • Some funds add a look-back clawback provision (e.g., 3 months or one calendar quarter) requiring managers to refund incentive fees paid shortly before a loss.
  • Lockups of 1–3 years and limited redemption windows (month/quarter/year-end, with 10–90 days notice) are used to keep capital “sticky.”
  • Investor categories (accredited investor, qualified eligible participant, qualified purchaser) are defined by income/net-worth thresholds ($200k income or $1M net worth; $2M; $5M respectively) that gate legal access to funds.
  • Leverage is built through margin (Reg T initial margin, exchange maintenance margin), securities lending/short-selling, and futures (SPAN margin), each with different collateral mechanics.
  • Standard risk/performance tools: standard deviation of returns (annualized by multiplying by sqrt of periods/year), downside deviation/semivariance (loss-only volatility), and the Sharpe ratio (excess return over a benchmark divided by standard deviation).
  • Portfolio-level math is spelled out: weighted-average return, two-asset portfolio standard deviation from correlation/covariance, and worked after-tax return comparisons between hedge funds (taxed as ordinary income) and long-term stock holdings (taxed as capital gains).

Actionable rules

  1. Diversifying 10–20% of a portfolio into a low-correlation hedge fund can lower overall volatility even if the hedge fund’s own after-tax return is lower, per the book’s worked two-asset portfolio example.
  2. Annualize monthly standard deviation by multiplying by the square root of periods per year (e.g., monthly σ × √12) to compare risk across horizons.
  3. Track high-water marks and lockup/redemption terms before allocating; the incentive fee structure directly changes net investor returns.
  4. Use downside deviation/semivariance rather than plain standard deviation when returns are not symmetric, since it isolates loss-side risk.
  5. When comparing hedge fund and equity allocations after tax, model the different tax treatment explicitly (ordinary income vs. deferred long-term capital gains) — the book shows a hedge fund may need a materially higher pretax return to match a stock portfolio after tax.

Caveats

Written for industry practitioners and students, not retail traders — there are no entry/exit trading signals here. Some regulatory and tax figures (marginal rates, accredited-investor thresholds, margin rules) reflect mid-2000s U.S. law and have since changed. Math-heavy sections (equations for variance, Sharpe ratio, dollar-weighted return) assume comfort with basic statistics.

Who it is for

Readers preparing to work at, allocate to, or advise hedge funds — not swing/day traders looking for setups. Useful background for anyone evaluating a fund’s fee structure, leverage, or risk statistics.
