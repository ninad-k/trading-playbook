Overview

The 1998 sequel to The Compleat Day Trader, Volume I, continuing Bernstein’s futures-focused day-trading toolkit for the same audience. Volume II opens with a pointed “Observations, Claims, and Caveats” chapter (commissions eating marginal edges, broker fill practices, misinformation in commercially sold systems, futures-options myths), reviews the same family of timing indicators more briefly than Volume I, then introduces newer proprietary systems — most prominently the 30-Minute Breakout (30MBO) — alongside day-of-week seasonal patterns (including a Dual Breakout, or DBO, variant), the inside-day pattern, refined gap methods (including a multi-day “multigap” variant), an Accumulation/Distribution oscillator derivative, order-handling mechanics, and an expanded psychology close.

Core thesis

The same discipline-over-discretion thesis as Volume I, sharpened with more emphasis on realistic transaction-cost and slippage accounting, on choosing markets suited to day trading by volume and volatility rather than forcing a method onto an illiquid instrument, and on treating a chosen system as a closed contract with oneself — Bernstein explicitly warns against combining entry rules from one system with exit rules from another, and against building a pyramided position the wrong way round (adding smaller, not larger, size as a trade moves favorably).

Key concepts

  • 30-Minute Breakout (30MBO) — Bernstein’s newer breakout system, trading a market’s break of its first half-hour range by a market-specific number of ticks at the end of any subsequent half-hour period (full detail in the sub-page).
  • Dual Breakout (DBO) — a day-of-week seasonal breakout: buy on a stop a set number of ticks above the previous day’s high (or sell below the previous day’s low) only on days where a prior open/close relationship condition is met, exiting on the next profitable opening.
  • Inside day — a session whose high is below the prior day’s high and whose low is above the prior day’s low; used as a potential trend-change signal, traded by buying above the inside day’s high or selling below its low by a market-calibrated tick offset.
  • Multigap method — a gap-trading variant comparing gaps against a multi-day (not just prior-day) high/low window, exiting on the nth subsequent profitable opening rather than same-day.
  • Accumulation/Distribution Oscillator Derivative and Advance/Decline Derivative (ADD) — volume- and breadth-based derivative oscillators intended to time entries around institutional accumulation or distribution.
  • Range breakout system — a Keltner-type volatility-band breakout described as a day-trading variation on classic channel breakout methods.
  • MOC (market-on-close) — a standard session-end exit convention used across several of the book’s systems.
  • Pyramiding discipline — adding progressively smaller (not larger) size as a position moves favorably, to avoid a “top-heavy” position that a small adverse move can wipe out.

Rules and setups

  1. 30-Minute Breakout (30MBO) — full rule set in the [[bernstein-jake-the-compleat-day-trader-vol-ii–30-minute-breakout|30MBO sub-page]]: mark the first 30 minutes’ high/low, then buy or sell any subsequent half-hour close that exceeds that range by a market-specific number of ticks, with a stop-loss or reversing-signal exit and an end-of-day/MOC close.
  2. Dual Breakout (DBO), worked S&P example — trade only on the indicated day of the week; buy on a stop a set number of ticks above the previous day’s high if the previous close was above the previous open (mirror for sells). The book’s Monday S&P case study used long 3 ticks above Friday’s high or short 26 ticks below Friday’s low, a $3,400 stop, exiting on the first profitable opening. The general tick offset is stated to be market- and day-specific, not universal — only this one worked example survives in the sample.
  3. Inside-day breakout — after an inside day, buy at its high plus a tick offset, or sell at its low minus a tick offset; the offset must be large enough to avoid whipsaws and small enough to give sufficient trades, but no specific value is given, and a separate risk-management stop is called for.
  4. Gap methods — basic signals mirror Volume I (enter on a penetration back through the prior day’s extreme by a given number of ticks, exit at a fixed-dollar stop, a stop beyond the day’s extreme, or MOC); the multigap variant used a 9-day lookback for buy signals and 2-day for sell signals in S&P testing, averaging about $482 per trade same-day or $780 held to the next profitable opening (after slippage/commission).
  5. S&P stop-sizing caution — even a 500-point ($1,250) stop can be insufficient given S&P’s roughly 600-point average daily range; undersized stops are cited as a primary reason many S&P day traders lose.
  6. Pyramiding rule — if a position is built up at all, add successively smaller numbers of units, never larger ones; Bernstein calls the conventional escalating pyramid “clearly built upside down,” heaviest at the top and liable to violent collapse on the first reversal. No specific size sequence is given in the text.
  7. Order-handling discipline — insist on prompt fill confirmations (especially in currencies, T-bonds, S&P, petroleum), know which order types an exchange accepts, and treat the broker as an employee executing decisions, not a source of opinion.

Risk and money management

As in Volume I, risk rules are system-specific: the 30MBO uses either a predetermined (fail-safe) stop or the opposite signal as a reversing exit, plus an optional trailing stop once a profit objective is reached; the DBO worked example carries an explicit $3,400 stop; the inside-day breakout calls for “a risk management stop loss” without a specified value. The book’s clearest general risk lesson is the S&P stop-sizing caution above: a stop must be sized to the instrument’s actual average range, not an arbitrarily small, comfortable-looking figure. No percent-of-equity sizing formula is given; sizing decisions (including the pyramiding sequence) stay qualitative rather than numeric.

Psychology and discipline

The psychology chapters expand on Volume I with more material on broker relationships (be assertive; the broker executes your decisions and should not talk you into or out of a trade), self-control specifically around overtrading, and the same trading-diary and daily-goal discipline from Volume I. A closing “Frequently Asked Questions”-style section addresses two common discipline failures directly: combining entry rules from one system with exit rules from another (discouraged — trade one tested system’s rules “to the exclusion of all else”), and pyramiding incorrectly (discouraged unless sized in a shrinking, not growing, sequence as described above).

Chapter map

  • Ch 1-3 — Perspective on Day Trading; A Review of Market Timing Indicators; Day Trading: Art or Science: realistic expectations; condensed indicator survey (MA, stochastics/RSI, patterns, Parabolic, ADX/DMI, cycles, seasonals, sentiment); why most systems fail, S&P fill/commission problems.
  • Ch 4-5 — The 30-Minute Breakout; Day of the Week Patterns: the book’s flagship system (this book’s system page); key-date seasonals and the Dual Breakout (DBO) variant.
  • Ch 6-7 — Trading Systems: Pros and Cons; Power of the Inside Day: trader archetypes; the inside-day pattern and its breakout rules.
  • Ch 8-9 — System Testing and Optimization; Emotions, Traders, and Markets: overfitting risks; common ways traders lose money and countermeasures.
  • Ch 10-11 — What Markets to Day Trade?; Closings and Openings: volume/volatility/spread candidates; high/low/close and open/close relationships as system building blocks.
  • Ch 12-13 — Gap Methods for Day Trading; Accumulation Distribution Oscillator Derivative: basic and multigap methods; the A/D and Advance/Decline Derivative (ADD) oscillators.
  • Ch 14-15 — The Importance of Orders; The Psychology of Day Trading: order-type and broker mechanics; temperament, self-control, discipline.
  • Ch 16-17 — The Day Trader’s Rules for Success; FAQ-style close: orientation, trading frequency, pyramiding guidance; combining-systems Q&A.

Strengths and caveats

Volume II is notably more self-critical than a typical trading manual, devoting an entire opening chapter to why most commercially sold systems fail and to the specific mechanics (fills, commissions, undersized stops) that erode S&P day-trading edges. Its flagship 30MBO system is presented with a complete entry/exit/stop framework, but the tick threshold that triggers entry is explicitly market-specific and “discussed later in the chapter” — that figure did not survive the sampled OCR pages, so it must be treated as a parameter to calibrate per instrument, not a fixed constant. Other systems (DBO, inside-day breakout) similarly rely on an unspecified “x ticks” parameter, with only the DBO’s single S&P worked example (77 percent win rate per its Omega Research performance table) giving a concrete number — one historical backtest record, not an out-of-sample validated result. As with Volume I, examples and tick/dollar figures are calibrated to 1990s futures contracts and need translation to a stock or forex trader’s own instrument.

Who should read it

Futures and other short-term traders who have read or are familiar with Volume I and want its newer, generally better-specified breakout systems (especially the 30MBO), plus a sharper skeptical framing of why most day-trading systems and claims don’t hold up. Less useful as a standalone starting point — several chapters assume familiarity with concepts (MAC, CTOD, basic order types) introduced at greater length in Volume I — or for a reader who wants every system’s parameters fully pinned down rather than partially left to per-instrument calibration.

Related books in this library

  • The Compleat Day Trader, Volume I — the first volume, covering the indicator toolkit and systems (MAC, CTOD, gap trading) this book builds on and periodically references.
  • Stock Market Strategies That Work — Bernstein’s stock-market companion, sharing the discipline-first framing and the MAC concept in a slower-timeframe context.
  • The Original Turtle Trading Rules — a contrasting, fully parameterized mechanical breakout system with explicit position sizing, useful for comparison against this book’s partially-specified breakout methods.
  • Way of the Turtle — a more rigorously documented, statistically validated breakout/trend-following system in the same broad family as the 30MBO and DBO.
