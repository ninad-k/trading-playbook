Overview

Michael Maiello, a financial journalist, takes 85 well-known market sayings — “cut your losses and let your profits run,” “don’t fight the Fed,” “sell in May and go away,” “the market falls when the Mets win the World Series” — and examines each on its merits: where it came from, what evidence supports or contradicts it, and how a modern investor should actually use (or ignore) it. The book is organized into eight parts: general beliefs about markets, stock-picking folklore, Federal Reserve-watching rules, “smart money” indicators, calendar/seasonal effects, superstitions, macro/political claims, and common misunderstandings about investment safety. Each maxim gets a self-contained 2–4 page treatment rather than building toward one unified system.

Core thesis

Popular market maxims are compressed folk wisdom — some are genuinely evidence-backed risk-management rules, some are outdated relics of a different market structure, and some are pure superstition dressed up as pattern recognition. The book’s throughline is that a maxim’s usefulness depends entirely on the mechanism behind it: a rule with an identifiable causal or structural reason (transaction costs favoring buy-and-hold, index concentration effects, the mechanics of a short squeeze) deserves to be taken seriously; a rule that’s just a coincidence dressed in a catchy rhyme (Super Bowl indicator, hemline theory) should be dismissed regardless of its historical hit rate. Verifying a maxim means asking what data supports it, whether the sample period was cherry-picked, and whether the underlying market structure it describes still holds today.

Key concepts

  • Averaging down — buying more of a stock as it falls to lower the average cost basis; the book treats it as a value-investing tool that only works if the investor has done enough independent work to be confident the market, not the position, is wrong.
  • The Rule of 20 — an inflation-adjusted P/E heuristic: if the market’s P/E plus the annualized inflation rate is under 20, the market is considered fairly valued; over 20 signals overvaluation. Presented with historical backtest results but flagged as having failed during the late-1990s bull run.
  • Volatility skew via short squeeze — the mechanism by which too many short sellers covering at once drives the price up further against them, used to explain why the book advises against shorting small, illiquid stocks.
  • The Greenspan Put — market belief that the Federal Reserve will cut rates to rescue falling markets, creating a perceived floor under equity prices.
  • Two Tumbles and a Jump / Three Steps and a Stumble — informal rules linking consecutive Fed rate moves (cuts or hikes) to subsequent market direction.
  • The January Effect — the tendency for small-cap and previously depressed stocks to outperform in early January, historically linked to tax-loss selling in December reversing.
  • Reverse merger — a shell-company mechanism penny stocks use to go public without an IPO, cited as a reason penny stocks carry elevated manipulation risk.
  • Dogs of the Dow — a mechanical strategy of buying the ten highest-dividend-yield (lowest-priced-relative-to-yield) Dow stocks each year, presented as one of the few “beat the market” folk strategies with real backing.
  • Reward/risk asymmetry in shorting — a long position’s maximum loss is capped at 100% of capital, while a short position’s loss is theoretically unlimited, framed as the core reason short sellers need tighter risk control than long-only investors.

Rules and setups

This is a maxims-explanation book, not a trading system, so it gives no single strategy with entry/exit rules; the concrete, numbered guidance that does appear:

  1. Stop-loss on short positions. Set a predefined maximum loss percentage before entering a short sale (the book cites investors comfortable with anywhere from 3% to 10%) and exit automatically when hit — calibrated so that, in percentage terms, losses are never allowed to exceed wins across the portfolio.
  2. Diversification threshold. Concentrated single-stock wealth (the Bill Gates/Warren Buffett pattern) is achievable mainly for founders or already-wealthy investors; ordinary investors are better served holding a diversified portfolio monitored and rebalanced over years/decades.
  3. Avoid penny stocks / low market cap floor. Stick to listed stocks with market caps above roughly $100 million to avoid the manipulation and illiquidity risk concentrated in sub-$10 shares.
  4. Avoid shorting small stocks. As a practical floor, the book cites Forbes’ own rule of never recommending a short sale on a stock with a market cap below $400 million, due to short-squeeze risk in illiquid names.
  5. Averaging-down cost check. Because every additional purchase incurs a commission, small investors averaging down in fixed dollar increments (e.g., $100/week) should calculate the cumulative fee drag (the book’s example: $60 in fees over a month on $400 invested is a 12% headwind) before assuming the tactic is cost-free.
  6. Rule of 20 as a valuation gauge, not a timing trigger — inflation-adjusted P/E above 20 historically preceded weaker returns, but the book documents a multi-year period (1995–2003) where the market stayed elevated well past the “sell” signal, so it should inform position sizing/caution rather than trigger hard exits.

Risk and money management

Risk management themes are woven through individual maxims rather than presented as a unified framework: the stop-loss chapter is the most explicit, framing a stop as a pre-committed, mechanical decision rule that removes the temptation to “wait and be proven right” on a losing position — particularly important for short sellers, who face structurally unlimited downside versus a long investor’s capped-at-zero downside. The diversification-themed maxims argue that concentration risk (single-stock wealth) should be actively reduced once created, even after a successful concentrated bet, because the same volatility that built the wealth can erode it. Commission/fee drag is treated as a real, quantifiable risk to strategies like averaging down, especially for small accounts trading frequently.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s psychological content is mostly implicit in how it frames investor behavior: the tendency to hold losers “to collect the dividends” or avoid admitting a mistake, the urge to check portfolio value obsessively (the “never check prices on Friday” maxim), the emotional pull of a home-run stock story (survivorship bias in rags-to-riches narratives), and herd behavior around market folklore (Super Bowl indicator, hemline theory) that persists purely because pattern-seeking minds find coincidental correlations satisfying. The recurring corrective is to ask for a causal mechanism before acting on a rule, rather than trusting a catchy phrase or a short back-tested track record.

Chapter map

  • Part 1 — Beliefs from the Street: market-timing, trend, and efficiency maxims (beat-the-market, cut losses/let profits run, sell down to the sleeping point, trend is your friend, random walk, market efficiency, buy the dips, buy the rumor/sell the fact, buy and hold).
  • Part 2 — Stock Picking: individual-stock folklore (follow a few stocks well, never fall in love with a stock, catching a falling knife, share buybacks, Dogs of the Dow, stock splits, penny stocks, shorting small stocks, stop-losses when shorting).
  • Part 3 — The Federal Reserve: Fed-driven market rules (don’t fight the Fed, the Greenspan Put, Two Tumbles and a Jump, Three Steps and a Stumble).
  • Part 4 — The Smart Money Talks: following institutional/analyst signals (actively managed funds in down markets, fund manager tracking, analyst price targets, “smart money,” economist forecasts).
  • Part 5 — It’s That Time of Year: calendar effects (January effect, first-days-of-month profit-taking, “as January goes,” sell in May, St. Patty’s Day, October surprise, Santa Claus rally).
  • Part 6 — People Believe This Stuff?: pure superstition (Super Bowl indicator, Mets World Series effect, Triple Crown effect, cocktail shrimp theory, hemline theory).
  • Part 7 — The Economy and Politics: macro claims (baby boomer retirement, stock market as leading indicator, tax cuts ending recessions, wars feeding bull markets).
  • Part 8 — A Few Misunderstandings: safety myths (investment insurance limits, CD safety, tax-free account use, hedge fund sophistication, SEC investor protections, risk/return relationship).

Strengths and caveats

The book’s strength is its evidence-first method: most chapters cite specific studies, historical return data, or named analysts rather than asserting a maxim is true or false by fiat, which makes it more useful as a critical-thinking exercise than as a rulebook. Its weakness is scope — at ~230 pages covering 85 topics, no single maxim gets more than a few pages of treatment, so the data cited (a single academic paper, a handful of named funds) is illustrative rather than rigorous, and some conclusions (Rule of 20, January Effect) were already being complicated by market behavior at the time of writing (2004), a limitation the author himself flags in a few chapters. Some seasonal/Fed-based “rules” (Two Tumbles and a Jump, Three Steps and a Stumble) are presented with less scrutiny than the book’s own stated standard of evidence. The book is now two decades old; its “smart money” and Fed sections predate the 2008 financial crisis and post-2008 unconventional monetary policy, so conclusions about Fed behavior and rate-cycle patterns should be treated as describing an earlier regime.

Who should read it

Best suited to a beginner-to-intermediate investor who has already absorbed a stack of market clichés from financial media and wants a sober, mostly data-informed gut check on which ones deserve to influence real decisions. Less useful for an experienced trader seeking a tradable system, since the book explicitly stops short of prescribing one for almost every topic it covers.
