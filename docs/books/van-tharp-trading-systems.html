What Is a Trading System?

Van K. Tharp · 2005 · Tier B — concise note article intermediate

Defines the 8 components of a trading strategy and 8 sections of a full trading business plan, plus a system-development framework from an Army systems-design interview.

Summary

Van Tharp’s article distinguishes what most traders call a “trading system” (really a trading strategy) from the broader trading system, which includes the strategy plus the trader’s full business plan. Using Robert Kiyosaki’s employee/self-employed/business-owner/investor quadrant as a lens on how people relate to systems, Tharp argues that durable profitability requires designing simple, repeatable systems the way a business owner designs a franchise, not the way an employee or self-employed person just does the work. The article then lays out the 8 components of a trading strategy, the 8 sections of a trading business plan, and closes with an interview (Ken Long, U.S. Army) on general principles of system design and calibration.

Key points

  • Kiyosaki’s 4 quadrants applied to trading: employees ask “what should I buy,” self-employed traders over-complexify a discretionary system they can’t see because they’re inside it, business owners design simple repeatable systems, investors evaluate rate of return on capital deployed.
  • A trading strategy (what most people call a “system”) has 8 parts: market filter, set-up conditions, entry signal, worst-case stop loss, re-entry rule, profit-taking exit, position-sizing algorithm, and often multiple systems for different market regimes.
  • The protective stop defines “1R” (initial risk); Tharp: entering without a stop is “like driving through town ignoring red lights.”
  • Position sizing example: on a $25,000 account risking 1% ($250) per trade, buying a $10 stock with a 25% trailing stop ($2.50/share risk) sizes to 100 shares ($1,000 position, 4x the dollar risk).
  • The full trading business plan has 8 sections: executive summary, business description, industry/competition overview, self-knowledge, the tactical trading plan, a listing of the trader’s specific edges, financial information, and worst-case contingency planning.
  • Ken Long’s system-design principles: define who you are (individual vs. fund vs. trust) before designing; keep objectives explicit; calibrate periodically by comparing actual trading expectancy to the system’s calculated expectancy (borrowed from “Statistical Process Control”).
  • A deviation of actual results beyond 1-2 standard deviations from expected expectancy is proposed as a trigger to stop trading and recalibrate.

Actionable rules

  1. Never trade without a worst-case protective stop; this defines your 1R initial risk.
  2. Size positions by dividing the fixed dollar risk (e.g., 1% of equity) by the per-share/per-unit risk (entry minus stop) to get position size.
  3. Build explicit re-entry criteria for when a stopped-out position reverses back in your original favored direction.
  4. Document a market filter that identifies whether current conditions (quiet/volatile, trending/flat) suit your system before trading it.
  5. Track actual trading expectancy against the system’s back-tested/calculated expectancy; treat a 1-2 standard deviation divergence as a signal to stop and recalibrate.

Caveats

The article is conceptual/organizational rather than a specific tradable method — it defines the categories of rules a system needs but supplies no concrete entry/exit signals, timeframe, or asset class. The 1% risk and position-sizing example are illustrative, not a universal prescription; readers are pointed to Tharp’s other materials (Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom, Money Management Report) for depth.

Who it is for

Traders who already have a strategy idea but lack the discipline of a written business plan, and anyone designing or auditing a systematic (discretionary or mechanical) trading operation from scratch.
