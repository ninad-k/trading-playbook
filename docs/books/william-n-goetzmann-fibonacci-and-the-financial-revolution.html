Summary

A Yale School of Management working paper (Goetzmann, October 2003) on the history of financial mathematics, not on the Fibonacci retracement/extension tool traders use today. Its thesis: Leonardo of Pisa (“Fibonacci”), in his 1202 mathematics text Liber Abaci, developed the first known systematic present-value method for comparing cash flow streams of different timing and structure — a discovery the paper argues was more consequential for the development of Western capital markets than his famous number sequence, which appears only as a minor example late in the book’s twelfth chapter.

Key points

  • Liber Abaci (1202) is overwhelmingly a book of commercial mathematics — multiplication, currency conversion, merchant profit-sharing, interest and present-value problems — with the Fibonacci sequence appearing as a single incidental example.
  • Fibonacci’s most sophisticated worked problem (“A Soldier Receiving Three Hundred Bezants for His Fief”) explicitly compares the value of two different annuity payment schedules by discounting each future cash flow back using a compounding ratio — functionally a present-value calculation, roughly 700 years before Irving Fisher’s 1930 formalization of the modern present-value formula.
  • Fibonacci solved multi-period compounding/discounting problems (e.g., finding an unknown number of compounding periods) by brute-force iteration, since logarithms had not yet been invented.
  • Historical bills of exchange (an early European remittance/discount instrument) date to around the same period — Ashtor documents one from 1220; Sivéry identifies an Italian discount contract from 1252 with an implied interest rate of roughly 11%.
  • Liber Abaci was written just before the Church’s 13th-century crackdown on usury intensified (Franciscan and Dominican orders founded 1206 and 1216); its interest-rate worked examples predate that intensified doctrinal conflict.
  • Venice issued a mandatory 5% forced loan to its wealthy citizens in 1171 to fund a fleet against Byzantium; the resulting and successor loans (“prestiti”) developed into a regular secondary market for government debt by the mid-13th century, later consolidated in 1262 — an early form of sovereign/municipal debt market roughly contemporary with Fibonacci.
  • Paper situates the argument within the “Great Divergence” debate (why Europe, not more technologically advanced China, developed modern capitalism first), suggesting mathematically-enabled present-value analysis was a contributing factor unique to Europe’s fragmented, internationally-trading city-state environment.
  • Traces Liber Abaci’s influence forward to 16th-century French mathematician Jean Trenchant, whose 1558 L’Arithmetique poses and solves interest-rate/present-value problems using the same underlying logic, scaled to percentage terms for easier comparison.

Actionable rules

None given, and none intended — this is a financial-history paper about the intellectual origins of present-value analysis, not a trading method. No entry, exit, sizing, or Fibonacci-ratio trading rule is discussed anywhere in the paper.

Caveats

This paper has no connection to the Fibonacci retracement/extension levels (23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) used in technical analysis — it is exclusively about the historical origin of present-value/discounting mathematics. Readers looking for Fibonacci-ratio trading techniques should look elsewhere in this library; this document is purely historical/academic and belongs with financial-history rather than technical-analysis material. It is a working paper (author explicitly requests “for comments only, do not quote without permission” on the cover), so some claims may have been refined in later published versions.

Who it is for

Readers interested in the intellectual history of quantitative finance and where present-value thinking originated — not traders looking for a Fibonacci-based trading technique.
