The Wyckoff Method of Trading Stocks

Various · 1987 · Tier A system intermediate

Richard D. Wyckoff's price/volume method for reading accumulation and distribution and timing entries at the lowest-risk point in a turn.

What it is

A discretionary, chart-based method (originated by Richard D. Wyckoff around 1908, expounded here as a 15-part series by Jack K. Hutson, S&C Volume 5) for reading the balance of supply and demand in a stock, group, or index from price and volume alone, and for timing entries at the point where risk is smallest relative to the expected move. It assumes all price action is the visible footprint of a “composite operator” — an aggregate of large, informed traders — running a four-phase campaign: accumulation, markup, distribution, markdown. The trader’s job is to identify which phase a security is in and act with the operator rather than against them.

Rules

Chart tools. The method uses three chart types in combination: a Vertical (bar) chart of price and volume, a Figure (point & figure) chart of price alone, and — for advanced practitioners — a Wave Chart plotting the intraday aggregate price of five leading stocks versus time to detect turning points ahead of the composite averages.

Position Sheet. Track candidate stocks by classifying each into one of five positions based on its chart behavior relative to the general market: Position 1 (reaction — pulling back on light volume while holding support) and Position 2 (rally — breaking a supply line on rising volume) are the most important buy signals; Positions 3 and 4 are their mirror images for shorts. Recompute weekly; concentrate on stocks in Position 2 or 4 (rally/decline already confirmed) for the best risk/reward.

Recognizing a bottom (mirror-image for tops). The classic sequence is: 1. Selling Climax — abnormally high volume, wide range, close near the low, after a sharp decline with no corrective rallies (an “oversold” condition). 2. Technical Rally (Automatic Rally) — volume dips, price range jumps; weak-handed climax buyers unload. 3. Secondary Reaction (Test) — price pulls back; if it holds at or above the climax low on shrinking volume, the low is likely to hold (bullish); if it undercuts the climax low, expect a new decline. 4. Springboard — price and volume compress into a narrow “hinge”; this is the lowest-risk entry point, just before the move.

Buying tests (summarized; selling tests are the mirror image). Look for, in combination: (1) a Figure Chart downside price objective (built from the count of a horizontal congestion formation) being met; (2) increasing volume on rallies versus decreasing volume on reactions; (3) a “line” of preliminary support forming with volume expansion; (4) the stock outperforming a weak general market (higher lows while the average makes new lows); (5) the stock breaking its own down-sloping supply line on a rally; (6)-(9) higher supports and higher tops confirming a new uptrend is underway. A shakeout (a sharp plunge that snaps stops and scares out weak holders, then reverses) and a thrust (the mirror-image sharp spike through resistance that fails to hold) are both treated as tests to be recognized, not entry triggers by themselves.

Entry. Buy (or sell short) at the Position 1/Springboard stage, i.e., after the Selling Climax and Technical Rally, when the Secondary Reaction holds above the climax low on light volume. A purchase made only after price exceeds the Technical Rally’s high is explicitly called the “third and least favorable” entry, since it occurs mid-wave rather than at a turn.

Stops. Place every stop at a “danger point” derived from chart structure, not an arbitrary distance: 1-5 points beyond a support/resistance level, a 50% retracement mark, or a clearly drawn support/supply trendline (fewer points for higher-priced stocks, more for low-priced ones — roughly 3-5 points on high-priced issues, 2-3 on moderately priced ones, 1-2 on issues under $50). Use odd fractions (e.g., 47-7/8 rather than 48) since stop orders cluster at round numbers where operators are likelier to trigger them deliberately. Move the stop to breakeven (plus 1/2-3/4 point for commissions) as soon as possible without added risk, then crowd it progressively closer to market price as the position approaches its Figure Chart objective — “don’t hold out for the last point.”

Risk

Commit to a trade only when probable profit is at least 3 times the dollar risk to the stop; this reward:risk floor is treated as non-negotiable. Use a stop on every single transaction, long or short, investment or trade, decided before the order is placed. Do not commit all capital to one idea; venture only a fraction of capital initially, especially as a new position, and add (pyramid) only when the setup shows potential for a further 10-15 point move — never to average down. A normal reaction retraces about half of the preceding rally (and vice versa); a retracement deeper than half signals technical weakness and reduced conviction to hold or add. If stops are being triggered unusually often, Wyckoff’s diagnosis is one of three causes: entering too early (before conclusive tests are met), trading against the prevailing trend, or misplacing/mismanaging the stop itself — the prescribed response is to stop trading entirely until the cause is identified, not to remove the stops.

Caveats

This is a fully discretionary, pattern-recognition method with no coded rules for objective backtesting; “the operator’s footprint” is an interpretive frame, and two Wyckoff analysts can read the same chart differently, particularly around shakeouts versus genuine breakdowns. The magazine’s own author cautions against cherry-picking pieces of the method (e.g., just the stop rules) since it is designed as an integrated system of chart reading plus discipline. The stop-distance table (points-from-danger-point by price level) reflects 1980s-and-earlier stock prices in whole points and fractions and needs rescaling (e.g., as a percentage of price or ATR) for modern decimalized, often much higher-priced or much lower-priced instruments. No explicit guidance is given on modern index/ETF or futures application, though the underlying accumulation/distribution logic has been widely adapted by later Wyckoff-influenced traders.
