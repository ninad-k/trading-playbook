Overview

Kathy Lien, a former J.P. Morgan/Chase interbank FX dealer, wrote this book as a practitioner’s guide to trading the spot currency market intraday. The first half (Chapters 1–6) covers FX market structure, history, the differences between FX and equities/futures, what moves currencies in the long and short term, best trading times by session, and currency correlations. The second half (Chapters 7–9) is the actionable core: a trading-journal and market-classification framework (Chapter 7), seven fully specified technical day-trading strategies with numeric entry/exit rules (Chapter 8), and two fundamental strategies — pairing selection and the leveraged carry trade (Chapter 9). Written in 2006 using pip-denominated examples across major pairs (EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, USD/CAD, etc.) from 2003–2005 price data.

Core thesis

Consistent FX day trading is not about finding a “holy grail” indicator but about (1) classifying the current market regime as trending or range-bound before choosing a strategy, (2) applying pre-defined, mechanical entry/stop/exit rules matched to that regime, and (3) enforcing risk discipline (fixed risk-reward ratios, per-trade risk caps, stop-loss and trailing-stop rules) and psychological discipline (a trading journal, emotional detachment) uniformly across every trade. Currency-specific edges come from understanding FX market microstructure (order-flow clustering around round numbers, interbank stop-hunting patterns) and macro capital flows (interest-rate differentials, risk aversion cycles) that don’t have direct analogues in equities.

Key concepts

  • Trending vs. range-bound classification — the first step before any trade: ADX above 25 (trending) or below 20 (ranging), confirmed by Bollinger band width, moving-average posture, and FX options risk reversals.
  • Risk reversals — a call/put pair on the same currency and expiration, quoted by their implied-volatility difference; used as a sentiment/positioning gauge (near-zero = indecision/range; strongly one-sided = trending bias).
  • Perfect order — moving averages (10/20/50/100/200-day SMA) stacked in strict sequential order, signaling a strong trend with multiple layers of dynamic support/resistance.
  • Double zeros / round numbers — price levels ending in .00 (or .000 for “triple zeros”) where take-profit and stop orders cluster, creating exploitable intraday bounce/reversal zones.
  • Inside day — a session whose high and low fall entirely within the prior session’s range; consecutive inside days signal a coming volatility expansion (breakout).
  • Multiple time frame analysis — use a higher time frame (daily) to establish trend direction/bias, then a lower time frame (hourly/15-min) to time entries in that direction.
  • ADX (Average Directional Index) — the book’s primary trend-strength filter; >25 (ideally rising) = trending, <20–25 (ideally falling) = ranging.
  • Carry trade — going long a high-interest-rate currency and short a low-interest-rate currency to earn the rate differential, amplified with leverage; profitability is driven by aggregate investor risk aversion, not just the rate spread.
  • Risk aversion cycle — when investors are risk-seeking, capital flows into high-yield currencies (carry trades work); when risk aversion spikes (crises), capital flees to low-yield “safe haven” currencies and carry trades unwind sharply.
  • Trading journal (3-part) — a daily currency-pair checklist, a “trades I am waiting for” watch list with pre-set entries/stops/targets, and a log of completed trades with rationale and lessons.

Rules and setups

Lien specifies seven named, fully mechanical strategies (see linked system pages for complete rule sets and worked examples):

  1. Fading the Double Zeros — counter-trend scalp off round-number order clusters on 10–15 min charts; ~15-pip entries, ~20-pip stops, scale out at 2R with a trailing stop on the remainder. Fading the Double Zeros
  2. Waiting for the Real Deal — fades the initial GBP/USD move at the London open (a stop-hunt), entering only after the early range is broken in the opposite direction. Waiting for the Real Deal
  3. Inside Day Breakout Play — breakout entries 10 pips beyond the high/low of the most recent of 2+ consecutive inside days, with a stop-and-reverse order on the other side. Inside Day Breakout Play
  4. The Fader — a variant of “Waiting for the Real Deal” using ADX<35 and daily-range breaks (15 pips) to fade apparent breakouts on the hourly chart. The Fader
  5. Filtering False Breakouts — buys/sells a 20-day high/low re-break after a 2-day pullback, designed to catch trend resumption after weak hands are shaken out. Filtering False Breakouts
  6. Channel Strategy — trades breakouts from a narrow, parallel-line-defined trading channel, entering 10 pips beyond either channel boundary. Channel Strategy
  7. Perfect Order — trend-following entry five candles after a sequential moving-average stack forms, holding until the stack breaks. Perfect Order
  8. Leveraged Carry Trade — buy the higher-yielding currency, sell the lower-yielding currency, sized with leverage, entered when aggregate investor risk aversion is low/falling. Leveraged Carry Trade

General execution rules that recur across strategies: pip-based fixed initial stops (typically 10–30 pips depending on strategy and timeframe), a “close half at 2x risk, move stop to breakeven, trail the rest” scaling pattern, and preference for entering in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend rather than picking tops/bottoms.

Risk and money management

Lien recommends a minimum 1:2 (stated elsewhere as 2:1 reward-to-risk) ratio on every trade, and caps risk at no more than 2% of account equity per single trade. Ten rules she says all traders (forex, equities, or futures) should live by: limit losses; let profits run; keep position sizes reasonable; know your risk-reward ratio before entering; be adequately capitalized; don’t fight the trend; never add to losing positions; know market expectations (economic calendar); keep a trading journal to learn from mistakes; and set a maximum loss or profit-retracement limit. Two stop-placement methods are given explicitly: the “two-day low/high method” (initial stop ~10 pips beyond the two-day low/high) and the parabolic SAR as a dynamic trailing-stop indicator. A standard scaling pattern — take partial profit at a 1:1 gain (double the amount risked), move the stop on the remainder to breakeven, then trail — appears throughout the named strategies.

Psychology and discipline

Central to the book is “emotional detachment”: traders should be fully engaged in analysis but not emotionally attached to open positions, basing every decision on pre-defined strategy rather than fear or greed. Lien specifically warns against the common failure mode of taking profits too early while letting losses run (producing more winning trades than losing ones yet a net loss), and recommends stepping away from the screen during a losing streak rather than trading through it. The three-part trading journal (checklist, watch list, trade log with post-mortem comments) is presented as the single most important habit for building this discipline, modeled on the accountability structure used on interbank trading desks.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Foreign Exchange: The Fastest-Growing Market — FX vs. equities/futures, market participant hierarchy.
  • Ch 2 — Historical Events in the FX Market — Bretton Woods, the Plaza Accord, Soros/ERM, the Asian financial crisis, the euro’s introduction.
  • Ch 3 — What Moves the Currency Market Long Term — capital/trade flows, purchasing power parity, interest rate parity, monetary and asset-market models.
  • Ch 4 — What Moves the Currency Market Short Term — the changing relative importance of economic data releases.
  • Ch 5 — Best Times to Trade Individual Pairs — Asian/U.S./European sessions and their overlaps.
  • Ch 6 — Currency Correlations — how to calculate and use pair correlations, and why they change over time.
  • Ch 7 — Trade Parameters for Different Market Conditions — trading journal design, trend/range classification (ADX, Bollinger bands, risk reversals), trade-horizon rules, risk management, psychology.
  • Ch 8 — Technical Trading Strategies — the seven named setups (double zeros, waiting for the real deal, inside day breakout, the fader, filtering false breakouts, channel strategy, perfect order).
  • Ch 9 — Fundamental Trading Strategies — picking the strongest currency pairing, the leveraged carry trade.

Strengths and caveats

Strong point: nearly every strategy is given as an explicit, numbered, testable rule set with real historical trade examples (dates, entry/stop/exit prices, pip results), making this one of the more directly codeable forex day-trading books in the library. Caveats: examples are drawn from a narrow 2003–2005 window and, being interbank-informed anecdotes rather than a statistically audited backtest, the reported win rates and pip totals should be treated as illustrative, not as expected performance. Some microstructure claims (e.g., the mechanics of “gunning stops” around round numbers, U.K. dealer stop-hunting at the London open) reflect the more fragmented, voice/quote-driven interbank market structure of the mid-2000s and may be less reliable in the more electronic, algorithmically-arbitraged FX market of later years. The book does not address modern ECN/prime-brokerage execution, negative-carry environments, or the zero/negative interest-rate periods that followed 2008, which materially affects the carry-trade chapter’s applicability.

Who should read it

Retail or aspiring professional FX day traders who want concrete, rule-based intraday setups rather than general market commentary, and who are comfortable pairing technical entries with a basic macro/rate-differential overlay. Useful secondary reading for anyone wanting a plain-English primer on FX market structure and carry trades even without adopting the specific day-trading rules.
