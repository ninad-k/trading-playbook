Elliott Wave Rules and Guidelines

A.J. Frost and Robert R. Prechter, Jr. · 1978 · Tier A — deep note system advanced

The codeable core of Elliott Wave analysis: the three inviolable wave rules, the alternation and channeling guidelines, and the Fibonacci ratios used to project wave targets.

What it is

A structural pattern-recognition method for labeling price action into a nested hierarchy of motive (trend-following, 5-subwave) and corrective (counter-trend, 3-subwave) waves, then using precise rules and Fibonacci-ratio guidelines to validate the labeling and project the likely extent of the next wave. It is not a standalone entry/exit system with position sizing; it is a market-structure framework that other methods (stops, sizing) must be layered onto.

Rules

A wave count is invalid if any of these three rules is broken. Applied to an impulse (5-wave motive structure) trending in either direction:

  1. Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1. If price moves back beyond the start of wave 1, the count is wrong.
  2. Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3, and 5 (measured in price, and almost always in percentage/semilog terms too). It need not be the longest — but it cannot be the shortest of the three.
  3. Wave 4 never enters the price territory of wave 1 (no overlap) in non-leveraged cash markets. The sole exception is a diagonal triangle, where wave 4 typically does overlap wave 1; brief overlap can also occur in highly leveraged futures markets.

If a labeling violates any of these, relabel: the most common fix is to treat the apparent wave 3 as still-unfolding (an extension in progress) rather than accept a rule violation.

Corrective waves are never fives. An initial five-wave move against the larger trend is only ever part of a correction, never the whole of it — this is the single most important corrective-wave rule and prevents mislabeling a counter-trend impulse as a completed reversal.

Pattern construction rules by type: - Impulse: 5 subwaves, no wave-1/wave-4 overlap, wave 3 not shortest, only one of waves 1/3/5 is extended. - Diagonal triangle (leading or ending): 5 subwaves each internally a “three” (except a leading diagonal’s waves 1 and 4, which subdivide as fives); converging (or, rarely, diverging) trendlines; wave 4 overlaps wave 1. - Zigzag: 5-3-5 subwave count (A-B-C); wave B does not retrace all of wave A. - Flat: 3-3-5 subwave count; regular (B ≈ start of A, C slightly beyond A), expanded (B beyond start of A, C well beyond A), or running (B beyond start of A, but C falls short of A’s end). - Triangle: 3-3-3-3-3 subwave count (a-b-c-d-e); contracting (symmetrical, ascending, descending) or expanding; occurs only prior to the final actionary wave of the next-larger pattern (e.g., wave 4, wave B, or the final wave of a combination).

Risk

The Wave Principle carries no built-in stop-loss, position-sizing, or capital-allocation rule — it is purely a structural/forecasting layer. Its risk-control mechanism is methodological, not monetary: maintain a preferred wave count and a next-best “alternate count” simultaneously. When price violates a rule under the preferred count (e.g., a presumed wave-4 low breaks below the wave-1 high in an impulse, or a presumed wave 2 retraces past 100% of wave 1), the count is immediately falsified and the alternate becomes the new working hypothesis — this gives an objective, rule-based invalidation point that can be translated into a stop-loss level by a trader overlaying conventional risk management on the wave count. Because two or more valid counts are frequently admissible at any given moment, position sizing should account for count ambiguity, not just directional conviction.

Caveats

Guidelines are not rules and can fail: alternation (wave 2 and wave 4 tending to differ in form), the 61.8%/50%/38.2% retracement tendencies, and the various Fibonacci multiple relationships (wave 3 = 1.618× or 2.618× wave 1; wave 5 = 0.382×, 0.618×, or equal to waves 1-through-3; wave C = equal, 0.618×, or 1.618× wave A) are statistical tendencies drawn from historical example, not certainties — treat them as targets to watch, not levels to bet the account on. Real-time wave counting is explicitly acknowledged by the authors as subjective while a pattern is still unfolding; the correct count is often only obvious in hindsight, and the same price data can admit multiple rule-compliant labelings simultaneously. Degree identification (is this a Minor wave or an Intermediate wave?) has no fixed price or time threshold and depends on relative context, which further compounds real-time ambiguity. Given these limitations, this method is best used as a probabilistic overlay for timing and target-setting within a broader system that supplies its own hard risk controls, not as a fully mechanical, code-it-and-forget-it trading system.
