Clinical psychologist and trader Brett Steenbarger adapts cognitive-behavioral exposure therapy into a self-coaching method for breaking repetitive trading-emotion patterns like hesitation and impulsive exits.

Summary

An article (reproduced from a trading-psychology column) by clinical psychologist and trader Brett Steenbarger, explaining exposure therapy — a technique used clinically for phobias and panic disorder — as a tool traders can self-administer to break recurring emotional/behavioral patterns (hesitation, impulsive entries, premature exits). It walks through a relaxation-skill foundation, then progressively harder “exposure” to the trigger situation, illustrated with a clinical case (panic disorder) and a trading case (a trader who freezes on gap-open trend days).

Key points

  • Emotional patterns, not lack of willpower, sabotage trading plans — resolutions fail because plans are anchored to a state of mind that shifts once stress hits; the fix is learning to stay anchored, not analyzing “why.”
  • Relaxation skill is the prerequisite — a four-step technique (deep diaphragmatic breathing, mental focus on a calming image, progressive muscle relaxation head-to-toe, self-awareness of the resulting “zone” feeling) must be rehearsed until it can be triggered quickly.
  • Exposure requires activating the problem, not avoiding it — you must experience a milder version of the triggering situation while practicing the coping skill, repeatedly, to unlearn the automatic emotional reaction.
  • Imaginal exposure precedes real exposure — visualizing the triggering scenario (e.g., a gap-down open) while staying relaxed is the psychological equivalent of paper trading, cheaper and safer than acting on real capital.
  • Progression ladder — imaginal exposure, then historical bar-by-bar paper-trading simulation, then small real (in vivo) positions, only building size once each stage proceeds without anxiety.
  • Five keys to success — patience (fully relax before starting), persistence (repeat often), gradualism (start with manageable versions of the problem), consistency (daily practice), and realism (exposure fixes behavior, not a lack of a researched edge).
  • Case example — a futures trader (RSI-based short-term breakout method) who habitually hesitated and missed opening-gap trend days because of regret-driven freezing; exposure work paired relaxation with rules for confirming true trend days (e.g., gaps on multi-day TICK breakout highs/lows).

Actionable rules

  1. Master the four-step relaxation sequence (breathing, mental focus, muscle relaxation, self-awareness) before attempting exposure work.
  2. Progress in this fixed order: imaginal exposure to hardest real-time (in vivo) exposure, never skipping stages.
  3. Only advance to the next stage once the current one produces no measurable anxiety.
  4. Pair exposure work with previously researched, concrete entry/exit rules for the specific trading situation being desensitized — exposure alone does not create an edge.
  5. Favor fewer, longer, more intensive exposure sessions over many brief ones (cited as more effective per the underlying clinical research).

Caveats

This is a magazine-style psychology article, not a controlled study of traders; its clinical analogy (panic disorder treatment) and trading case are illustrative, not evidence that the method generalizes. The author explicitly warns that exposure techniques cannot substitute for a properly researched trading strategy and cannot resolve chronic psychological conditions, which may require other treatment. No specific market, timeframe, or instrument focus.

Who it is for

Traders with an otherwise sound, researched strategy who consistently sabotage it through a specific, identifiable emotional reaction (freezing, chasing, premature exits) in recurring market situations.
