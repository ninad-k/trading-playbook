Wall Street: How It Works and for Whom

Doug Henwood · 1997 · Tier B — concise note book advanced

Left political-economy critique of the U.S. financial system: how stocks, bonds, and derivatives actually work, who the major players are, and why efficient-market theory misdescribes how markets behave.

Summary

A critical, data-heavy analysis of the U.S. financial system aimed at demystifying how Wall Street functions, structured around Instruments (stocks, bonds, derivatives mechanics), Players (banks, brokers, institutions, financial media), Market models (efficient market hypothesis and its empirical failures), Renegades (chartists and behavioral departures from theory), Governance (shareholder/creditor power over corporate decisions), and a closing chapter on alternatives. It is not a trading manual; it argues the financial system performs poorly at channeling savings into productive investment, instead mainly concentrating wealth and disciplining management and labor on rentiers’ behalf.

Key points

  • Stock market doesn’t mainly raise capital — 1981-1997 U.S. nonfinancial corporations retired more stock (buybacks/takeovers) than they issued; most trading is of existing shares.
  • Derivatives mechanics — futures obligate delivery; options give the right, not the obligation; fewer than 1% of futures contracts settle by delivery; short positions and “naked” option sales carry theoretically unlimited loss versus a long’s capped loss.
  • Retail derivatives trading is a poor bet — cites the widely used estimate that 80-90% of non-professional futures/options traders lose money.
  • Technical analysis skepticism — cites Burton Malkiel’s experiment: coin-flip-generated “charts” shown to professional chartists were confidently read as containing familiar reversal patterns, suggesting chart patterns may reflect projection as much as real signal.
  • Consistent outperformance is rare — cites money-manager rater Norm Zadeh’s estimate that only 1-2% of managers show a consistent, substantial market-beating record.
  • EMH critiqued at length — surveys the efficient market hypothesis and academic evidence of anomalies and departures from the random-walk model.
  • Wealth concentration — the wealthiest 0.5% of the U.S. population held more net worth than the bottom 90% combined; the top 10% held over three-quarters of total wealth.
  • Institutional investor power — argues rising pension-fund/institutional influence (not just globalization/technology) drove much of the era’s corporate downsizing, since portfolio managers press for higher near-term stock prices.

Actionable rules

None given — a critical/descriptive political-economy text, not a trading manual. Takeaways for a trader: treat “consistent market-beating” claims skeptically (only 1-2% of managers show durable outperformance per the cited research); validate chart-pattern setups statistically rather than trusting visual pattern recognition alone (per the Malkiel coin-flip result); and read corporate buyback/issuance headlines as often reflecting ownership rearrangement rather than fundamental capital-raising.

Caveats

A work of left/Marxian political-economy criticism, first published 1997 (lightly updated for a 2005 web edition); its statistics on debt, wealth, and market structure are dated to the mid-to-late 1990s. Its argument is explicitly normative rather than a neutral trading explainer, so its value here is mainly as a skeptical counterweight to EMH and technical-analysis orthodoxy and as background on market structure.

Who it is for

Traders and investors wanting a critical, well-sourced outsider’s view of how Wall Street institutions, instruments, and theories like EMH actually function and whose interests they serve.
