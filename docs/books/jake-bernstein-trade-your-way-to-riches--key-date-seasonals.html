Key Date Seasonals

Jake Bernstein · 1996 · Tier A — deep note system intermediate

Bernstein's calendar-based futures system: buy/sell on a fixed date, exit on a second fixed date, sized with a percentage stop, chosen for a historically strong seasonal tendency.

What it is

Key Date Seasonal is Jake Bernstein’s mechanical calendar-timing system for futures markets, presented in the “Seasonals Part II” module of his Trade Your Way to Riches Now! video seminar manual. Rather than trading a chart pattern or indicator signal, the trader takes a position on a fixed calendar date each year and exits on a second fixed calendar date, based on a historical tendency for that market to move in a consistent direction across that specific window in most years. Bernstein illustrates the method with fully worked historical trade tables (not just an asserted win rate) for at least two markets in the sampled pages: the Dow Jones Industrial Average (buy 4/6, sell 4/16) and Live Hogs (buy 10/30, sell 11/27), each shown with year-by-year entry price, exit price, profit/loss, and percent profit going back several decades.

Rules

  1. Entry: buy (or sell, for a bearish seasonal window) at the close on the fixed calendar date identified for the market, e.g., April 6 for the Dow Jones trade, October 30 for the Live Hogs trade. The tables use “KEY PRICE: CLOSE,” i.e., trades are marked using the closing price on the entry and exit dates.
  2. Exit: close the position at the close on the fixed calendar exit date, e.g., April 16 for the Dow Jones trade (a roughly 10-calendar-day hold), November 27 for the Live Hogs trade (a roughly 28-calendar-day hold). Exact entry/exit dates shift by a day or two in some years to land on the nearest trading day.
  3. Stop-loss: a percentage stop-loss is applied to each trade, shown on the results table as “SL:”. The Dow Jones April 6-16 trade uses a 5.0% stop; the Live Hogs October 30-November 27 trade uses a 6.0% stop. The stop percentage is set per market/window; no universal stop percentage applies across all Key Date Seasonals in the sampled pages.
  4. Direction: determined by the historical seasonal tendency for that specific date window (both worked examples in the sample are long/buy setups); Bernstein’s broader “Introduction to Seasonals” module (same book) identifies candidate windows using a seasonal array that flags “high odds up” and “high odds down” months/periods from historical percentage price-change data, but the precise statistical rule for selecting a Key Date pair (minimum sample size, significance threshold, selection process) is not present in the sampled pages.
  5. Market selection: the manual applies the method to individual futures markets (Dow Jones, wheat, T-Bonds, Live Hogs shown with full tables; other markets referenced by chart title only) and to seasonal spreads between two related contracts (e.g., April Live Cattle long vs. April Live Hogs short), where the spread itself — not either outright leg — is the traded instrument.

Risk

Position risk is defined solely by the per-trade percentage stop-loss shown for each Key Date pair (5.0% on the Dow Jones example, 6.0% on the Live Hogs example); this is a price-percentage stop measured from entry, not a percent-of-account risk figure, so account-level position sizing (how many contracts, or what percent of equity a given stop represents) is left to the trader and is not specified in the sampled pages. No pyramiding, scaling, or trailing-stop rule is given for this system — the sampled tables show a single entry, a single exit (or an earlier stop-out), and no reference to using this method to manage risk beyond the stop percentage.

The historical result tables report aggregate statistics per market: for the Dow Jones April 6-16 trade, 39 positive years versus 10 negative years out of 49 (79.59% of trades profitable), an average profit of 24.66 points (2.23%) against an average loss of 10.62 points (-2.36%). For the Live Hogs October 30-November 27 trade, 22 positive years versus 5 negative years out of 27 (81.43% of trades profitable), average profit 2.10 (5.46%) against average loss of roughly 2.01 (-4.26%). These are the only two fully quantified examples recoverable from the sampled OCR pages; results for the other Key Date Seasonals referenced by chart title (wheat, T-Bonds, and the various inter-market spreads) are not shown with full tables in the sample and their win rates should not be assumed.

Caveats

These notes are drawn from a scanned training manual where only a sample of pages was OCR’d; the manual is chart/slide-based with minimal connected prose, so the underlying rationale for how a Key Date window is selected, whether the historical results are adjusted for slippage/commissions, and how many total date-pairs were tested before arriving at the ones shown (a material data-mining/curve-fitting risk for any date-based seasonal system) are not documented in the sampled pages and are not stated here. The historical windows shown span mostly 1945-1993 (Dow Jones) and 1967-1993 (Live Hogs) — pre-decimalization, pit-trading-era data — so contract specifications, tick sizes, and market microstructure have changed materially since; the underlying seasonal tendency (if genuine) may or may not persist unchanged. No out-of-sample validation, walk-forward test, or discussion of survivorship among the many possible calendar windows is present in the source pages sampled for this system.
