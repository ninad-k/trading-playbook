Overview

The third book in Schwager’s Market Wizards series, this one narrows the lens to equity and equity-derivatives traders (with one options/futures crossover in Fletcher and one pure quant in Shaw). Fourteen practitioners are interviewed — hedge fund managers, a floor-trained options trader, a short-seller, a quant-fund founder, an S&P options seller, and a psychiatrist (Ari Kiev) who coaches professional traders — followed by a closing chapter, “Wizard Lessons,” that extracts 65 numbered cross-cutting principles from all the interviews, and a short appendix explaining basic option mechanics. Each interview follows Schwager’s format: biography and career arc, a Q&A on method and formative mistakes, then Schwager’s own summary and commentary. There is no single system in this book; the point is the diversity of approaches that each produced exceptional risk-adjusted returns.

Core thesis

There is no single “true path” to trading success — the interviewed traders range from pure fundamentalist (Lauer, Okumus, Watson) to pure technical (Minervini), from discretionary (Walton, Cohen) to fully systematic (Lescarbeau, Shaw), from long-only value to dedicated short-selling (Galante) to market-neutral quant (Shaw, Guazzoni, Bender). What they share, despite radically different methods, is a small set of traits: absolute discipline in executing a predefined plan, rigorous loss control, a clearly articulable “edge,” the flexibility to abandon an approach that stops working (including their own earlier, failed methods), and enough confidence and hard work to implement good ideas in meaningful size. Schwager’s explicit argument is that these traits — not any specific indicator or valuation screen — are the actual transferable content of the book.

Key concepts

  • Edge — a specific, articulable reason a trader expects to make money (a catalyst-based model, a valuation screen, a statistical mispricing); Schwager states no one in the book trades without being able to name theirs.
  • Catalyst — a known or anticipated event that will cause a stock to move (earnings, insider buying, a product launch); several traders (Lauer, Watson, Masters) treat a catalyst as a required condition for any trade, distinct from mere undervaluation.
  • Concentration vs. diversification — several traders (Lauer, Okumus) deliberately hold few positions (10–15) on the argument that broad diversification guarantees index-like, mediocre results and dilutes true best ideas.
  • Statistical arbitrage / pairs trading — buying/selling related securities whose prices have diverged, on the expectation of reconversion; the conceptual (not exact) basis attributed to David Shaw’s quantitative strategies.
  • Relative strength — Minervini’s core screen: buying stocks already outperforming the market (or declining least in a correction), on the premise that the market’s best performers tend to keep performing.
  • “Get smaller” — Mark Cook’s rule of cutting position size at the first sign of being wrong, rather than exiting all-or-nothing.
  • Time stop — liquidating a position if it fails to move as expected within a predefined window, used by both Cook and Masters alongside price-based stops.
  • Insider buying as confirmation — Lauer, Okumus, and Watson all use insider purchases (scaled to the insider’s net worth/salary, per Okumus) as a supporting, not sufficient, buy signal.
  • Option box spread / synthetic financing — Fletcher’s early strategy exploiting the gap between the risk-free rate used in option pricing models and the higher commercial borrowing rate faced by real option buyers.
  • Betting against the option pricing model — Bender’s approach of estimating his own probability distribution for a stock’s future price and trading options only where it diverges from the market-implied (lognormal) distribution.
  • Data mining / overfitting — the danger, flagged explicitly by Shaw and implicitly by Lescarbeau’s caution, of finding “profitable” patterns that are pure artifacts of testing too many variable combinations on historical data.

Rules and setups

The book gives no single rule set; it gives risk-management and process rules repeated across traders (drawn mainly from the “Wizard Lessons” chapter):

  1. Have a documented business plan before trading, per Cook — specifying markets traded, capitalization, order-entry method, drawdown thresholds that trigger a trading halt, profit goals, and a trade-review procedure.
  2. Define your edge explicitly before risking capital; if you cannot state it clearly, you are not ready to trade (Wizard Lesson #9).
  3. Use a stop-loss set before entry, with wider stops for higher-profit-potential trades (Minervini, Cook); alternatively, control risk through highly restrictive stock selection instead of stops (Lauer, Okumus, Watson).
  4. Cover a short at a fixed maximum loss (Watson: roughly 20–30% against the position) regardless of continued conviction — short-side losses are open-ended and must be capped mechanically.
  5. Cap net portfolio exposure. Okumus, after being caught 200% net long into a 53% drawdown in August 1998, adopted a rule of never exceeding 100% net long or short.
  6. Reduce position size at the first sign of being wrong rather than holding for an all-or-nothing exit (Cook’s “get smaller”; Cohen’s “take in half, then half again”).
  7. Use a time stop: exit if a position hasn’t moved as expected within a predefined period (Cook, Masters).
  8. Exit immediately if the trade’s stated premise is violated — e.g., Cohen covered a short on IBM the moment it reported better-than-expected earnings, taking the loss rather than waiting.
  9. Concentrate capital in high-conviction ideas. Lauer: a “good idea” bought in only 50,000–100,000 shares instead of a million-plus is itself a mistake; scale position size to conviction, not just to available capital.
  10. Sell out-of-the-money puts at a strike where you’d want to own the stock anyway (Okumus) — collects premium if the stock doesn’t reach your buy price, and reduces effective cost basis if it does.
  11. Screen for value with a floor, not just a ceiling — e.g., Watson’s 8–12 P/E range, Okumus’s 60%+ decline off highs with P/E under 12 and price near book value, Lauer’s 50%+ market-adjusted decline — but always require a catalyst on top of cheapness, since “value alone is not enough.”

Risk and money management

Risk control is the book’s most consistent theme across otherwise incompatible styles. Cohen states plainly that even his best trader is profitable only 63% of the time and the firm-wide average is 50–55%, so “you better make sure your losses are as small as they can be” — nearly all of Cohen’s profit (Kiev’s data: ~100% of gains) comes from about 5% of trades, meaning correct sizing on the rare high-conviction trade matters more than average win rate. The book documents several near-fatal blowups from excess leverage or unmanaged risk: Lauer’s full-margin position was forced out in the 1987 crash despite an eventually correct thesis; Cook lost roughly $500,000 (and nearly his family’s savings) on naked short options in a 1982 takeover that halted trading before expiration, afterward banning himself from ever selling naked options again; Minervini and Walton both wiped out early by ceding decisions to others’ tips. On short selling specifically, the book makes a structural argument: despite unlimited theoretical loss, capped 100% max gain, and mechanical disadvantages (borrow recalls, forced buy-ins, uptick rule), 13 of 14 traders interviewed shorted regularly because shorts are negatively correlated with long portfolios, letting a trader run more long leverage at the same net portfolio risk — provided every short carries a hard, mechanically enforced loss limit.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s psychological throughline, reinforced by the dedicated Ari Kiev interview, is that technical skill is necessary but not sufficient — nearly every trader profiled had at least one complete early wipeout, and the differentiator was flexibility (the willingness to discard a “beautiful theory” contradicted by results) plus confidence that appears to precede rather than merely follow success. Kiev’s practice at Cohen’s firm (S.A.C.) centers on getting traders to publicly commit to stretch performance targets, on the theory that “believing an outcome is possible makes it achievable,” and on diagnosing specific self-defeating patterns (bargain-hunting for a slightly better entry that causes missed trades, anxiety-driven partial exits, loss of discipline after early-month gains). Recurring behavioral rules across interviews: never let others make your decisions (Walton and Minervini both cite this as their costliest lesson), keep positions secret to avoid both information leakage and unwanted second-guessing (Lauer), treat “I hope this comes back” as a mandatory exit signal (Cook), and separate personal-life stress from trading risk — several traders’ worst periods coincided exactly with divorce, injury, or family illness, and the advice is to cut size or stop trading during such periods rather than assume competence is unaffected.

Chapter map

  • Stuart Walton — “Back from the Abyss”: near-bankruptcy from following tips, then a fundamentals-plus-technicals-plus-sentiment blend (“blessed stocks”) with fast in-and-out trading and no fixed holding period.
  • Michael Lauer — “The Wisdom of Value, the Folly of Fad”: concentrated (≤15 positions) value investing via a strict six-screen process, explicit critique of “closet index” mutual funds and mega-cap overvaluation.
  • Steve Watson — “Dialing for Dollars”: Peter Lynch-style primary research (calling companies, customers, competitors) combined with cheap valuation (P/E 8–12) and disciplined short-side stops.
  • Dana Galante — “Against the Current”: dedicated short-selling using red-flag screens (rising receivables, auditor changes, CFO turnover) confirmed by high P/E, a catalyst, and a stalling uptrend.
  • Mark D. Cook — “Harvesting S&P Profits”: self-taught S&P options seller who nearly went bankrupt on naked options in 1982, rebuilt via a diary-based study of his own trades (day-of-week edge, expiration-Friday danger) into high-reliability short-term pattern trading.
  • Alphonse “Buddy” Fletcher Jr. — “Win-Win Investing”: structured financing deals (e.g., convertible-like private placements with capped upside and downside protection) combined with option hedges for near-guaranteed profitability.
  • Ahmet Okumus — “From Istanbul to Wall Street Bull”: deep-value stock picking (60%+ declines, low P/E, near book value) after a near-disastrous 1998 attempt to short overpriced Internet stocks via naked calls.
  • Mark Minervini — “Stock Around the Clock”: relative-strength momentum trading in higher-priced growth names, extreme discipline and a hard cap on per-trade losses discovered by analyzing his own trade history.
  • Steve Lescarbeau — “The Ultimate Trading System”: fully mechanical mutual-fund timing system, disclosed in no technical detail, with extraordinarily low drawdowns and near-total avoidance of shorting.
  • Michael Masters — “Swimming Through the Markets”: a catalyst-based trading model built from years of handwritten market observations, with three explicit exit triggers (profit target, catalyst failure, time stop).
  • John Bender — “Questioning the Obvious”: options trading built on rejecting the standard lognormal option-pricing assumption and betting on his own price-probability estimates instead.
  • Claudio Guazzoni — “Eliminating the Downside”: began with a simple strategy of buying restricted shares at a steep discount to market price, evolved into broader hedged, protected-downside structures.
  • David Shaw — “The Quantitative Edge”: large-scale, highly secretive multi-strategy quantitative trading across global equities and related instruments, grounded in hypothesis-driven statistical testing to avoid data mining.
  • Steve Cohen — “The Trading Room”: high-turnover discretionary trading combining an internal network of traders/analysts with rapid loss-cutting (63% peak individual win rate) and a small fraction of trades driving nearly all profit.
  • Ari Kiev, M.D. — “The Mind of a Winner”: a psychiatrist’s account of coaching traders (notably at Cohen’s firm) using goal-commitment, mental-state replication, and diagnosis of self-sabotaging patterns borrowed from Olympic sports psychology.
  • Wizard Lessons — 65 numbered cross-cutting principles on edge, discipline, risk control, exits, psychology, and market structure, distilled from all interviews.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s strength is breadth with genuine specificity: unlike generic trading-psychology books, each interview grounds its lessons in named strategies, real numbers (return streams, drawdowns, specific loss events), and traceable mistakes, and the closing “Wizard Lessons” chapter is an unusually well-organized synthesis rather than a vague summary. The central caveat is that almost none of the individual methods are fully specified — Lescarbeau and Shaw explicitly refuse to disclose their systems, several other traders (Minervini, Cohen) give philosophy more than mechanics, and no interview provides a codeable rule set comparable to a dedicated systems book; this is why no system sub-page accompanies this note. Some strategies described (Lauer’s index-fund overvaluation warning, Okumus’s Internet-mania caution) are explicitly time-bound to the late-1990s dot-com period and dated as forward guidance, though the underlying lessons (fundamentals are relative to price, “this time is never different”) are framed by Schwager as durable. A number of interviews also involve small sample periods (three to seven years of live returns) that, while statistically impressive, are shorter than a full market cycle, and none of the results are independently audited within the book itself.

Who should read it

Best for an intermediate-to-advanced trader or investor who already has a working method and wants to stress-test their discipline, risk control, and psychological framework against a range of genuinely different successful approaches, rather than someone looking for a single system to copy. Also useful as a primer on how differently value investing, short selling, options trading, and quantitative strategies can each be run profitably with proper risk control. Less useful for a beginner seeking step-by-step entry/exit rules, since virtually every trader here treats their specific method as proprietary and gives away process and psychology, not code.

Related books in this library

  • Reminiscences of a Stock Operator — an earlier single-trader narrative covering the same themes of discipline, loss-cutting, and psychological control that this anthology generalizes across fourteen traders.
  • Trade Like Jesse Livermore — offers the concrete, codeable rule set (pivotal points, position sizing, 10% stop) that this more anecdotal anthology mostly avoids specifying.
  • The Disciplined Trader: Developing Winning Attitudes and Trading in the Zone: Master the Market with Confidence, Discipline, and a Winning Attitude — deeper treatments of the psychological discipline theme that recurs in nearly every interview here, especially the Ari Kiev chapter.
  • Black-Scholes Option Pricing Model — the standard option-pricing framework that John Bender’s chapter explicitly argues against and profits from betting against.

Related in this library

  • Reminiscences of a Stock Operator — Fictionalized memoir of Jesse Livermore distilling tape reading, cutting losses, pyramiding into strength, and the psychology of speculation.
  • Trade Like Jesse Livermore — Reconstructs Jesse Livermore's technical system — Pivotal Point timing, industry-group top-down analysis, and five money-management rules — from his heirs' interviews and his own 1940 market-key notebooks.
  • The Disciplined Trader: Developing Winning Attitudes — Argues that trading losses come from unmanaged beliefs and fear, not bad analysis, and lays out a seven-step process for rebuilding a trader's mental framework around predefined rules.
  • Trading in the Zone: Master the Market with Confidence, Discipline, and a Winning Attitude — Argues consistent trading success is purely a psychological/belief problem: think in probabilities, fully accept risk, and mechanically execute a defined edge.
  • Black-Scholes Option Pricing Model — A step-by-step mathematical derivation of the Black-Scholes PDE and its closed-form solution for a European call option.
