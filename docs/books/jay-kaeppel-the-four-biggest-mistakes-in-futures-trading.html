Overview

Jay Kaeppel, director of research at Essex Trading Company, structures this short manual around a single claim: roughly 90% of futures traders lose money, and the overwhelming majority of that failure traces back to four specific, avoidable mistakes rather than to bad luck or unpredictable markets. Each of the four sections follows the same pattern — define the mistake, explain why traders make it, and give a concrete method to avoid it. The book is light on chart patterns or entry signals and heavy on account structure, leverage sizing, and behavioral discipline, making it a money-management and psychology text rather than a systems book.

Core thesis

Futures trading fails traders not because the underlying commodities are unusually volatile (Kaeppel shows average futures volatility is actually lower than a comparison basket of stocks) but because of leverage: a retail trader routinely controls a position 20–35 times the cash committed as margin, and most traders don’t consciously grasp that ratio. Combine unrecognized leverage with no trading plan, no formal risk controls, and predictable lapses in discipline under real money pressure, and the same four failure modes recur across almost all blown-up accounts. Avoiding all four mistakes does not guarantee profitability, but failing to avoid them makes long-run failure close to certain.

Key concepts

  • The four mistakes — (1) lack of a trading plan, (2) using too much leverage, (3) failure to control risk, (4) lack of discipline; presented as sequential layers, each building on the last.
  • Leverage ratio — the multiple between the notional value of a futures contract and the margin required to control it (illustrated at 33-to-1 for a $5.00 Soybean contract against $750 margin); Kaeppel argues this, not market volatility, is what makes futures dangerous.
  • Optimal f — Ralph Vince’s formula for the fraction of capital that maximizes long-run geometric growth from a given trade history; used here as one input to account sizing, not as a standalone bet-sizing rule.
  • Margin-to-equity ratio — total initial margin required for all open positions divided by account equity; a fast proxy for how “levered” a portfolio is at any moment.
  • The Litmus Test — Kaeppel’s readiness check: withdraw a meaningful sum in cash, throw it away, and see if you can calmly say “I wish I hadn’t done that” and move on; inability to do so means you are not financially/emotionally ready to risk that amount trading futures.
  • The Four Cornerstones — go with the trend, cut your losses, let your profits run, don’t let big winners get away; the organizing framework for building entry/exit rules.
  • Points A, B, C, D — a psychological framework separating what happens before entry (A→B, irrelevant), during the trade (B→C, the only part that matters), and after exit (C→D, irrelevant) — used to cure “woulda, shoulda, coulda” second-guessing.
  • System tinkering — continuously altering a system mid-stream based on the most recent trade(s), which prevents building enough confidence in it to follow under pressure.

Rules and setups

The book is organized as four “mistakes,” each with concrete, numeric guidance rather than an entry/exit chart pattern; full formulas for Mistake #2’s account-sizing method are detailed in the system sub-page.

  1. Mistake #1 — Trading plan: before trading, answer in writing how much capital you can commit (minimum recommended account $10,000; cap risk per trade at 5% of capital, e.g., $500 on a $10,000 account), which market(s) to trade (diversify across at least three unless you have a genuine floor-trader edge), what time frame fits your temperament, and explicit entry/profit-exit/loss-exit criteria; commit the entire decided amount rather than a partial “risk-only” sub-amount, since an already-shrunk account has disproportionately less flexibility.
  2. Mistake #2 — Leverage / account sizing: estimate required capital per market by blending Optimal f, the largest historical overnight gap, and maximum drawdown, then roll per-market figures up into portfolio-level “aggressive” and “conservative” account sizes and average them (full formulas in the system sub-page).
  3. Mistake #3 — Risk control: diversify across uncorrelated markets and across trading time frames/methods; keep margin-to-equity ratio under roughly 30% as a rule of thumb (above 30% is “aggressive”); use stop-loss orders — Kaeppel favors placing them in the market over relying on mental stops, since mental stops require pulling the trigger at the moment of maximum psychological pain.
  4. Mistake #4 — Discipline: predefine reactions to likely stress points so you can react rather than deliberate in real time; review and adjust a system only at scheduled intervals (never mid-drawdown, never based on the last 1–3 trades) to avoid “system tinkering”; treat market action before entry and after exit as irrelevant to avoid second-guessing (the Points A–D framework).

Risk and money management

Money management is effectively the entire book. Concrete anchors: cap single-trade risk at 5% of trading capital (and “ideally a lot less”); keep the margin-to-equity ratio below about 30%; size each market’s minimum required capital using the blended Optimal-f/overnight-gap/drawdown formula (full worked example in the system sub-page). Kaeppel notes that traders who tell themselves “I can sit through a 30% drawdown” typically abandon the approach about 25% of the way into it (his estimate: roughly 90% quit early), so account sizing should target a drawdown the trader can survive both financially and psychologically, not just the historical maximum.

Psychology and discipline

Kaeppel treats discipline as the hardest of the four mistakes to fix because, unlike a trading plan or a leverage calculation, it can only be tested live, “in the line of fire,” with real money on the table. He attributes discipline failures to fear, greed, and ego, and gives a repeated behavioral prescription: “don’t think, react” — meaning that once a predefined exit trigger is hit, execution should be immediate and non-analytical, because time spent trying to understand why the market is moving against you is time spent losing money. He also flags a specific irony (the “IQ obstacle”): highly analytical traders are more prone to freeze at exactly the moment a rule says to act, because they instinctively want to understand the move before accepting the loss. The book’s cure for revenge trading and “shoulda” regret is the Points A–D framework, and its cure for chronic rule-drift is separating live trading from system-review, which should happen only at scheduled off-market intervals.

Chapter map

  • Introduction — the 90% failure rate, what sets futures apart (leverage vs. volatility), a preview of the four mistakes.
  • Mistake #1: Lack of a Trading Plan — the Litmus Test, capital commitment, market/time-frame/method selection, entry/exit criteria, the Four Cornerstones.
  • Mistake #2: Using Too Much Leverage — leverage mechanics illustrated with a Soybean example, Optimal f, largest overnight gap tables, maximum drawdown, the blended account-sizing formulas (aggressive/conservative/optimum).
  • Mistake #3: Failure to Control Risk — the Niederhoffer cautionary tale, five risk-control methods (market diversification, time-frame/method diversification, account sizing, margin-to-equity ratio, stop-loss orders and their three schools of thought).
  • Mistake #4: Lack of Discipline — fear/greed/ego, “don’t think, react,” the IQ obstacle, avoiding traps, the Points A–D cure for regret, system development vs. system tinkering.
  • Conclusion — synthesis of the four mistakes into a single readiness checklist.
  • Appendix A — mathematical formula for standard deviation (supporting the account-sizing math in Mistake #2).

Strengths and caveats

The numeric examples (leverage ratios, overnight-gap tables, account-sizing formulas) are unusually concrete for a psychology-adjacent book, and the underlying math (Optimal f, standard-deviation-based drawdown estimation) is presented with enough detail to actually apply, not just cite. Caveats: overnight-gap and margin figures (e.g., $750 Soybean margin) reflect year-2000 market conditions and are stale as absolute numbers, though the update method remains valid; the “90% of traders lose money” and “~90% quit 25% into an expected drawdown” statistics are cited without an academic source and should be treated as informed estimates, not rigorously sourced figures; and Optimal f is presented with its own limitation clearly stated — it ignores drawdown and is not recommended standalone, only as one input into the blended formula.

Who should read it

Best suited to traders who already have (or are building) a mechanical or semi-mechanical futures system and need a rigorous framework for account sizing, margin discipline, and stop-loss policy before risking real capital; also valuable for any leveraged-instrument trader (not just futures) for its treatment of leverage misunderstanding and discipline failure. Less useful for readers seeking entry-signal or chart-pattern content, since the book deliberately stays at the account-structure and behavioral level throughout.
