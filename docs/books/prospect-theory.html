Summary

A July 2005 SFO Magazine article by David Silverman introducing Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky’s prospect theory (for which Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences) and applying its findings to trading behavior. The article walks through the classic Kahneman/Tversky gain-vs-loss experiments, explains why traders act irrationally when facing losses, and closes with Kahneman’s “System 1 / System 2” model of intuitive versus deliberate thinking as a lens on trading decisions.

Key points

  • Prospect theory grew out of Kahneman and Tversky’s research into how people actually make decisions under risk and uncertainty, as opposed to how classical (efficient-market) economics assumes they should.
  • Classic experiment: offered a certain $3,000 gain vs. an 80% chance of $4,000 (20% chance of nothing), 80% of respondents chose the certain gain — status-quo bias/risk aversion — even though the gamble had a higher expected value ($3,200).
  • Mirror experiment framed as losses: offered a certain $3,000 loss vs. an 80% chance of losing $4,000 (20% chance of breaking even), 92% chose to gamble — despite both scenarios being mathematically identical in risk to the gain-framed version.
  • Conclusion: people are not simply risk-averse; they are loss-averse — risk-averse when facing gains, risk-seeking when facing losses.
  • Tversky’s finding: people are more sensitive to negative than positive stimuli, and risk valuation depends on the reference point (e.g., account balance at time of entry), not absolute wealth.
  • Applied to trading: moving or removing a stop-loss just before it triggers, based on a post-hoc rationalization (e.g., “the 200-day moving average will hold”) not considered at entry, is cited as a textbook prospect-theory error driven by fear of realizing a loss.
  • Kahneman’s two-system model: System 1 (intuition) is fast, automatic, and emotionally driven; System 2 (reason) is slow, deliberate, and rule-following. System 1 tends to dominate decisions involving risk and fear, which the article frames as the root of impulsive trading mistakes.
  • The article includes a reading list on behavioral finance (Thaler’s “The Winner’s Curse,” Bernstein’s “Against the Gods,” and others) for readers wanting to go deeper.

Actionable rules

None given as numbered, tradable rules — this is a psychology/framing article, not a system. The implicit takeaways: (1) do not move or cancel a stop-loss based on a rationalization formed after entry; (2) recognize that the urge to gamble to “get back to even” after a loss is a predictable cognitive bias, not a rational read of the market; (3) decisions should be evaluated by their process (System 2, rule-following) rather than by System 1 impulses triggered by fear.

Caveats

The article is a magazine-length popularization of academic research (Kahneman & Tversky’s 1979 Econometrica paper “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decisions Under Risk” is cited but not reproduced), so it simplifies the underlying theory considerably. No trading system, statistics, or backtest is presented; content is entirely about decision psychology.

Who it is for

Traders who want a short, accessible introduction to why fear and loss aversion distort trading decisions, as a bridge to deeper behavioral-finance reading.
