Home Bias in Equity Allocations

David Brown · 2004 · Tier B — concise note paper intermediate

Studies Australian versus global equity allocations, distinguishing long-horizon results from unstable short-term portfolio rankings.

Summary

This Autumn 2004 PortfolioConstruction Journal paper asks whether Australians’ preference for domestic shares was justified by historical outcomes. Brown compares Australian equities with currency-hedged and unhedged MSCI World ex Australia exposure from January 1986 through December 2003, then varies the equity mix within a balanced-fund proxy. The central distinction is between long-horizon performance and the discomfort of frequently looking wrong in shorter reporting periods (PDF pp. 1-4).

Key points

  • The sample contains 206 rolling 12-month periods. These overlap and must not be treated as independent observations (pp. 2, 4).
  • Average rolling annual returns were 12.8% for hedged global shares, 11.1% for unhedged global shares and 11.0% for Australian shares. Corresponding standard deviations were 18.2%, 19.9% and 17.5% (p. 2, Figure 2).
  • Despite the higher hedged-global average, the paper says the average-return differences were not statistically significant (p. 2).
  • Currency hedging materially changed the global-equity comparison; an overseas asset allocation and its currency exposure are separate decisions (pp. 1-3).
  • The balanced-fund proxy drew on surveys of over 30 funds, generally with 70% growth and 30% defensive assets (p. 4, endnote 3).
  • Extreme domestic/global mixes frequently occupied both the best and worst short-term rankings. Moderate mixes were usually less extreme (p. 3, Figure 5).

Actionable rules

  1. Compare the domestic/global decision in the context of the whole portfolio, not solely by ranking two standalone equity indices (pp. 2-3).
  2. Evaluate both long-horizon outcomes and the frequency of disappointing rolling-period reports; the latter affects an investor’s ability to maintain an allocation (pp. 3-4).
  3. Brown’s historical proposal was to move toward a 50/50 domestic/international equity mix, with a possible later 30/70 mix depending on circumstances. These are his 2004 conclusions, not current allocation guidance (p. 4).

Caveats

Results depend on the selected period, benchmarks, hedging and proxy portfolio. “Efficiency” in the ranking exercise is a simple return-to-volatility ratio, not a comprehensive risk measure. The paper notes that detailed methodology is available separately and explicitly disclaims persistence of its historical conclusions. It supplies no market-timing or stock-selection rules.

Who it is for

Readers studying diversification and the tension between a long-term allocation thesis and short-term performance reporting.
