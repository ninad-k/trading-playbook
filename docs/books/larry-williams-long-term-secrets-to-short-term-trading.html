Overview

A practitioner’s manual of short-term (1–5 day) futures trading patterns and money management formulas, written by Larry Williams from roughly 40 years of trading Bonds, the S&P 500, and grains. The book centers on one claim: price is not perfectly random, and small, measurable statistical edges (day-of-week bias, volatility expansion off the open, emotional overreaction patterns) compound into large profits once paired with a disciplined exit and correct position sizing. Nearly every pattern is backed by a specific backtest (date range, trade count, win rate, dollar profit) rather than a general chart-reading heuristic.

Core thesis

Markets are “not a coin flip” — Williams presents day-of-week and day-of-month statistics showing prices do not close up or down with even 50/50 odds, and short-term patterns reflecting public overreaction (emotional buying/selling into gaps and false breakouts) reliably set up short-term reversals. His approach layers several independent, individually weak biases (a chart pattern, a calendar bias, an intermarket relationship such as Gold vs. Bonds) — “stacking the deck” — to raise win rate and profit per trade far above any single filter alone. He argues money management, not entry timing, is what turns a mediocre edge into large compounding wealth, and devotes his longest chapter to sizing formulas.

Key concepts

  • Trading Day of the Week (TDW) — certain weekdays show a statistically different open-to-close bias (e.g., S&P Mondays close up 57% of the time, avg +$109; Bond Tuesdays positive 55%, avg +$53); used as a trade filter.
  • Trading Day of the Month (TDM) — count of trading days elapsed in the current month (not the calendar date), used to time recurring monthly rally/decline windows.
  • Volatility breakout entry — buying/selling a fixed % of the prior day’s range added to/subtracted from the next day’s open; Williams calls this “the most consistently profitable mechanical entry technique” he has traded.
  • Smash day — a day that closes below the prior day’s low (buy setup) or above the prior day’s high (sell setup), signaling public over-reaction; a “hidden” version has the close in the outer 25% of range against the trend.
  • Specialists’ trap — a Wyckoff-derived pattern: a range-bound market breaks out with a naked close beyond the range, then the breakout day’s extreme fails within 1–3 days, trapping breakout traders.
  • Oops! — Williams’ best-known pattern: an opening gap beyond the prior day’s range that reverses back into that range, trapping traders who acted on the gap.
  • Greatest Swing Value (GSV) — the average “failure swing” (open-to-high move on a down-close day, or open-to-low on an up-close day) over 1–4 days, used as a volatility entry offset from the next open.
  • Will-Spread — an intermarket indicator from the ratio of a primary market’s price to a related secondary market (e.g., Bonds vs. Gold), smoothed with a fast/slow EMA crossover.
  • Bailout exit — exit on the first profitable opening after entry (immediate for fast markets like the S&P; delayed a day or two for slower markets).
  • Optimal f / fixed-fraction sizing — position size = fixed % of current equity ÷ largest historical loss per contract, recalculated every trade.

Rules and setups

See linked system pages for full entry/exit/stop specifications on each named pattern (Smash Day, Specialists’ Trap, Oops!, Greatest Swing Value, TDW/TDM). Williams’ three universal exit rules (Chapter 11), which apply across every pattern in the book:

  1. Always use a dollar stop on every trade as final protection (values given per pattern, typically $1,000–$2,500 depending on market).
  2. Bailout exit: close the trade on the first profitable opening — if profit is even one tick, take it (delay a day or two in slower markets to let profit grow).
  3. Reverse on an opposite signal: if short and a buy signal fires, exit and reverse immediately rather than waiting for the stop or bailout exit.

Money management formula (Chapter 13, his own solution after testing the Kelly criterion and Ryan Jones’s fixed-ratio approach): contracts to trade = (account balance × risk%) / largest loss per contract. He states typical risk% by trader temperament: ~5% (conservative) up to ~20%+ (aggressive), with 10–15% as his general recommendation and his own trading using up to ~15%; he found the point where drawdown grows faster than profit typically falls between 14% and 21% risk.

Risk and money management

  • Every pattern trade carries a hard dollar stop (examples: $1,500–$1,800 in Bonds, $2,000–$2,500 in the S&P 500, $1,600–$1,750 for GSV trades).
  • Williams rejects the “bet a thousand to make a thousand” mindset; in his own results (turning $10,000 into $1,100,000 in one year, and $50,000 into over $1,000,000), he never risked more than 20% of his stake on a single trade — and calls that “larger than it should have been.”
  • The Kelly criterion (F = ((R+1)×P − 1) / R, P = win rate, R = win/loss size ratio) is presented then explicitly discredited for trading — Kelly assumes a fixed win/loss size (true in blackjack, false in trading, where the largest loss is the real killer of an account).
  • His own formula recomputes size every trade from current equity, shrinking after losses and growing after wins — avoiding the “add a contract every $10,000 of profit” trap that let one 90%-accurate hypothetical system still net a loss after one large loss with an oversized position.
  • Illustrative backtests scale position size up to 5,000 Bond contracts to show formula behavior; Williams warns this is unrealistic and to focus on the shape of the results, not the raw totals.

Psychology and discipline

Williams frames his core trading belief as “the current trade will be a loser” — a deliberately pessimistic stance meant to counteract overconfidence, which he says causes traders to hold losers too long and oversize positions. Speculation, in his framing, has three components — selection, timing, and trade management — and only the last is where most traders fail. He stresses that patience (waiting for a “stacked deck” of confirming filters rather than trading every day) and unemotional rule-following separate career traders from the 80% of short-term traders he cites as losing their capital. A recurring theme is “press your winners, not your losers”: traders instinctively do the opposite, increasing size after losses and cutting size after wins — the single most damaging behavioral error alongside oversized bets and skipped stops.

  • Ch 15 — Just What Does Make the Stock Market Rally? — logic, data, and habit-breaking essays on market drivers.
  • Ch 16 — Closing Comments — concluding reflections.

Strengths and caveats

  • Every pattern is backed by specific, falsifiable backtest statistics (date ranges, trade counts, win rates, dollar totals) — unusually rigorous for a pattern-trading book of this era.
  • Results reflect same-author, in-sample optimization on a handful of liquid futures (Bonds, S&P 500, grains) from 1977–1998; no out-of-sample validation is shown, and Williams himself flags the curve-fitting risk of the Oops!-plus-calendar combination.
  • Dollar figures and stops reflect late-1990s futures tick values, margins, and commissions ($50/round-turn cited); sizing examples up to 5,000 Bond contracts are explicitly unrealistic, shown only for formula mechanics.
  • The book does not detail Williams’ best-known indicator, Williams %R — its toolkit here centers on Will-Spread, Greatest Swing Value, and the pattern catalog instead.
  • The Kelly-formula episode (a managed account swinging from $10,000 to $2,100,000 to $700,000 in one year) is presented candidly as a cautionary tale, which strengthens rather than undermines the book’s credibility.

Who should read it

Short-term futures and index traders who want concrete, quantified pattern rules (with exact stop and profit figures) rather than qualitative chart reading, plus traders looking for a plain-English, worked-example introduction to fixed-fraction position sizing.
