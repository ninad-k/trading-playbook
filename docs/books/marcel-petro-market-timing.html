Summary

An engineer’s take on technical analysis: the author treats stock prices as a nonlinear dynamic system analogous to physical vibration (comparing price swings to a spring-mass system) and builds a toolkit around Bollinger Bands, moving-average crossovers, MACD, and Fibonacci-based objective points to identify cycle length and forecast turning points. Later chapters apply the same cyclic logic to portfolio construction using Markowitz mean-variance optimization and the Capital Market Line. Numerous stock examples are drawn from the 1999–2000 tech/dot-com period (Dell, Amazon, DoubleClick, AOL).

Key points

  • Bollinger Bands — 20-period moving average ± 2 standard deviations; captures ~95% of price data under a normal-distribution assumption; band narrowing (“squeeze”) often precedes a strong move.
  • %b indicator — Bollinger’s formula for where price sits between the bands, used to spot divergence between price and band position.
  • Moving-average crossover (“Golden Cross”) — a buy signal requires both a fast and slow moving average rising with the fast one crossing above the slow one; example used 20-day/40-day SMAs.
  • MACD — built from two EMAs (typically 12/26) with a 9-period EMA signal line; divergence between MACD peaks and price peaks flags a likely reversal.
  • Spring-mass cycle analogy — models a stock’s dominant price cycle using an analog to physical resonant frequency, with “stiffness” derived from Bollinger Band range ÷ average volume and “mass” from float × price, used to estimate how many days between cycle peaks.
  • Fibonacci objective points — projected price target (OP) and bounds (COP = 0.618×, XOP = 1.618× the prior swing) applied to the next expected peak or valley.
  • Nested moving-average/Bollinger bands — a fast (e.g., 10-day) and slow (e.g., 40-day) band pair used together to extrapolate future price envelopes.
  • Capital Market Line / efficient frontier — Markowitz portfolio optimization applied to select and weight stocks, with market-cap weighting favored under an efficient-market assumption.

Actionable rules

  1. Buy signal (Golden Cross): fast (e.g., 20-day) MA crosses above slow (e.g., 40-day) MA while both are rising.
  2. Use MACD divergence from price (MACD peak lower than a higher price peak, or vice versa) as an early warning of trend exhaustion.
  3. Treat Bollinger Band penetration as a powerful move, but expect price to revert toward the band or midline afterward.
  4. Project the next swing target using Fibonacci multiples (0.618, 1.0, 1.618) of the prior comparable swing.
  5. The “GBS method” (attributed to Gary B. Smith): buy a breakout to a new high on rising volume if no other peak has formed in the prior six weeks; expects roughly a 5% gain per trade; downside entries use a $1.00 breakout below the recent low.

Caveats

Self-published and not peer-reviewed; several core claims (spring-mass cycle analogy, resonant-frequency estimation of stock cycles) are the author’s own physics-inspired heuristics rather than established market theory, and no out-of-sample backtest results are given. Examples are concentrated in the 1999–2000 internet-stock bubble, a highly unusual volatility regime, which limits how representative the case studies are of normal markets.

Who it is for

Technically-minded readers comfortable with a physics/engineering framing of price cycles who want a Bollinger Bands + MACD + Fibonacci toolkit for swing timing, plus a light introduction to Markowitz portfolio construction.
