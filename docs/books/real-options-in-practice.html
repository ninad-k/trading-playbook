Summary

This Wiley Finance book applies financial option-pricing theory to corporate capital-budgeting decisions, not to trading securities. Its thesis: DCF analysis undervalues investment projects because it assumes a rigid plan and ignores the value of managerial flexibility to defer, expand, contract, abandon, or switch a project as new information arrives. Using the binomial option-pricing model as its core tool, the book works through six basic “real options” embedded in projects, then extends the analysis to competitive game-theory settings, contracts (joint ventures, licensing, auctions), and strategy communication, closing with newer applications (manufacturing flexibility, technology adoption).

Key points

  • Real option — management’s right, not obligation, to take a future action (invest, expand, abandon, switch) on a business asset, analogous to a financial call/put.
  • Option to defer — delaying a decision until uncertainty resolves; valued like a call, where waiting costs foregone cash flow but preserves the right to walk away.
  • Option to abandon — a put: the right to dispose of or repurpose an asset if outcomes are poor, capping downside.
  • Option to switch — flexibility to change inputs, outputs, or operating mode as relative prices/conditions change.
  • Option to expand/contract — the right to scale a project once initial uncertainty resolves.
  • Option to stage — splitting an investment into sequential tranches, each contingent on the prior stage (a compound option).
  • Binomial asset tree — the core tool: an asset’s value branches to a best- and worst-case outcome at each node, with a risk-neutral probability derived from the expected/min/max values and the risk-free rate.
  • Later chapters combine option value with game-theoretic competitive-response modeling — a rival’s likely reaction changes the value of waiting versus moving first.
  • Real option analysis is contrasted with capital budgeting (risk priced indirectly via discount rate) and portfolio analysis (risk assessed relative to existing holdings); it instead prices risk directly via probability-weighted payoffs discounted at the risk-free rate.

Actionable rules

None as trading rules — this is a capital-budgeting/valuation framework with no entries, exits, or position sizing. The methodological “rule”: (1) identify the embedded flexibility (defer, abandon, switch, expand/contract, stage), (2) build a binomial tree of best/worst-case asset value, (3) compute the risk-neutral probability from expected/min/max value and the risk-free rate, (4) discount the expected option payoff at the risk-free rate and compare to static NPV.

Caveats

Aimed at valuing physical/strategic projects (plants, joint ventures, R&D), not market trading — no strategy for trading securities is offered. The best-case/worst-case binomial simplification is easier to state than to calibrate for illiquid, non-traded corporate assets, which lack an observable market price or volatility unlike financial options. Worked numerical examples are illustrative, not real company outcomes.

Who it is for

Corporate finance professionals and strategy analysts evaluating capital investment decisions who want option-pricing rigor applied to managerial flexibility — not retail or professional securities traders.
