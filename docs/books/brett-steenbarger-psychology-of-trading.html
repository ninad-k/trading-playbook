Overview

Despite the filename, this document is not Brett Steenbarger’s 2003 Wiley book The Psychology of Trading. It is a large compiled archive of dozens of his short, stand-alone articles and columns (originally published on his website brettsteenbarger.com, in SFO Magazine, and elsewhere between roughly 2001 and 2005), several of which explicitly reference “my book, The Psychology of Trading (Wiley, 2003)” as a separate, already-published work. Each article ends with the same recurring author bio, confirming the compilation structure. The content is genuine and on-topic — Steenbarger applies his background as a clinical psychologist and active index-futures trader to recurring trading-performance problems — but it should be read as a reader/anthology of short pieces rather than a single continuous book argument.

Core thesis

Steenbarger’s throughline across the articles is that trading is a performance activity, not primarily a battle against emotion: expertise, consistency, and “the zone” come from deliberate, structured practice and rule-governance, the same way they do for athletes, musicians, and elite performers in any field. He repeatedly argues that most “psychological problems” traders bring to coaching are not deep-seated emotional pathology but self-reinforcing patterns the mind constructs out of ordinary variance (a losing streak becomes “I am a bad trader,” an insomnia episode becomes “I have a sleep problem”), and that treating these constructed problems too directly often reinforces them. His practical answer is a mix of solution-focused brief-therapy technique, cognitive-behavioral self-monitoring, and rules that offload decision-making from the emotionally compromised, in-the-moment brain onto a pre-built structure.

Key concepts

  • Solution-focused perspective on trading problems — many trading “problems” (cold streaks, “insomnia,” lost discipline) are normal variance that becomes a self-reinforcing identity once the trader labels and dwells on it; treatment that focuses on the “problem” can make it worse.
  • The zone — a state of absorbed, low-self-consciousness performance; Steenbarger reviews research (Csikszentmihalyi’s flow, Maslow’s peak experiences) suggesting it results from mastery and structured practice, not from simply “clearing your mind.”
  • Trendiness measure — Steenbarger’s own quantitative indicator comparing directional movement to total movement over a lookback window (daily and a 45-minute intraday version), used to flag shifts in short-term trend before price confirms them.
  • Three vices of trading — perfectionism, ego-involvement, and (implied) related self-esteem-driven patterns that distort risk-taking and self-talk.
  • The cognitive journal / cue cards — a written record of triggering situations and one’s automatic thoughts, used to interrupt negative thought patterns before/during trading.
  • Exposure methods — a therapeutic technique (mental rehearsal plus graduated real-world practice) adapted to desensitize traders to feared scenarios (large losses, volatile markets).
  • Rule-governance and the prefrontal cortex — rules reduce ambiguity and offload decisions onto pre-built structure, which Steenbarger links to executive-function (prefrontal cortex) research: emotional stress reduces blood flow to the prefrontal cortex, degrading exactly the judgment traders need in the moment.
  • OODA loop (Boyd) — Observe-Orient-Decide-Act, a military decision-making framework Steenbarger applies to trading, emphasizing rapid synthesis/destruction of “mental images” (schemas) as new information arrives.
  • Lead fish — a qualitative breadth concept (from an anecdote with his daughter): watch the strongest/weakest stocks in a basket as leading indicators of a broader market turn.
  • Well-being vs. distress balance — Steenbarger’s view (via his Market Psychology Questionnaire) that trading problems arise as much from an absence of positive emotional states as from the presence of negative ones.

Rules and setups

The collection is overwhelmingly about psychology rather than a codified trading system, but one article (“My Trading System: A Brief Summary,” dated Dec. 2003) gives a concrete, if non-mechanical, framework for intraday ES (S&P e-mini) futures trading:

  1. Three inputs: short-term Trend, Institutional Buying/Selling Activity (via NYSE TICK and breadth), and Momentum.
  2. Trend-following mode: in an established short-term trend, buy pullbacks in uptrends / sell bounces in downtrends, using historical profiles of similar past pullbacks/bounces to time entries (the system is “non-mechanical” because these profiles are continuously re-estimated).
  3. Breakout mode: when trend is neutral (range-bound), take a breakout to a new high/low only if it is confirmed by TICK strength and at least half of a followed stock basket also registering fresh highs/lows; otherwise ignore or fade the move.
  4. Stop loss: averages 3 ES points, adjusted for volatility; moved to breakeven once the trade is profitable by an average of 1.5 points (also volatility-adjusted).
  5. Exit logic: primarily time-based (exit once an expected move’s typical duration/extent is reached and short-term momentum stalls), with two overrides — take profit early if volume/volatility expands unsustainably in the trade’s favor, or hit the fixed stop loss. Steenbarger notes the time-based “scratch” exit (getting out flat before a stalling trade becomes a loser) is, in his own observation, the most important of the three exit conditions.

Beyond this one system summary, the book gives no position-sizing formula or portfolio-level risk rules — see Risk section below.

Risk and money management

No systematic money-management framework (position sizing, percent-of-equity risk rules, portfolio construction) is presented across the collection; risk control is discussed almost entirely at the level of the single ES-futures system above (a ~3-point volatility-adjusted stop, moved to breakeven at ~1.5 points profit). Where risk is discussed elsewhere, it is framed psychologically rather than numerically — e.g., ego-driven overleveraging (trading size that makes losses “hurt” disproportionately) is identified as a driver of poor risk decisions, and Steenbarger’s “Trader’s Self-Evaluation Checklist” asks whether the trader has and strictly follows risk-management parameters, without prescribing specific ones.

Psychology and discipline

This is the entire substance of the collection; the exercises are its most reusable content:

  • A Trader’s Self-Evaluation Checklist — 10 questions covering self-talk quality, off-market review habits, the profit/loss impact of poor risk control, whether position size reflects genuine opportunity, revenge trading, premature profit-taking, whether trading fulfills non-trading emotional needs, and whether the trader can name a specific, maintained edge.
  • Market Psychology Questionnaire — a 24-item, 5-point self-rating scale (almost never to almost always) covering 8 emotional subscales (happiness, satisfaction, energy, attachment, depression, anxiety, anger, guilt), scored into a well-being total and a distress total; a well-being:distress ratio of 2:1 or better is framed as favorable, with a full worked scoring/interpretation guide, intended to be retaken periodically to track emotional trend over time.
  • Techniques for disrupting negative thought patterns — (1) Shifting gears: physically leave the trading station and change activity as soon as a negative pattern’s physical/emotional signature appears; (2) the cognitive journal and cue cards, used as a “pre-flight checklist” for mindset; (3) stress inoculation: mentally rehearsing and pre-combating negative reactions before they occur in live trading; (4) making negative self-talk patterns “the enemy” by attributing them (in imagination) to a disliked person, making it easier to argue back against them.
  • Keys to success with exposure methods — patience (fully relax before starting), persistence (repeat coping responses to the point they generalize), gradualism (start with manageable versions of the feared scenario before the hardest cases), consistency (apply daily, not sporadically), and realism (exposure removes negative patterns but does not substitute for real trading skill/research).
  • The three vices — perfectionism (chronic “not good enough” self-talk; antidote is to talk to yourself as you would to a good friend), ego (using trading P/L as a referendum on self-worth; antidote is diversifying sources of self-esteem outside trading), and a third, related theme of needing to be right rather than wanting to trade well.
  • Repetition and rule-governance — Steenbarger describes keeping a daily trading weblog/journal specifically to “get the variables out” of his own trading, reduce in-the-moment discretion, and create public accountability for mistakes.

Chapter map

The document has no formal chapter structure; it is an alphabetized/thematic run of independent articles. Representative pieces, grouped by theme:

  • Foundational aphorisms & self-assessment — “A Dozen Reflections on Life and Markets” (opening quotes); “A Trader’s Self-Evaluation Checklist”; “Market Psychology Questionnaire.”
  • Market reading / behavioral pattern recognition — “Accepting the Obvious”; “Anticipating Market Turns”; “Assessing Intraday Trendiness”; “Identifying Breakout Moves”; “Trusting the Rally”; “Profiles of Market Bottoms I: New Annual Lows.”
  • Performance psychology & the zone — “Understanding the Zone,” “Creativity and the Zone,” “Exemplary Achievement and the Zone” (a three-part sequence); “Becoming the Person You Know You Can Be”; “Repetition and Greatness.”
  • Clinical/therapeutic frameworks applied to trading — “How Problems Are Constructed”; “Why Self-Help Techniques Often Don’t Work” (solution-focused therapy); “Techniques for Disrupting Negative Thought Patterns”; “Keys to Success With Exposure Methods”; “The Three Vices of Trading.”
  • Cognitive science / decision-making — “How Experts Make Decisions Under Uncertainty” (Parts I–II); “Experts, Novices, and Trading Performance”; “Trading as Mental Warfare” (Boyd’s OODA loop); “Quantifying Mind: The Neuropsychology of Trading”; “The Psychology of Rules.”
  • Trading systems & practice — “My Trading System: A Brief Summary”; “Using Stops as a Psychological Tool”; “Micropsychology of the Markets”; “Fundamentals of Short-Term Trading, Part One”; “Fundamental Uncertainty in Trading.”
  • Resilience & personal accounts — “Responding to Adversity – A Personal Account”; “Seeking the Path of Least Comfort”; “Why We Trade”; “Will Your Portfolio Become a Prisoner of War? Lessons from History and Psychology.”

Strengths and caveats

The self-assessment tools (the Checklist and the Market Psychology Questionnaire) are unusually concrete and directly reusable compared to most trading-psychology writing — they give scoring keys, not just advice. The solution-focused framing of “constructed problems” is a genuinely distinct and useful lens not found in most trading books, which tend to treat every losing streak as a discipline failure to be fixed rather than possibly-normal variance being over-interpreted. The major caveat is bibliographic: this is not the book its filename claims to be — a reader looking specifically for the structured, chapter-by-chapter 2003 Wiley book The Psychology of Trading will not find it here; this is a looser, repetitive (the same author bio appears dozens of times) collection of stand-alone pieces of varying depth and date, some highly practical (the checklist, the trading system summary) and others more essayistic or anecdotal (“A Dozen Reflections,” personal accounts). Several articles reference specific, dated market technology (Globex sessions, specific data vendors, a research collaboration with MIT/Andrew Lo referenced as “already under way”) that place the material squarely in the early-to-mid 2000s.

Who should read it

Useful for traders who want concrete, low-effort self-assessment instruments (the Checklist, the Questionnaire) and short, actionable psychological techniques (cognitive journal, exposure methods, disrupting negative thought patterns) rather than a single sustained argument. Best paired with expectation-setting: treat it as a dip-in reference/article archive, not a linear book, and go to Steenbarger’s actual 2003 book or his later The Daily Trading Coach for the fuller, structured treatment this compiled document does not provide.

Related books in this library

  • Trading in the Zone: Master the Market with Confidence, Discipline, and a Winning Attitude — covers similar “trading is a mental performance game” territory from a non-clinical trader’s perspective; useful contrast to Steenbarger’s clinical/CBT framing.
  • Come Into My Trading Room — another practitioner combining psychology with concrete trading rules, for comparison of structure and depth.
  • Stock Market Wizards — interview-based accounts of trader psychology and discipline that complement Steenbarger’s more clinical framework.
  • Special Report on Money Management — supplies the systematic risk/position-sizing rules this collection largely omits.
