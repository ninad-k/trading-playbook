Summary

Michael Sheimo treats market axioms as prompts for analysis rather than universal laws. The 50 chapters span research, trend interpretation, objectives, trading practice, and fraud risk. This note synthesizes their recurring questions: why a stock moves, what success means for the position, and when a rule fits the company, market, and holding period. The 2005 charts are examples rather than current recommendations (PDF pp. 8-12).

Key points

  • Research precedes commitment. A price or volume jump can flag a candidate, but does not replace understanding the company and the market move (PDF pp. 12-18).
  • Objectives should specify expected return, risk, timeframe, and a measurable review standard. A stock that does not fit should not enter the portfolio (PDF pp. 89-91).
  • “Let winners run” still requires diagnosis. Broad-market weakness may justify patience, while declining sales, legal trouble, higher rates, or impaired future earnings can justify selling (PDF pp. 92-93).
  • Avoid overtrading by recording the reason for each transaction, auditing losing streaks, and looking for compulsive activity patterns (PDF pp. 164-167).
  • Follow a small set of companies deeply enough to track market direction, sector themes, and earnings expectations without information overload (PDF pp. 168-174).
  • Emotional attachment is not evidence; reassess management, financing, products, and lawsuits when a holding deteriorates (PDF pp. 175-177).

Actionable rules

The following rules paraphrase Sheimo’s recommendations in the reviewed chapters:

  1. Write an objective before buying: target outcome, acceptable risk, horizon, and a measurable review standard (PDF pp. 89-91).
  2. Record the thesis for every trade and compare monthly activity with results; reduce activity when churn is not producing value (PDF pp. 164-167).
  3. Separate planning from execution. Once a reviewed order is ready, place it without adding last-second distractions (PDF p. 178).
  4. Maintain transaction and account records so cost basis, open orders, and performance remain auditable (PDF pp. 179-181).

How the book is organised

Sheimo groups the 50 axioms into four parts. Research covers getting information before investing, why doubling is easier at low prices, company buybacks, the volume/price relationship and bellwether stocks. Analysis covers “it’s always a bull market,” divergence in trends, trend persistence, insider trading, order types, institutional activity, support and resistance, and the recurring bear-market warning. Strategy is the largest part and holds the familiar contradictory pairs — sell losers and let winners run, buy low sell high, buy high sell higher, sell high buy low, never short the trend, buy the rumour and sell the news, buy the stock that splits, buy weakness and sell strength, average up rather than down, and buy Monday sell Friday. Later parts continue into portfolio and behavioural axioms.

The book’s method is to state each axiom, explain the reasoning behind it, then test whether it still holds. The preface is explicit that several no longer do — “Buy on Monday, Sell on Friday” is reported as statistically weaker after 1990 than before it; buybacks may benefit the company without benefiting the shareholder; and bellwethers are described as inconsistent, sometimes turning ahead of the market and sometimes with or after it. Sheimo’s stated point is that knowing an axiom is not enough: “sell the losers and let the winners run” is useless until the reader can say how a loser is identified and when profit-taking becomes appropriate. The charts illustrate the concepts rather than serving as recommendations (PDF pp. 6-12).

Caveats

This focused review covers the overview and selected strategy and risk chapters, not every axiom. Examples and market structure reflect the early 2000s; current rules, trading technology, and costs require separate verification.

Who it is for

Newer stock investors who want a decision framework for evaluating common market advice.
