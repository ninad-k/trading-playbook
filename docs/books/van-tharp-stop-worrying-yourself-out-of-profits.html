Stop Worrying Yourself Out of Profits

Van K. Tharp · 2005 · Tier B — concise note article beginner

Summary

An excerpt from Van Tharp’s course volume “How to Control Stress to Become a More Successful Investor.” Tharp distinguishes two components of trading stress: a biological fight-flight response (useful for physical survival, not markets) and a psychological component — worry — which he argues does far more damage to performance. Using an illustrative memory-recall exercise, he argues worry consumes limited conscious processing capacity needed to read the market accurately, and also distorts perception itself, causing traders to selectively notice information matching their fears. Five hypothetical commodity traders reacting differently to the same soybean price drop illustrate that P&L outcome and emotional experience are independent — what matters is interpretation, not the event. The article closes with two exercises for identifying a trader’s personal “worry loop” so it can be deliberately interrupted and replaced.

Key points

  • Two stress components: biological fight-flight (narrows focus, physiological) and psychological worry (fear of failure, negative self-evaluation).
  • Worry consumes conscious processing capacity: students worrying about test performance devote roughly 25% of conscious thought to the worry rather than the task.
  • Worry is self-fulfilling: “if you worry about what will happen if you make a mistake, you probably will make that mistake.”
  • Perception is an active filter shaped by expectation — a trader expecting a bull market selectively notices bullish patterns; worry is a negative-expectation version of the same filtering.
  • The five-trader vignette shows P&L outcome and emotional distress are not the same thing — some losers felt fine, some winners were anxious.
  • Worriers fall into a self-reinforcing loop: negative self-talk produces bad feelings, which produce more negative self-talk.
  • Two techniques to find a personal worry pattern: replaying a past loss as if watching a movie of yourself, and keeping a real-time “worry diary” of trigger, self-talk/imagery, and resulting loop.

Actionable rules

  1. When worry arises while trading, consciously name it before acting, to preserve processing capacity for market information.
  2. Keep a worry diary logging the trigger, self-talk/imagery, and loop each time it occurs; review after several weeks.
  3. Use the “movie replay” technique on a specific past loss to identify what triggered the worry.
  4. Deliberately interrupt an identified loop with a different voice, image, or action, and rehearse the replacement until automatic.
  5. “None given” for market/sizing rules — this is purely a self-management technique.

Caveats

Short (5 pages) and purely psychological — no market rules or system content; cited research is described generally without full citation detail. Meant to be applied alongside an existing trading system and risk plan.

Who it is for

Traders whose worry impairs decision-making or sleep, wanting a structured self-diagnosis exercise rather than a market technique.
