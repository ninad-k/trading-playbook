Summary

A short introductory research note from DailyFX (FXCM’s research arm), authored by then-Chief Strategist Kathy Lien, explaining Ralph Nelson Elliott’s wave theory: markets move in a five-wave impulsive sequence (in the direction of the larger trend) followed by a three-wave corrective sequence (A-B-C), with waves nested inside waves across timeframes. It states the three hard rules for a valid wave count and ties wave analysis to Fibonacci retracement levels for setting stops and targets, illustrated with a EUR/USD example.

Key points

  • Elliott Wave Theory (1930s) rests on the idea that price action reflects mass investor psychology and is therefore not perfectly random.
  • A basic bull cycle: Wave 1 (initial buying), Wave 2 (profit-taking correction), Wave 3 (typically the longest wave as more participants join), Wave 4 (shallow correction), Wave 5 (final, often overextended move often coinciding with an RSI divergence).
  • The corrective sequence that follows: Wave A (initial move that may look like “just a correction”), Wave B (a bear-market rally/short-covering bounce), Wave C (confirms the trend has reversed, often breaking key support).
  • Rule 1: Wave 2 can never retrace more than 100% of Wave 1.
  • Rule 2: Wave 4 can never overlap the price territory of Wave 1.
  • Rule 3: of Waves 1, 3, and 5, Wave 3 can never be the shortest — it is often the longest.
  • Fibonacci retracements are used to find likely turning points; a 38% retracement of Wave 1 by Wave 2 is called out as a popular relationship, with 50% and 61.8% also common.
  • The theory is described as popular in forex specifically because roughly 80% of FX volume is considered speculative, and waves are grounded in mass psychology rather than fundamentals.

Actionable rules

  1. Only count a wave sequence as valid if Wave 2 does not retrace more than 100% of Wave 1.
  2. Only count a wave sequence as valid if Wave 4 does not overlap Wave 1’s price range.
  3. Only count a wave sequence as valid if Wave 3 is not the shortest of Waves 1, 3, and 5.
  4. Trade with the larger trend: look to sell corrective waves within a larger downtrend and buy corrective waves within a larger uptrend (“the trend is your friend”).
  5. Use Fibonacci retracement levels (38%, 50%, 61.8%) of the prior wave to anticipate stop placement and reversal zones for the next wave.
  6. Watch for RSI divergence at the end of a Wave 5 as a warning that the impulsive move is exhausting.

Caveats

Very short promotional/educational research note (6 pages, one figure-heavy example) from a retail forex broker’s research desk (2004); it explicitly acknowledges Elliott Wave is “the least correctly understood theory of technical analysis” and highly subjective in practice — wave counts can be drawn multiple valid ways, and the guide does not address how to resolve ambiguous counts. No backtested performance data. Best read as a glossary of terms and rules rather than a complete trading method.

Who it is for

Beginners who want a concise, plain-language introduction to Elliott Wave terminology and its two hard counting rules before tackling a full-length Elliott Wave text.
