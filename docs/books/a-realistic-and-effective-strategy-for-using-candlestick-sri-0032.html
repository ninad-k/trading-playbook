A Realistic and Effective Strategy for Using Candlestick Charts

Tsutae Kamada · 2001 · Tier B — concise note article intermediate

Candlestick patterns are presented as confirmations alongside trend, moving averages, volume, and Fibonacci levels.

Summary

Kamada argues that candlesticks are a clear visual representation of open, high, low, and close, but their named patterns are not automatically better than equivalent bar-chart shapes (pp. 1–2). The central method is to combine candle structure with trendlines, moving averages, volume, and Fibonacci retracements. Long real bodies are treated as stronger directional evidence, while hammers, engulfing bars, shooting stars, harami, and dark-cloud cover are interpreted in the context of the prevailing trend (pp. 2–6). Stock examples illustrate the filter: bullish engulfing or gap-up candles failed in downtrending Sun Microsystems when price met its declining 50-day average or trendline (p. 4), while bullish harami and hammer breakouts near a 50-day or 20-day average preceded renewed advances in General Dynamics and Pepsi Bottling (pp. 5–6). The author says breakouts work better in an uptrend and recommends checking the stock and sector trend before trading a candle breakout (p. 7). A second set of examples combines a bearish shooting star with a 38.2% Fibonacci retracement in Intel and a bullish hammer at a 38.2% retracement in Greenpoint Financial (pp. 9–10). The conclusion is explicit: candlesticks are one tool, and multiple confirmations should precede a trade (p. 11).

Key points

  • Pattern names do not replace context; trend direction is the first filter (pp. 2, 4–7).
  • The examples use 20-day and 50-day moving averages and 38.2% retracements as confluence areas (pp. 5–6, 9–10).
  • No win rate or controlled comparison is reported.

Actionable rules

  1. Determine the stock and sector trend before using a candle; in a downtrend, the author looks for selling opportunities even when a bullish candle appears (pp. 4–7).
  2. Use a moving average, trendline, volume condition, or Fibonacci level as confirmation rather than trading the candle name alone (pp. 4–10).
  3. For the illustrated bullish Harami and Hammer setups, enter only after price exceeds the signal candle’s high (pp. 5–6, 10). The article does not specify a universal stop or position size.

Caveats

The evidence consists of selected stock charts and narrative examples, so selection bias is substantial. Candle definitions are partly subjective, and the suggested confirmation does not specify stops, sizing, costs, or a complete sample.

Who it is for

Useful for traders building a confluence-based candlestick hypothesis.
