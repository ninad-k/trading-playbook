Overview

John Piper, a UK futures trader and editor of the newsletter The Technical Trader, structures this book around what he calls the “Trading Pyramid”: a ten-level hierarchy running from personal psychology (You, Commitment, Discipline) up through Money Management and Risk Control, “the three simple rules,” system design, and finally operation and results. Section 1 builds this framework in detail; Section 2 applies it to specific, named intraday and swing setups built mostly on Peter Steidlmayer’s Market Profile (Minus Development, Value, Acceptance) rather than conventional indicators, which Piper is openly dismissive of.

Core thesis

Piper’s central claim is that trading skill is roughly 5% analysis and 95% the psychological/structural work of consistently applying a simple edge: cut losses, let profits run, and trade selectively. He argues Money Management (MM) — not entry technique — is what actually determines survival and profitability; a mediocre system with good MM survives, while even a near-perfect system with no MM eventually blows up. He is skeptical of most conventional technical analysis (including Elliott Wave, which he calls “the garbage of Elliott”) on the grounds that patterns must be statistically distinguishable from a 50/50 random split to have any edge, and prefers Market Profile concepts because they describe who is trading (the “longer time frame participant”) rather than fitting shapes to charts.

Key concepts

  • The Trading Pyramid — Piper’s 10-level model: You → Commitment → Discipline → Money Management → Risk Control → The Three Simple Rules → System Parameters → Your System/Methodology → Operation → Profits/Losses; each level must be built before the next is stable.
  • The three simple rules (“trading secrets”) — cut your losses, run your profits, trade selectively (Piper adds a fourth, informal one: trade with the trend).
  • Minus Development (MD) — a Market Profile concept: price levels rejected quickly (spike highs/lows, failed gaps) that show determined buying or selling and make natural stop-placement references.
  • Value / Value Area — the price range where most volume/time was accepted by the market (Market Profile); Piper’s rule of thumb is to sell above value and buy below it.
  • Acceptance — whether the market stays at or rejects a price level after testing it; used to judge whether a breakout or trend is “real.”
  • Low-risk trading opportunity — Piper’s filter for all his setups: an entry where the stop can be placed close to entry (small defined risk) with asymmetric upside if the trade works.
  • Key levels — round numbers, prior major support/resistance, or widely-watched targets where the “longer time frame participant” is likely to act.
  • The 55 steps / Evolution of a Trader — Piper’s staged model of trader development through three phases: greed-orientated, fear-orientated, risk-orientated (paralleling emotional → mechanical → intuitive trading).
  • Bill Williams’ brain model — left hemisphere (management/experience), right hemisphere (intuition), and “core” (operational ability); trading well requires all three in balance (“Profitunity”).
  • Random reinforcement — the market’s tendency to occasionally reward bad habits and punish good discipline, which Piper cites as the reason bad habits are so hard to unlearn.
  • Edge — Piper states his own methodology’s edge at roughly 60–70% (conservatively modeled at 55% for MM illustration), stressing that without a demonstrable edge no MM scheme can save a trader.

Rules and setups

Piper’s Money Management worked example: risking 10% of an “at-risk” $10,000 sub-account per trade (itself 20% of a $50,000 total account) equals a maximum single-trade loss of about 2% of total capital — his stated ceiling for any one trade. Position sizing on a new system: start with one contract only until live results confirm the edge, then scale up in steps. His Section 2 sets out named, code-worded low-risk setups, each with a defined stop:

  1. Gap open (“Goose”) — trade in the direction of the established trend when the market gaps and shows a fast move away from the open within the first ~90 minutes; stop is a fixed distance (e.g. a few points) beyond the opening high/low.
  2. Failed breakthrough (“Goat”) — a key level (round number, support/resistance) is broken by a small amount (often 1–10 points), then reclaimed; enter in the direction of the reclaim; stop beyond the failed breakout’s extreme.
  3. Failed re-test (“Snake”) — price re-tests a prior key high/low, fails to exceed it, and reverses; enter counter to the immediate move; stop just beyond the level being tested. Piper flags this as contrarian and lower-conviction than the Goat.
  4. Square congestion (“Box”) — on a 5-minute futures chart, a sideways range where highs/lows are repeatedly re-touched (“squared”); trade the breakout in either direction; stop at the opposite side of the range.
  5. Key levels (“Piano”) — a filter, not a standalone entry: signals (gaps, failed breaks, etc.) occurring at long-term support/resistance or round numbers are given extra weight.
  6. Elliott Fives (“Illusion”) — treat a completed 5-wave Elliott move as a trend signal in the direction of the five; enter on a low-risk setup in that direction, or when the lows/highs of the five are taken out; can also mark trend exhaustion.
  7. Aborted patterns (“Platypus”) — when a widely-recognized pattern (e.g. an Elliott five, head-and-shoulders, or a failed breakthrough) breaks down before completing, fade the crowd positioned for the original pattern.
  8. Trend following (“Horse”) — define trend via Market Profile (up in the absence of initiating selling, down in the absence of initiating buying); stay with it using any low-risk entry as a way in, giving the trend “benefit of the doubt,” exiting only on confirmed initiating action against it.
  9. Corrective action — treat pullbacks that fail to show real initiating counter-trend action as continuation setups; re-enter with the trend once the correction is judged exhausted.

Risk and money management

Piper treats MM as more important than analysis and states flatly that a system with a 99% historical win rate still fails if a trader risks 100% of capital per trade. His stated risk ceiling is 1–2% of total capital per trade (illustrated via a worked example: $1,000 max loss on a $50,000 account, equal to 20 points on one S&P e-mini contract). He recommends a target hit rate of roughly 50% combined with a profit/loss ratio of at least 2.5:1 (average winner at least 2.5x average loser) as the arithmetic baseline for a viable system. Additional risk controls: trade smaller size than feels comfortable (“halve your position size” is cited as market-wizard advice), avoid holding through news, overnight, or weekends without a profit buffer, and treat options-buying and futures/option-writing as carrying structurally different risk profiles (a bought option’s maximum loss is capped at the premium; a written option or an outright futures position is not).

Psychology and discipline

Piper frames trading psychology as the dominant skill (analysis is “for building your system,” not for predicting markets) and repeatedly stresses that ordinary life instincts — take pleasant things immediately, defer unpleasant ones — are exactly backwards for trading, producing the classic “cut profits, run losses” failure mode. He walks through why cutting losses is emotionally hard (ego, self-esteem, denial, “random reinforcement” of bad habits) and argues running profits is a nearly opposite skill (passive vs. active) that should only be attempted once loss-cutting is mastered. He recommends working with a trading coach (Adrienne Toghraie is cited as a resource), treating the market as a mirror of the trader’s own psychology, and building a personalized system precisely because “there is no absolute truth” in markets — only useful, individually-fitted beliefs.

Strengths and caveats

The Trading Pyramid gives a genuinely coherent, hierarchical way to think about why systems fail even when analysis is sound — an organizing idea many trading books lack. The Money Management chapter is unusually concrete, with worked arithmetic (binomial odds of consecutive losing streaks at a given win rate) that a reader can replicate. On the caveats side: several named setups (Goat, Snake, Box) are given with only loosely defined triggers (“a fairly good move,” “fairly close” stops) and Piper explicitly admits he has not done formal statistical testing on some of them (e.g. failed breakthroughs); the book is UK-FTSE/S&P-futures-centric and dated in places (references to specific data-feed vendors, pre-decimalization tick values); and Piper’s dismissal of Elliott Wave sits oddly alongside his own use of “Elliott Fives” as a named setup two chapters later — a minor internal contradiction he acknowledges but doesn’t fully resolve.

Who should read it

Best suited to intermediate futures/index traders who already have a workable edge or system idea and are struggling with consistency, position sizing, or discipline rather than needing a first system. The Market Profile-based setups will appeal most to intraday/short-swing index and futures traders; readers with no interest in the Market Profile framework will get more value from the first half (Pyramid, MM, psychology) than the second.
