The 4x1 Strategy and Median Grid

Dirk du Toit · 2004 · Tier A — deep note system intermediate

Du Toit's core forex day-trading method: one currency, low gearing with multiple staggered entries, one direction only, entered within a self-drawn support/resistance grid divided into four quadrants.

What it is

The 4x1 Strategy is du Toit’s discretionary forex day-trading method, named for its four constraints: one currency, one lot (low gearing), one direction, one-percent profit edge. Median Trading is the methodology used to deploy it: a manually-drawn horizontal support/resistance grid (“comfort zone”) on a single pair, split into four price quadrants, giving a fixed reference for judging whether price is relatively high or low. The two combine - 4x1’s rules for what/how much/which direction to trade are executed using the grid’s quadrants to decide where to enter. Premise: FX prices oscillate around a median band most of the time and periodically overshoot to test the grid’s extremes, and low leverage with multiple staggered entries (cost averaging) beats one precisely-timed entry with tight stops.

Rules

Instrument and direction 1. Trade one pair at a time (EUR/USD, occasionally GBP/USD); avoid non-USD crosses (thinner liquidity, less transparent fundamentals). 2. Establish a medium/long-term fundamental trend view (the “one-way play”) and trade exclusively in that direction.

Building the grid 3. Use a 60-minute chart with ~3-4 weeks of history. Draw horizontal lines at the clearest support and resistance; typical width ~300-400 pips (EUR/USD) or ~400-500 pips (GBP/USD), ~2.5-3% of price. 4. Identify the median band (~20-40 pips) where price action concentrates. Divide the grid into four equal quadrants (Q1 bottom through Q4 top).

Entries (in an uptrend) 5. Q1/Q2 (bottom half): primary buying zone. Q3 (just above median): continuation buys at reduced size. Q4 (top): high-risk, breakout-only. 6. Enter on price levels (20-40 pip zones near a quadrant boundary), not pinpoint timing - intraday moves are treated as noise around the fundamental direction. 7. Use multiple staggered entries: as price dips into lower quadrants, add at each new level (cost averaging), broadening the position like a base-up pyramid.

Sizing 8. Cap gearing at ~2:1-3:1 per entry; total exposure across all entries can run higher (10:1-15:1 in worked examples) but only via several small entries, never one large position. 9. Reduce gearing higher in the grid; increase willingness to add size lower in the grid.

Exits 10. Take profit at an “acceptable” gain (~30-40 pips, ~1% of capital at 3:1 gearing per entry) as a repeatable target, not a ceiling. 11. Let profitable entries run through adverse dips if the original setup is unchanged; exit for loss when that fundamental setup no longer holds - not on a fixed pip distance. 12. On a confirmed break of the grid’s extreme, expect a new grid ~300-400 points further out; wait for it to establish or scale out immediately.

Risk

Gearing discipline substitutes for tight stops: a 20:1-geared position with 30-pip stops was wiped out by a 175-pip adverse move, versus 1:1 gearing needing a 2,500-pip move for the same loss. Set a maximum drawdown tolerance (example: 25%) but act well before it - start asking “when” at 10%, act by 5% (scale out, reduce gearing, or hedge). Stops, if used, sit beyond genuine support/resistance, never at a fixed tight distance (30-40 pips is called too tight for normal FX volatility). Avoid tight positions around scheduled data releases. Hedging is an advanced tool, not recommended for novices.

Caveats

This is discretionary, not mechanical: grid placement, “acceptable” profit, and whether the “fundamental setup” still holds are trader judgment calls, not a fixed formula. The author had not backtested or automated the method at time of writing and flags, without resolving, curve-fitting risk in his own automation efforts. Pip widths and leverage assumptions reflect mid-2000s EUR/USD and GBP/USD norms and should be recalibrated (e.g., as % of price/ATR) for other regimes or instruments.
