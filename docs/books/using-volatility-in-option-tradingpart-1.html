Using Volatility in Options Trading, Part 1

Guy Bower · Tier B — concise note article intermediate

An introduction distinguishes statistical volatility from option-specific implied volatility.

Summary

Bower introduces two volatility measures used in option analysis. Statistical or historical volatility measures past movement in the underlying through standard deviation, commonly over 20 or 30 days, and is expressed as a percentage (p. 1). Implied volatility is the volatility input that makes a pricing model, such as Black–Scholes, reproduce an option’s current market price. It therefore depends on strike, underlying price, remaining life, call or put status, dividends or coupons, interest rates, and cost of carry (pp. 1–2). Because each strike has its own premium, each listed strike can have its own implied volatility (p. 2). A chart comparing 20-day statistical volatility with at-the-money implied volatility is suggested as a way to inspect volatility highs, lows, consolidation, and speed of change (p. 3).

Key points

  • Historical volatility looks backward; implied volatility embeds the market price of future uncertainty.
  • Volatility is an input to pricing, not a directional forecast.

Actionable rules

  1. None given. This first article defines volatility measures but defers trading ideas to a later article.
  2. For the comparison it proposes, chart a consistent historical-volatility window—20 or 30 days are described as common—alongside at-the-money implied volatility, while keeping strike and expiry distinctions explicit (pp. 1–3).

Caveats

The article is an introduction with no strategy test or discussion of volatility surface dynamics, skew, jumps, or model error.

Who it is for

Option traders learning the basic language of volatility.
