Overview

A short, plainly-written 1970s stock-selection book built around a single idea: prices move only from an imbalance of buyers and sellers, and that imbalance can be measured directly from a stock’s own daily open/high/low/close/volume data rather than from charts, fundamentals, or moving averages. Williams walks through why he rejected classic charting and fundamental analysis, presents his own accumulation/distribution volume formula (the “Million Dollar Formula”) alongside a simpler comparative-strength price-pattern method, then layers on market-timing indices, cycle analysis, and a set of numeric trading and stop-loss rules for turning the stock selection into actual trades.

Core thesis

A stock’s price is set entirely by the balance of buying versus selling pressure, and “professional” (informed, well-capitalized) money can be distinguished from “public” (uninformed) money by comparing a stock’s price action to the broad market and by measuring what fraction of each day’s trading volume was buying versus selling. When a stock’s accumulation/distribution line diverges from its price — holding up while price falls, or failing to confirm a new price high — that divergence has forecasting value the price chart alone does not show.

Key concepts

  • Accumulation — professional (informed) buying that shows up as a stock holding up better than the market on down days and rallying harder on up days, often while price itself looks weak.
  • Distribution — the mirror case: professional selling that shows up as a stock lagging the market on up days, often while price still looks strong.
  • Comparative strength (price-pattern method) — overlaying a stock’s chart against a broad market average (Dow, S&P, NYSE Composite) to spot bullish or bearish divergence between the two, used as a fast first-pass screen across many stocks.
  • The A/D (accumulation/distribution) formula — Williams’ core volume-based tool: (close − open) / (high − low) × volume = net daily buying/selling pressure, cumulated into a running flow line (see Accumulation/Distribution Stock Selection System).
  • Divergence between price and the A/D line — the central buy/sell signal: a new price low not confirmed by a new A/D-line low (bullish), or a new price high not confirmed by a new A/D-line high (bearish).
  • Immediate profit signal — a sharp one-day surge in the A/D line while price is still consolidating in a narrow range; treated as an advance tip-off of the direction price will break out.
  • Payout-time formula — Williams’ preferred fundamental filter: the number of years for a company’s earnings per share, compounded at its current growth rate, to equal the current stock price; lower payout time is more attractive.
  • Yield extremes — using a stock’s own 10-20 year historical yield range (not an absolute yield level) to flag overvalued (low relative yield) tops and undervalued (high relative yield) bottoms.
  • Will-Go index / short and intermediate-term timing indices — a family of overbought/oversold breadth-style indices (built from advances/declines and most-actives data) used to time entries into the stocks already screened for accumulation.
  • Buy on down days, sell on up days — Williams’ timing overlay: once a stock passes the selection checklist, wait for a sharp adverse market day to actually execute, rather than chasing confirmation.

Rules and setups

  1. Screen 1 — comparative price pattern. Overlay a stock’s price chart against a broad market average. Accumulation candidate: stock holds up on market weakness and outpaces the market on rallies (higher highs/higher lows while the market makes new lows). Distribution candidate: the reverse. The longer the divergence persists (days vs. weeks vs. months), the longer the resulting move is expected to last.
  2. Screen 2 — accumulation/distribution formula (the core system). See Accumulation/Distribution Stock Selection System for the full buy/sell rules.
  3. Master check-list (must be 100% satisfied before any trade): (1) is this a short- or long-term move — which indices apply; (2) has the market timing index reached a buy/sell zone; (3) is the stock selected on its comparative price accumulation pattern; (4) does the stock’s own daily A/D line confirm accumulation; (5) is this stock’s divergence the strongest among all candidates followed. Williams states trades that don’t meet all five criteria are “high risk” and consistently cost him money.
  4. Entry timing. Buy only on a sharp down day for the broad market (Williams cites a 7-10 point DJIA decline as an example) once all screening criteria are met; sell/short only on a sharp up day. Buying on up days is, in his words, wrong “80% of the time.”
  5. Minimum price filter. Do not trade any stock under $30/share — Williams found the A/D formula unreliable on low-priced, retail-dominated, easily-manipulated issues.
  6. Position count. Concentrate capital in the 3 best-ranked stocks that pass the full checklist (using odd lots if necessary) rather than diversifying across many names; follow no more than roughly 10-15 stocks actively at all (he personally tracked ~15, down from 40 when running an advisory service).
  7. Trailing stop. Set an initial stop 2 points below the stock’s most recent base/bottom; as new higher bases form, raise the stop to sit 2 points below the new base. Never lower a stop once set. Stops must be actual broker orders, not mental stops (“mental stops don’t work”). Elsewhere he refers to a “3-point must” stop system as the non-negotiable minimum discipline.
  8. Seasonal selling bias. Friday is historically the strongest market day (up ~65% of the time per Williams’ data) and Monday historically the weakest; he times short sales for late Friday afternoon into stocks that have rallied that day. The trading day before a market holiday is up roughly 70% of the time.
  9. Five common causes of loss to avoid: (1) plunging — using all capital plus margin on a single position; (2) overcomplicating with sophisticated derivatives/spreads instead of simple long/short; (3) demanding the exact high or low of a move instead of acting within the range; (4) letting losses ride instead of exiting after ~3 consecutive days of confirmed adverse movement; (5) trading on emotion rather than the checklist.
  10. Profit handling. Let winners ride; cut losers. Reinvest realized gains rather than withdrawing them, to compound position size over time (worked example: withdrawing a $500 gain versus reinvesting it changes the dollar size of the next 20% gain from $100 to $300).

Risk and money management

Risk control in this book is almost entirely about stop discipline and position concentration rather than percentage-of-equity sizing: a hard, broker-placed trailing stop set 2 points under the latest price base (never loosened, only tightened as new bases form), combined with a strict rule to cut a position after roughly three consecutive days of confirmed adverse price action rather than hoping for a recovery to breakeven (illustrated with a cautionary example of an investor who rode a stock from 65 down to $4 waiting for it to “come back”). Williams explicitly warns against margin-fueled “plunging” and against over-diversifying attention across too many names — he prefers concentrating in the 3 strongest checklist-qualifying stocks. The $30/share price floor is itself a risk-management filter, intended to avoid the more erratic, manipulation-prone trading patterns of low-priced stocks where his A/D formula is less reliable.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s psychological guidance centers on patience and mechanical adherence to the checklist: Williams recounts personally losing about $3,000 in a trade where he ignored his own rules out of impatience, and uses it to argue that emotional decision-making — not the method itself — is the main source of trading losses. He repeatedly stresses “sitting on your hands” until every checklist criterion is met, resisting the urge to buy on confirmation (which he argues means buying too late, after the professionals have already moved), and separating emotional attachment to a “pet stock” from what the accumulation/distribution data is actually showing. He frames speculation as a business requiring dedication of daily time to updating the indicators, not sporadic attention.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — My Million Dollar Stock Market Concept — why charts, fundamentals, and standard moving-average systems failed him; introduces yield and growth-rate (payout time) fundamentals filters.
  • Ch 2 — My First Tool for Selecting the Best Stocks — the comparative price accumulation/distribution pattern method.
  • Ch 3 — My Second Tool for Selecting Stocks — tracking the daily open/high/low/close supply-demand battle; introduces volume.
  • Ch 4 — How to Profit from the Accumulation Distribution Formula — full derivation and worked example of the “Million Dollar Formula,” basic buy/sell signals, and the immediate profit signal.
  • Ch 5 — Should You Follow the Short or Intermediate Term Trends and How to Do It — short-term overbought/oversold indices and the “Will-Go” intermediate-term index.
  • Ch 6 — How Cycles Can Improve Your Stock Timing — individual stock cyclical (bottom-to-bottom) patterns.
  • Ch 7 — What You Need to Know About Long Term Stock Market Timing — identifying selling climaxes and major tops/bottoms with technical and fundamental indicators; a “master pattern” long-term cyclical thesis.
  • Ch 8 — How to Combine Market Timing and Stock Selection — the master check-list; buy on down days/sell on up days.
  • Ch 9 — How to Know When It’s Time to Sell — distribution warning signs, Friday/pre-holiday seasonal selling bias, trailing stops.
  • Ch 10 — How to Begin Using My Methods — building the tracking notebook, stock universe selection ($30 floor, growth/flyer categories).
  • Ch 11 — How to Start Making Money in the Market…Tomorrow Morning — the 7-step summary process, a stock-rating matrix, profit reinvestment.
  • Ch 12 — Priceless Trading Hints — order execution timing, stop placement, day-to-day and intraday (three-hour cycle) forecasting.

Strengths and caveats

The accumulation/distribution formula and comparative-strength screening method are fully specified and easy to code, and Williams is unusually candid about presenting worked numeric examples (including a real Bausch & Lomb trade) rather than vague chart pictures. The book is heavily dated: stock recommendations and price levels are explicitly from 1971-72 (pre-decimalization prices in eighths, single-digit commission-era trading, a $30/share floor that meant something very different before decades of splits and inflation), and several named “growth” and “flyer” stocks from the example lists (Polaroid, Itek, Control Data) no longer exist in their described form. The seasonal Friday/Monday and pre-holiday statistics are drawn from data through the early 1970s and are not re-verified against later markets. Some claims (e.g., specific yield and payout-time thresholds constituting “the only two reliable measures of fundamental value”) are asserted rather than statistically tested in the text, and the long-term “master pattern” cyclical thesis in Chapter 7 is speculative pattern-matching over a small number of historical cycles.

Who should read it

Traders and investors interested in the historical origins of volume-based accumulation/distribution analysis (a direct precursor to On Balance Volume-style indicators) who want the original, fully worked formula and screening rules, understanding that price levels, named stocks, and some seasonal statistics need updating for modern markets.

Related books in this library

  • How I Made $2,000,000 in the Stock Market — Nicolas Darvas’s contemporaneous box-theory method shares Williams’ core premise that price/volume action alone, not fundamentals or charting folklore, drives entries and exits.
  • Reminiscences of a Stock Operator — an earlier account of reading tape and volume for signs of professional accumulation/distribution, the tradition Williams’ formula formalizes.
  • A Short Course in Technical Trading — Kaufman’s more modern, rigorously backtested treatment of moving averages, momentum, and volume-based indicators complements Williams’ original but less quantitatively tested framework.
