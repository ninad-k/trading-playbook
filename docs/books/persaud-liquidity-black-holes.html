Liquidity Black Holes

Avinash Persaud · 2002 · Tier B article intermediate

A short presentation arguing that liquidity is not a stable, single number but a variable state that can vanish suddenly ('liquidity black holes'), and that liquidity requires diversity of market participants.

Summary

A slide-deck presentation by Avinash Persaud (State Street Global Markets) making the case that liquidity should be measured by the price impact of trading, not by quoted bid-ask spreads, and that liquidity is inherently unstable — it can disappear in sudden episodes (“liquidity black holes”) that have become more frequent even as markets have grown larger.

Key points

  • Liquidity = speed, ease, and cost of transacting; quoted spreads are a poor proxy because they don’t capture price impact of larger trades.
  • Emerging-market buy/sell price-impact data (1997-2002) shows liquidity varies significantly and unpredictably over time.
  • “Liquidity black holes” are measured as the number of days per quarter that a broad stock index (US, Japan, UK) moves by more than 2 standard deviations from its average daily move; the frequency of these episodes has been rising, even as markets have gotten bigger.
  • Liquidity needs diversity of opinion among participants; diversity has declined because information costs have fallen, firms behave more similarly, and market-sensitive (mark-to-market) risk-management systems push everyone toward the same trades at the same time.
  • “Liquidity needs losers” — for markets to remain liquid, someone has to be willing to be on the wrong side of a trade; regulators pushing risk-reduction uniformly can itself reduce diversity and worsen liquidity crises.
  • Average number of liquidity black holes per quarter, with change from the prior “bubble” period, was tabulated across FTSE 100, UK/US/JP 10-year bonds, S&P 500, Toronto SE, Topix, and several FX pairs — showing different asset classes have distinct liquidity dynamics.

Actionable rules

None given — this is a conceptual/academic presentation, not a trading system. Practical takeaway: don’t rely on current bid-ask spread as a liquidity signal for sizing positions; account for the possibility that liquidity can vanish abruptly (size positions and set stops assuming a liquidity black hole can occur), and be aware that periods when most market participants use similar risk models (e.g., VaR-based sizing) are more prone to synchronized de-risking and liquidity evaporation.

Caveats

Extremely condensed — a slide deck (365 words of extracted text) with charts that lack full data tables in places; conclusions are asserted rather than derived step-by-step. Dated to 2002; specific liquidity-black-hole frequencies would need updating for current markets.

Who it is for

Risk managers and traders who want a short conceptual primer on why liquidity should be treated as a variable, fragile condition rather than a constant, and why homogeneous risk management across a market can be self-defeating.
