Overview

Callum Henderson, an emerging-market currency strategist, wrote this as a practitioner’s synthesis rather than an academic text: it surveys the main schools of currency-rate theory (purchasing power parity, monetary, interest rate, balance-of-payments, portfolio-balance models), explains why they routinely fail to predict short-term exchange-rate moves, and then builds a more useful applied framework around four disciplines — currency economics, capital-flow tracking, technical analysis, and long-term valuation. The back half of the book is organized by market participant: how corporations should manage transaction/translation/economic currency risk, how institutional investors should set hedging benchmarks and add active-management alpha, and how speculators (hedge funds, prop desks, currency overlay managers) actually operate and how to trade more disciplined. A full chapter is devoted to modeling currency crises in pegged and floating-rate regimes.

Core thesis

No single analytical discipline — fundamentals, flow, technicals, or valuation — reliably predicts currency moves in isolation, because currency markets are dominated by non-profit-seeking participants (central banks, corporate treasuries, tourists) and short-term capital flow dynamics that classical efficient-market theory cannot explain. The practical answer is to combine all four disciplines into a single “signal grid” and only act when a majority of the readings agree, supplementing that grid with a risk-appetite indicator to catch tradeable moves when the grid itself is ambiguous (which Henderson says is most of the time). Currency risk management is not one-size-fits-all: a corporation’s transaction risk, an investor’s portfolio hedge ratio, and a speculator’s directional bet require different tools, benchmarks, and discipline even though they operate in the same market.

Key concepts

  • Speculative cycle — currencies (especially in emerging or pegged regimes) move through phases of capital inflow and real appreciation, followed by fundamental deterioration, a violent speculative reversal, and eventual recovery; recognizing the phase in progress is more useful than a static fair-value estimate.
  • Risk appetite / instability indicator — a composite measure (leverage, credit spreads, cross-asset correlation) of whether capital is flowing toward risk assets and high-carry currencies (“risk-seeking”/”risk-neutral”) or fleeing to havens like the Swiss franc and Japanese yen (“risk-averse”).
  • IMM Commitments of Traders (COT) report — weekly, publicly available net long/short futures positioning of “non-commercial” (speculative) accounts on the Chicago IMM, used as a proxy for broader interbank speculative positioning and a contrarian extreme-positioning signal.
  • Forward rate bias — the empirical finding that forward exchange rates are poor, and often negatively biased, predictors of the future spot rate, contradicting covered/uncovered interest rate parity theory — the basis for both the carry trade and the differential forward strategy.
  • Transaction, translation, and economic currency risk — a corporation’s three distinct exposure types: near-term payables/receivables, balance-sheet/subsidiary valuation on consolidation, and long-run competitiveness effects of exchange-rate shifts.
  • Currency hedging benchmark — the reference hedge ratio (0%, 100%, or a partial/symmetrical ratio such as 50%) an institutional investor or corporation manages around; symmetrical benchmarks allow active managers to add value in both directions.
  • Tracking error — the deviation of a hedged portfolio’s return from its benchmark; a 50% symmetrical benchmark carries roughly 70% (1/√2) of the tracking error of a polar (0% or 100%) benchmark.
  • Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) / FEER — trade-weighted, inflation-adjusted exchange rate measures used for long-term fair-value and competitiveness analysis.
  • J-curve — the short-run worsening followed by longer-run improvement of a trade balance after a currency depreciation, due to the lag between quantity and price effects.
  • Signal grid — Henderson’s four-cell decision framework (currency economics / flow / technical / long-term valuation, each scored buy or sell) used to filter which currency views are strong enough to act on.

Rules and setups

Concrete, numbered techniques given in the book (these are presented as existing institutional strategies Henderson documents and recommends, not proprietary inventions):

  1. Technical building blocks — moving averages of 20, 55, and 200 days (and 55/200 weeks) are the most widely used; a “golden cross” (55-day MA crosses above the 200-day MA) is read as bullish, a “death cross” (55-day below 200-day) as bearish. 14-day RSI with 70/30 overbought/oversold bands; 12/26-day MACD (with a 9-day signal line).
  2. Fibonacci retracement — corrective waves in currency pairs are said to retrace 38.2%, 50%, or 61.8% of the prior swing; Fibonacci fan lines mark the corresponding support/resistance.
  3. Signal grid rule — score currency economics, flow, technical, and long-term valuation each as buy/sell/neutral; only initiate a major new position when at least 3 of the 4 readings agree.
  4. Differential forward (hedging/trading) strategy — hedge (or go long the higher-yielding currency) at 100% when the interest-rate differential favors the hedger (forward points at a discount); hedge at 0% when the differential is a cost (forward points at a premium); under a 50% symmetrical benchmark, this becomes overweight/underweight the hedge by 50% either way.
  5. Trend-following currency overlay — using three moving averages of 32, 61, and 117 days (Lequeux and Acar, 1998): hedge 100% of the exposure if spot is above all three MAs; hedge one-third if spot is above exactly two of the three; otherwise remain unhedged (mirror-image for a directional short).
  6. Optimized carry trade — go long a basket of higher-carry currencies (short the lower-carry funding currencies) when the risk-appetite indicator is risk-seeking or risk-neutral; take half profit when the indicator shifts from risk-seeking to risk-neutral; close the position entirely and reverse to short the carry basket when the indicator turns risk-averse. Volatility-adjusted optimization further tilts toward low-volatility, high-carry currencies and away from high-volatility, low-carry ones.
  7. Emerging-market volatility signal — when an emerging-market currency’s implied volatility trades below that of a comparable developed-market pair, this has historically proven unsustainable and a good level to buy the emerging-market currency; implied EM volatility tends to fall only on EM currency strength but always rises on EM currency weakness.

Risk and money management

For corporations, Henderson frames risk management around three hedging styles — passive (a fixed, regularly executed hedge ratio with no currency view), tactical/selective (case-by-case, the default for most transactional risk), and strategic (a directional view held over a longer horizon than the underlying cash flow, used sparingly). Institutional investors are advised to avoid “polar” (0% or 100%) hedging benchmarks in favor of a partial or symmetrical (typically 50%) benchmark, which both reduces tracking error (to roughly 70% of the polar-benchmark level) and gives an active manager room to add value in either direction; a Corporate Risk Optimizer (CROP) framework is described for finding the cheapest hedge for a given risk tolerance across forwards, vanilla options, risk reversals, and low-delta call structures, measured via VaR and effective hedge rate. For speculators, Henderson’s explicit rule is to take fewer, higher-conviction positions (only when 3 of 4 signal-grid readings and/or the risk-appetite indicator agree), always define entry, exit, and stop levels from flow/technical evidence before entering, always cut losses, and never widen a stop to avoid realizing a loss. No fixed percentage-of-equity position-sizing formula is given for speculators.

Psychology and discipline

Chapter 9 (aimed at speculators) is explicitly behavioral: it lists heuristic-driven bias (assuming a trend that has run six weeks will run a seventh), frame dependence (loss aversion distorting how a position’s risk is perceived), and overconfidence after strings of gains as the three predictable failure modes. Henderson’s stated discipline is to remain emotionally detached from P&L, to treat rising confidence after a winning streak as a warning sign rather than validation, and to make trading discipline “at least as important as having the right view” — a currency speculator can be fundamentally correct and still lose money through poor discipline. He also stresses that not hedging is itself a speculative decision, and that corporate treasurers who decline to hedge are implicitly taking a currency view whether they acknowledge it or not.

Chapter map

  • Part One (Ch 1–4) — Theory and practice: PPP, monetary, interest-rate, balance-of-payments, and portfolio-balance exchange-rate models; currency economics; capital-flow tracking (COT report, proprietary flow models); technical analysis schools (charting, moving averages, RSI, MACD, Fibonacci, Elliott Wave, Gann).
  • Part Two (Ch 5–6) — Regimes and crises: fixed vs. floating exchange-rate regime sustainability; a phased model of currency crises under pegged and freely floating regimes.
  • Part Three (Ch 7–9) — The real world of the practitioner: corporate treasury FX strategy and hedging tools; institutional investor hedging benchmarks and active currency management (differential forward, trend-following, carry-trade optimization); the currency speculator’s motives, methods, and a discipline guide.
  • Ch 10 — Applying the Framework: recap and integration of currency economics, flow, technical, and long-term valuation into the signal grid and risk-appetite framework.

Strengths and caveats

The four-discipline signal grid and the specific, numbered active-management strategies (differential forward, 32/61/117-day trend-following overlay, carry-trade optimization) are unusually concrete for a macro/hedging text and are attributed to named academic sources (Lequeux and Acar 1998; Kritzman 1993; Bansal and Dahlquist 2000), which makes them easy to verify or backtest independently. The currency-crisis phase models (Ch 6) and the emerging-market risk factors (Ch 7–8) are grounded in the 1990s Asian, Russian, and LTCM episodes and remain a clear framework for pegged-regime breakdowns. Caveats: market statistics, spreads, and daily-volume figures (e.g., EUR-USD volume, IMM data tables) reflect the early-2000s market structure and are dated; the book predates the post-2008 near-zero-rate era that muted many carry-trade dynamics it describes, and predates the shift of most institutional FX flow to electronic/algorithmic execution, which has changed how flow and COT data should be interpreted. It is a hedging and macro-strategy text, not a short-term trading manual — there is no intraday execution or stop-placement detail beyond the general “define levels from flow and technicals” guidance.

Who should read it

Corporate treasurers, institutional portfolio managers responsible for currency-hedging policy, and macro-oriented currency speculators who want a structured, multi-discipline framework rather than a single indicator or chart pattern. Less suited to short-term technical or intraday traders looking for specific entry/exit mechanics, or to readers wanting purely academic exchange-rate theory without a practitioner’s overlay.
