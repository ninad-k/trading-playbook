Summary

A technical-analysis tutorial from the American Association of Individual Investors (AAII) Journal, January 2000, by staff analyst Wayne A. Thorp. It explains Gerald Appel’s Moving Average Convergence/Divergence (MACD) indicator — a trend-following tool built from two exponential moving averages that also behaves like an oscillator — and walks through its three standard uses: signal-line crossovers, overbought/oversold reading, and price/indicator divergence, illustrated with weekly stock charts (Columbia Energy, Texas Utilities, Cascade Natural Gas, Allegheny Energy).

Key points

  • MACD = EMA(shorter period) − EMA(longer period); the signal line is a 9-period EMA of the MACD line itself. Most common parameters: 12/26/9.
  • MACD above zero means the shorter EMA is above the longer EMA (bullish); below zero means bearish.
  • Crossover use: sell when MACD crosses below the signal line, buy when it crosses above; crossovers below the zero line are more significant bullish signals, confirmed once both lines cross above zero.
  • MACD crossovers are a lagging indicator (like moving averages) — best in strongly trending markets, prone to whipsaws in choppy/sideways markets.
  • Example on Texas Utilities (June 1997–Aug 1999): 5 round-trip trades, average gain 3.75%/trade; the trending trade returned 18.7% over 8 months, while 3 trades in choppy conditions all lost money.
  • Overbought/oversold use: no fixed numeric threshold (unlike RSI) — a security’s MACD extremes must be studied historically and can be asymmetric.
  • Divergence use: bearish divergence = MACD makes a new relative low while price does not (stronger if price makes a new high); bullish divergence is the mirror case. Divergences tend to precede reversals, more weight at overbought/oversold extremes.
  • Weekly MACD signals carry more weight than daily; a common technique is to only take a daily crossover if the weekly MACD isn’t already in the opposite condition.

Actionable rules

  1. Standard parameters: 12-period and 26-period EMA for the MACD line, 9-period EMA of MACD for the signal line (daily or weekly bars).
  2. Buy when MACD crosses above its signal line; sell/exit when MACD crosses below its signal line.
  3. Weight crossovers below the zero line more heavily as bullish reversal signals (and vice versa above zero for bearish).
  4. Before trading overbought/oversold MACD extremes, chart the indicator’s own history on that specific security to establish its typical high/low range — do not assume a universal number.
  5. Treat a divergence between MACD and price (especially at an extreme) as an early warning of trend reversal, not a standalone entry trigger.
  6. Filter daily MACD signals against the weekly MACD’s condition before acting on them.

Caveats

A short (5-page) educational magazine article, not a full trading system — it gives no stop-loss, position-sizing, or exit rule beyond the opposite crossover, and the single backtest example (5 trades on one stock) is illustrative, not a rigorous performance study. As the article itself notes, MACD crossovers underperform in non-trending markets, which it demonstrates with real losing trades rather than glossing over the weakness.

Who it is for

Beginner-to-intermediate traders who want a plain-English explanation of how and why the MACD works, including its documented failure mode in choppy markets.
