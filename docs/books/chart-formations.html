Summary

This short Commodity Trading School article defines common chart formations and gives simple breakout interpretations. It starts with rising and declining trendlines, support as a prior-low area where buying overcomes selling, and resistance as a prior-high area where selling overcomes buying (pp. 1–2). Channels may rise, fall, or move sideways between parallel boundaries. Triangles are described as converging highs and lows; the basic instruction is to place a buy on an upside break or a sell on a downside break, while the stated prior-trend context can alter the expected direction (pp. 3–4). Pennants, rising and falling wedges, and bull or bear flags are treated similarly, often using the unfilled opposite breakout order as a stop-loss reference (pp. 4–6). Reversal formations include 1-2-3 tops and bottoms, head-and-shoulders, double and triple tops or bottoms, and rounded turns (pp. 6–9). The article also classifies congestion and continuation patterns, breakaway, running, and exhaustion gaps, and gives Elliott-inspired Fibonacci retracement levels of 38.2%, 50%, and 61.8% (pp. 9–11).

Key points

  • Pattern interpretation depends on the preceding trend and the location of the breakout (pp. 3–9).
  • The article repeatedly presents directional anticipation rather than measured probabilities.
  • It warns that markets and participants change, so historical success may decline (p. 1).

Actionable rules

  1. Mark prior lows as potential support and prior highs as potential resistance before naming a formation (pp. 1–2).
  2. For triangles, pennants, and wedges, the article places a buy on an upside break and a sell on a downside break; its directional expectations depend on the prior trend (pp. 3–5).
  3. For bull and bear flags, it places orders at both boundaries and uses the unfilled opposite order as the stop (pp. 5–6). This is a source rule, not a tested edge; the article supplies no performance sample.

Caveats

This is a simplistic educational reference with no sample, performance test, or definitions precise enough to remove chartist discretion. Its statements about anticipated breakouts are hypotheses, and gap patterns are less transferable to continuously traded FX than to markets with formal sessions.

Who it is for

Useful as a beginner glossary before developing and testing a formal pattern specification.
