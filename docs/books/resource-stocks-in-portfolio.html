Noll Moriarty · 2001 · Tier B — concise note article intermediate

Summary

An article from Preview (December 2001), written by geophysicist Noll Moriarty for an exploration/resources industry audience, applying Markowitz portfolio theory to argue that resource stocks deserve a place in an investment portfolio despite chronically lower average returns. Using Reserve Bank of Australia data from 1980-2001 comparing Resources, Banks, and Industrials against the ASX/S&P 200, the piece shows resources returned only about 4% annually (vs. 14.2% for banks) with higher volatility (25.5% standard deviation), but argues that because resource returns correlate weakly with banks and industrials (average correlation 0.5 vs. 0.8 between banks and industrials), adding resources to a portfolio reduces total portfolio volatility more than the return sacrifice would suggest — making the combined portfolio more “efficient” in the Markowitz sense.

Key points

  • 1980-2001 annual returns: Banks 14.2% (std dev 17.0%), Industrials 11.5% (17.0%), Resources 4.0% (25.5%), ASX/S&P 200 8.2% (18.0%).
  • 1990-2001 annual returns: Banks 16.3% (15.2%), Industrials 9.7% (8.8%), Resources 6.5% (13.8%) — resources’ relative volatility improved versus the prior decade.
  • Average pairwise correlations (1980-2001): Banks/Industrials 0.8 (high), Resources/Banks 0.5 (lowest), Resources/Industrials 0.7.
  • A portfolio’s volatility is driven by covariance between holdings, not the weighted average of individual volatilities — combining even two high-volatility assets can lower total portfolio volatility if their correlation is low.
  • Worked example: a 50% banks / 50% industrials portfolio returned 12.4% annually (1990s) with 10.6% volatility, versus a 35/35/30 (banks/industrials/resources) mix returning 11.6% with only 9.3% volatility — lower risk for a similar return.
  • In one specific year both portfolios returned 17%, but the resource-inclusive portfolio still carried lower volatility (9.3% vs. 10.6%).
  • Typical allocation cited: sophisticated investors hold roughly 5-20% of a portfolio in resource stocks.
  • Mining’s GDP-to-employment ratio (4.3) is cited as evidence resources are economically significant relative to their public visibility, despite being an “unfashionable” sector for retail investors.

Actionable rules

  1. Do not evaluate a candidate holding purely on its standalone average return or volatility — evaluate its correlation with existing portfolio holdings before including or excluding it.
  2. Target the “efficient frontier”: for a given desired volatility, select the asset mix (including lower-return, low-correlation assets like resources) that maximizes expected return, rather than maximizing individual holdings’ returns.
  3. As a rule of thumb, consider a 5-20% resource-stock allocation as part of a diversified equity portfolio, even in periods when resources underperform other sectors.
  4. Determining the exact optimal weighting requires Monte Carlo modeling given the investor’s volatility tolerance; a fixed percentage is only a starting heuristic.

Caveats

Data is specific to the Australian market (ASX, Reserve Bank statistics) and the 1980-2001 period, dominated by the resources sector’s underperformance during a long commodity bear market — conclusions about correlation benefits may not hold in commodity supercycle periods (e.g., 2000s) or in other markets. The article does not give transaction costs, rebalancing frequency, or a specific methodology for estimating forward-looking correlations, which the author admits are unstable. It is a condensed magazine treatment of the more rigorous methodology in the author’s companion academic paper; see Portfolio Risk Reduction: Optimising Selection of Resource Projects by Application of Financial Industry Techniques for the full derivation.

Who it is for

Investors and portfolio managers wanting a concrete, numbers-based case for including a chronically underperforming, high-volatility sector in a diversified portfolio on correlation grounds rather than return expectations.
