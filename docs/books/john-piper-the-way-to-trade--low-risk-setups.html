Piper's Low-Risk Setups (Goose, Goat, Snake, Box, Piano, Illusion, Platypus, Horse)

John Piper · 1999 · Tier A system intermediate

Eight code-worded, Market Profile-based futures setups sharing one filter: a stop close enough to entry that risk is small relative to potential reward.

What it is

A set of eight named, code-worded intraday/swing setups (mainly for stock-index futures such as the FTSE and S&P) that Piper actually traded, unified by his “low-risk trading opportunity” filter: only take an entry where a defined stop sits close enough to the entry price that risk is small relative to potential reward. Most setups lean on Peter Steidlmayer’s Market Profile concepts (Value, Minus Development, Acceptance) to judge whether a break or reversal is “real” rather than noise. Trend direction is determined first, using a simple rule (rising bottoms/falling tops, or Market Profile’s “trend is up in the absence of initiating selling”), and setups are then traded with or against that trend as specified below.

Rules

Trend filter (used across most setups): define the trend as up when the market shows rising bottoms and no initiating selling; down when it shows falling tops and no initiating buying. Simple alternates Piper offers: today’s O/H/L/C higher (lower) than X days ago, or close above (below) a Y-day moving average. Treat any countertrend initiating action as “flat” until the primary trend indicator resumes.

  1. Gap open — “Goose”: trade with the established trend. Confirm that the high/low made in the first ~hour is likely the extreme for the day, and look for a decisive move away from the open within the first 90 minutes (sooner is better). Entry on the rally/decline off the open; stop placed a defined distance (Piper’s example: a few points, or a filter beyond the opening high/low) beyond the opening extreme.

  2. Failed breakthrough — “Goat”: identify a key level (round number, or significant support/resistance). When price breaks it by a small amount (often under 10 points, frequently just 1–2) and then is reclaimed, enter in the direction of the reclaim — countertrend relative to the breakout. Stop is placed just beyond the peak/trough of the failed breakthrough. Best signals come with fast, high-participation reversal action.

  3. Failed re-test — “Snake”: after a key high/low is made, price re-tests that level, fails to exceed it, and reverses again. Enter counter to the immediate re-test move (i.e., selling a failed re-test of a high, buying a failed re-test of a low), ideally with Minus Development confirming rejection. Stop just beyond the level being tested. Piper treats this as the most contrarian and lowest-conviction of the eight, used more selectively than the Goat.

  4. Square congestion — “Box”: on a short intraday timeframe (Piper uses 5-minute futures bars), identify a horizontal congestion range where the highs and lows are each touched multiple times (“squared”). Trade the breakout in whichever direction the range is penetrated; stop at the opposite side of the range. Skip ranges where the resulting stop distance is too wide.

  5. Key levels — “Piano” (a confirmation filter, not a standalone entry): weight any of the other seven setups more heavily when they occur at long-term support/resistance, round numbers, or widely-publicized target levels, on the logic that the “longer time frame participant” is more likely to be active there.

  6. Elliott Fives — “Illusion”: treat a completed 5-wave Elliott impulse as a trend-direction signal (the “five’s” direction = the trend). Enter using one of the other low-risk setups in that direction, or when the extreme of the five (its high in an up-five, low in a down-five) is subsequently taken out. A completed five can also serve as a trend-exhaustion/reversal marker.

  7. Aborted patterns — “Platypus”: when a widely followed chart pattern (an Elliott five, head-and-shoulders, or a failed breakthrough itself) begins to break down before completing as expected, fade the crowd positioned for the original pattern — e.g., if a support break (the “obvious” sell signal) itself fails, buy.

  8. Trend following — “Horse”: no independent entry trigger; enter using any of the above low-risk setups once trend direction is established, then hold using the trend definition itself as the exit rule — stay long/short as long as no initiating countertrend action is accepted; exit once such action is confirmed. Multiple entries while pyramiding into the same trend are acceptable but must not become overtrading.

Corrective action (a ninth, related concept): treat a pullback within a trend as continuation-only as long as it fails to produce accepted initiating action against the trend; once judged exhausted, re-enter with the trend using one of setups 1–4 above.

Risk

No setup-specific position-sizing figures are given; all eight inherit Piper’s book-wide Money Management rule of capping any single trade’s risk at roughly 1–2% of total trading capital, with the stop distance specified per setup above (opening-range distance for Goose, the failed extreme for Goat/Snake, the opposite side of the range for Box). Because several setups are explicitly countertrend (Goat, Snake, Platypus), Piper stresses these need a stronger supporting reason and tighter stop discipline than the trend-following setups (Horse, Goose), and recommends closing out intraday positions rather than holding overnight unless there is a sufficient profit buffer in hand.

Caveats

Several triggers are qualitative rather than precisely parameterized: “a fairly good move” (Goose), “fairly close” stops (Snake), and a break “usually less than 10 points” (Goat) are the book’s own wording, not fixed numbers — a trader coding these must choose specific thresholds. Piper states outright that he has not done formal statistical testing on the win rate of failed breakthroughs (“Goat”) and offers logic, not backtested figures, for most of the eight. The setups are demonstrated almost entirely on UK FTSE and S&P index futures/cash from the late 1990s, so tick sizes, gap frequency, and the “Goose” opening-range logic may not transfer directly to other markets or the post-decimalization, near-continuous-trading environment of modern markets. “Elliott Fives” and “Aborted Patterns” both depend on subjective Elliott Wave counting, which Piper himself elsewhere in the book calls unreliable (“the garbage of Elliott”) — a tension he does not fully resolve.
