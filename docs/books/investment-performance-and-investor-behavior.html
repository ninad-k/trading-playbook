Investment Performance and Investor Behavior

Wilbur G. Lewellen, Ronald C. Lease, and Gary G. Schlarbaum · 1979 · Tier B — concise note paper advanced

Brokerage records from 1964-1970 show large, internally consistent differences among individual investors, with trading costs and activity strongly tied to net outcomes.

Summary

Lewellen, Lease, and Schlarbaum examine a 10% random sample of customers who kept accounts at one national brokerage throughout 1964-1970. The data include roughly 200,000 common-stock transactions in 2,500 accounts and 75,123 completed long or short round trips. A 1972 questionnaire adds demographic and strategy information for nearly 1,000 respondents (pp. 2-4).

Across all round trips, the duration-weighted mean annual return is 9.9% before costs and 5.5% after costs. Against risk-matched diversified benchmarks, the corresponding differentials are 4.5% and 0.1%. Thus, the sample exceeds its benchmark before commissions and transfer taxes but approximately matches it afterward (pp. 5-7).

After filtering out accounts with fewer than five round trips or one year of position-days, the authors rank 1,581 investors into performance quintiles (p. 8). Before costs, the top quintile averages 35.8% annually and the bottom -14.2%; after costs, the figures are 29.8% and -21.7% (p. 9). These differences reflect repeated outcomes, not merely a few outsized trades: return consistency and performance quintiles are strongly associated (pp. 10-14).

Key points

  • Both the best and worst groups concentrate in fewer, higher-beta, non-NYSE securities; trading style distinguishes their outcomes (pp. 15-20).
  • The best post-cost group trades less frequently, holds longer, and uses less margin and short selling than the poorest group (pp. 16-20).
  • Demographics add little classification power; age, experience, wealth, and sex show no clear advantage (pp. 20-22).
  • Fundamental analysis, technical analysis, account-executive advice, research spending, and stated diversification do not significantly distinguish performance (pp. 23-25).
  • A dividend-income emphasis is associated with middle-of-the-range outcomes, while capital-gains-oriented investors populate both extremes (pp. 25-27).

Actionable rules

  1. Evaluate individual-investor results after transaction costs and against a risk-matched benchmark; the conclusions differ sharply from raw returns (pp. 5-7).
  2. Test whether apparent skill comes from consistently positive trades rather than one exceptional outcome (pp. 10-14).
  3. Treat behavioral and demographic links as sample associations, not trading prescriptions; the paper does not test persistence in a later period (pp. 12, 20-28).

Caveats

Requiring accounts to remain open for seven years can create selection bias. Returns exclude financing costs, benchmarks exclude transaction costs, and only completed round trips are analyzed. Questionnaire responses were collected after the trading period, and the 1964-1970 market favored riskier non-NYSE shares.

Who it is for

Researchers and investors studying realized retail performance, trading costs, apparent skill, and behavioral correlates.
