Trend Determination: A Quick, Accurate, and Effective Methodology

John Hayden · 1999 · Tier B — concise note manual intermediate

John Hayden presents RSI as a bounded, smoother momentum measure for trend analysis and explains its use across markets and timeframes.

Summary

Hayden uses the Relative Strength Index to determine trend and help set price objectives. He contrasts RSI with momentum, rate of change, and stochastics, arguing that those measures can jump when a large old move leaves the lookback window. RSI’s bounded 0-100 construction dampens that distortion and makes its readings easier to compare (pp. 1-4). The document describes RSI as applicable across markets and time periods, while crediting Andrew Cardwell’s trend-oriented interpretation. The later pages discuss trend ranges and how RSI behavior can differ in bullish and bearish regimes; the guide treats the indicator as a context tool rather than a mechanical buy-sell switch.

Key points

  • RSI is calculated from average up closes relative to average down closes over N periods (p. 1).
  • A 9- versus 10-day example shows RSI changing less erratically than simple momentum after large moves roll out (pp. 2-4).
  • RSI’s fixed 0-100 range avoids the need to compare unbounded historical oscillator levels (p. 4).
  • Hayden prefers a 14-period lookback, notes that some intraday traders use nine periods, and says 25 performs well for gold, silver, crude, and financials; he requires at least 90 periods of input and prefers 200 days for daily-chart work (p. 4).
  • His trend ranges are 80/40 for an uptrend and 60/20 for a downtrend; a shift in which levels hold as support or resistance can warn of a regime change (pp. 4-6).
  • The moving-average framework compares a 9-period simple average with a 45-period weighted average on both price and RSI, producing up, down, sideways-to-up, and sideways-to-down classifications (p. 9).

Actionable rules

  1. Use enough history for the calculation: the author requires at least 90 periods and prefers at least 200 days when reading daily charts (p. 4).
  2. Classify the active RSI range before reading overbought or oversold: the author uses 80/40 for bullish conditions and 60/20 for bearish conditions (pp. 4-6).
  3. Treat divergence in its trend context. Hayden describes repeated bearish divergences as common in bull markets and repeated bullish divergences as common in bear markets, and calls two- or three-period divergences the strongest in his framework (pp. 6-8).

Caveats

This is an instructional indicator note, not a tested strategy. Thresholds and settings are not universal, and the assigned pages do not specify a complete stop, target, or sizing plan.

Who it is for

Intermediate traders learning RSI construction and trend-aware interpretation.
