Overview

This is a UK-focused beginner’s course on financial spread-betting (leveraged, bid/offer-priced bets on an index’s direction, rather than commission-based trading), written by a self-taught trader, Rob Walton, who trades stock indices (starting with the FTSE, then moving to the Dow) intraday. The stated goal is to teach a reader with no market background everything needed to open an account, read a chart, and place trades using free data, skipping market history the author considers irrelevant to making money. Most of the technical content (moving averages, Bollinger Bands, MACD, stochastics, RSI, and an extensive candlestick reference) is reproduced from a third-party educator (credited to Arthur Hill), framed by the author’s commentary on which settings he actually watches. Note: the PDF available for this note is a “free-view” excerpt that terminates immediately before Chapter 16 — the chapter the author repeatedly promises will contain his concrete, integrated method (“the best is saved for last”). That chapter is not present in this document.

Core thesis

Fundamental analysis, in the author’s view, is largely a waste of time for a trader because markets are driven by fear and greed rather than reasoned valuation, and by the time fundamentals are confirmed the price move is over; technical analysis is preferred because it reads the market’s actual, self-reinforcing sentiment in real time. The author trades very short holding periods (15 to 90 minutes) because an index can swing several hundred points intraday even in a week that closes flat, and the profit is in catching those swings rather than waiting out a multi-week position. His method is to watch a live intraday chart with a small set of standard indicators (moving averages, Bollinger Bands, MACD, stochastic, RSI) for confirming signals, using candlestick formations as the primary signal of a sentiment change, reacting to what the market is doing rather than predicting what it will do next.

Key concepts

  • Spread-betting vs. investing — a bet on an index’s point movement, quoted as bid (sell) and offer (buy); the spread is the broker’s built-in margin, paid on entry rather than through commission. Buying executes at the offer, selling at the bid, so every position opens already behind by the spread’s width.
  • Position sizing via “per point” — bets are sized in currency per point of index movement; profit or loss = points moved × stake per point, minus the spread.
  • Leading vs. lagging indicators — leading (RSI, Stochastic) signal earlier but less reliably and work best in ranging markets; lagging/trend-following (moving averages, MACD) confirm established trends but fail in sideways markets.
  • SMA vs. EMA — a Simple Moving Average weights all periods equally and lags more; an Exponential Moving Average weights recent prices more heavily and tracks price more closely, at the cost of more whipsaws.
  • Bollinger Bands — a moving average with bands at ±2 standard deviations; narrowing bands signal a low-volatility squeeze that often precedes a breakout, but band width alone does not signal direction.
  • MACD (12/26/9) — the difference between a 12-day and 26-day EMA plotted against a 9-day EMA signal line; used for centerline and signal-line crossovers.
  • Stochastic (%K/%D) and RSI — momentum oscillators (0–100); readings below 20/30 and above 70/80 flag extremes, with the most reliable signals from a divergence plus a second cross back through the extreme line rather than a single touch.
  • Candlestick reversal patterns — formations (doji, hammer, shooting star, engulfing, harami, morning/evening star, abandoned baby) spotting trend exhaustion, generally requiring 1–3 days of confirmation.
  • Confirmation stacking — a single indicator or candlestick pattern is unreliable; robustness comes from combining a candlestick reversal with support/resistance, a momentum divergence, and a volume signal.

Rules and setups

The source material available for this note stops short of a fixed, numbered trading system; the author defers his complete integrated method to a final chapter not included here. The settings and practices stated explicitly are:

  1. Timeframe: intraday (“1-day” chart setting showing live price action), typical trade duration 15 to 90 minutes; no daily/weekly swing charts.
  2. Instrument: primarily the Dow Jones index (started on the FTSE for stability, switched to the Dow for larger swings); the author only personally trades indices, though he claims the method generalizes.
  3. Indicator settings: a 2-line SMA at 9 and 18 periods; standard Bollinger Bands (20-period SMA ± 2 SD, watched for tightening as a breakout alert); standard MACD (12/26/9) for centerline and signal-line crossovers; standard 14-period Stochastic and RSI with 20/80 or 30/70 bands, favoring a divergence plus a second cross back through the extreme line over a single touch.
  4. Trigger for attention: the “Daily Change” figure on the live price screen — any move of more than 5 points in a short interval is a cue to focus on the chart.
  5. No explicit numeric stop-loss, profit target, or position-sizing rule is given — a position is closed “by doing exactly the opposite” of the entry trade, with exits triggered when candlesticks and indicators collectively signal the move is exhausted.

Risk and money management

The document contains no explicit position-sizing formula, maximum-risk-per-trade percentage, or stop-loss rule; risk control is implicit in stake size (currency per point), left to the reader, and in reacting quickly to reversal signals rather than holding through adverse moves. The author warns that spread-betting is “highly speculative” and that only risk capital should be used, but does not translate this into a numeric rule. The one operational risk practice described is accepting delayed free data over paid real-time feeds, since a 1–15 minute delay is a minor inconvenience rather than a risk factor at the 15–90 minute holding periods the author trades.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s stance is explicitly anti-forecasting: “I have no intention of working out what the market is going to do… I just follow its movements in the here and now and react when it reacts.” The author frames this as a discipline in itself, warning against waiting for predicted patterns to materialize and against outsourcing trading judgment to paid signal services or gurus who “want you to attend one of their seminars.” The recurring advice is to treat indicators as filters that must agree with each other and with the candlestick signal before acting, rather than reacting to any single overbought/oversold reading in isolation, since single signals are repeatedly described as prone to false breaks and whipsaws.

Strengths and caveats

The technical-indicator and candlestick chapters are competent, standard educational material (attributed by the author to a third-party source) rather than a proprietary edge, and the same content is available in many other technical-analysis references in this library with more rigor (see Related). The book shows its age in places (dial-up-era tips like “how to double your internet speed for free”) and is UK-specific in its product framing (spread-betting is a UK/Ireland retail product, distinct from US futures or CFDs in tax and margin treatment). Most importantly, the copy available here is a promotional excerpt that stops immediately before the chapter positioned as the actual payoff, so no complete, numbered entry/exit/stop system could be extracted; what remains is market mechanics plus generic indicator/candlestick education, useful as background but not a standalone tradable method. There is no back-tested performance data or win-rate statistic anywhere in the document.

Who should read it

Complete beginners to UK financial spread-betting who need account-opening and bid/offer mechanics explained from scratch, and readers wanting a gentle, jargon-light first introduction to moving averages, Bollinger Bands, MACD, stochastics, RSI, and candlestick patterns before moving to more rigorous treatments. Traders wanting a complete, numerically specified system with defined entries, exits, and risk rules should look elsewhere, since this document’s promised system chapter is not present in the available material.
