Position-Sizing Effects on Trader Performance: An Experimental Analysis

Johan Ginyard · 2001 · Tier B — concise note paper intermediate

A simulated trading experiment finds smaller positions reduced bankruptcy risk, although training did not produce significantly higher profits.

Summary

Ginyard experimentally studied position size by having participants trade fictitious capital through repeated simulated stock trades. The only participant-controlled variable was the percentage of available capital allocated to each position. A treatment group received a three-hour lecture on position sizing, risk management, and psychological biases; a control group did not. The treatment group went bankrupt less often, 2 of 32 participants (6.3%) versus 8 of 20 (40.0%), a statistically significant difference (p < .01), while mean profits were not significantly higher (pp. 3, 20). Winning traders used smaller average positions than losing or bankrupt traders: at the first risk level, 6.0% versus 15.0% and 22.9%; at the second, 3.8% versus 6.5% and 23.7% (p. 20). The experiment supports survival benefits from smaller sizing in its simulated setting.

Key points

  • A 30% drawdown requires about 42.9% growth on remaining capital to recover; a 50% drawdown requires 100% (p. 7).
  • Treatment positions averaged 5.5% and 3.6% at the two levels, versus 12.0% and 5.3% for controls (p. 20).
  • The lecture reduced bankruptcy risk by an estimated factor of ten but did not significantly raise average gains (pp. 3, 20-22).
  • The study links loss aversion, sunk cost, and disposition effects to oversizing and holding losers (pp. 9-10, 28).
  • Outcomes were generated from common simulated trades and random draws, not live markets (pp. 22-23).

Actionable rules

  1. Define risk per position before trading and examine its drawdown and recovery consequences (pp. 7, 28-29).
  2. Use the experiment’s 3.7%-6.6% surviving averages only as observations from this design, never as a universal sizing prescription (pp. 20-22).
  3. Test sizing separately from signal quality; position size was deliberately isolated as the manipulated variable (pp. 3-4).

Caveats

The sample was small, laboratory based, and used fictitious stakes. Participants could not choose trade outcomes, and the systems had positive expected value in the experiment. Real leverage, liquidity, slippage, and personal stakes may change behavior.

Who it is for

Traders and researchers interested in position sizing, drawdown mathematics, and behavioral risk.
