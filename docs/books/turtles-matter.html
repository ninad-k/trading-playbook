Summary

A short 2005 blog-style article (via TradingMarkets, reprinted on Yahoo! Finance) retelling the 1983 Richard Dennis vs. William Eckhardt “nature vs. nurture” bet that produced the Turtle traders, using it to argue that any disciplined trader can learn a mechanical system. It then presents Larry Connors’ “Connors Battle Plan” as an example of a simple, rule-based, high-win-rate system in the same spirit as the Turtle rules.

Key points

  • The bet — Dennis believed trading could be taught (“nurture”); Eckhardt believed great traders are born (“nature”); they hired 13 apprentices with no trading background to settle it.
  • Result — the Turtles, funded with reportedly $500,000+, earned an average compounded return of about 80% over roughly four years, supporting the “nurture” side.
  • Two components of the Turtle approach — a mechanical system (defined entry/exit criteria) plus explicit risk control to survive losing periods; the article stresses risk control as equally critical to the system’s edge.
  • Example system (Connors Battle Plan, S&P 500): 1. S&P 500 trading above its 200-day simple moving average. 2. Price makes an intraday 10-day low. 3. VIX closes 5%+ above its 10-day simple moving average. 4. Buy the close; exit when price closes above its 10-period simple moving average.
  • Backtest cited — over roughly 10.5 years, this setup signaled 58 times on the S&P (81% win rate, 700+ SPX points captured) and 52 times on the Nasdaq/NDX using the same rules (77% win rate, 2,100+ points), while in the market under 15% of the time.

Actionable rules

  1. Trend filter: only consider longs when the index is above its 200-day SMA.
  2. Entry trigger: buy on the close of a day that makes a 10-day intraday low.
  3. Volatility filter: require the VIX to close at least 5% above its own 10-day SMA on the entry day.
  4. Exit: close the position once price closes above its 10-period SMA.
  5. Position sizing beyond “one lot”/appropriate risk control is explicitly flagged by the article’s author as not yet determined (“we now need to know what kind of position sizes are appropriate… I will work on that”).

Caveats

This is a short motivational/blog article, not a full write-up of the actual Turtle rules (entry/exit prices, N-based position sizing, and pyramiding are not described here — see the dedicated Turtle sources in this library for those). The Connors system’s backtest statistics are asserted without methodology detail (data source, slippage, exact sample dates) and should be treated as illustrative rather than verified.

Who it is for

Traders who want the short-form origin story of the Turtle experiment as motivation for adopting a mechanical, rule-based approach, plus one worked example of a simple mean-reversion S&P setup; readers wanting the actual Turtle trading rules should go to a dedicated Turtle source.
