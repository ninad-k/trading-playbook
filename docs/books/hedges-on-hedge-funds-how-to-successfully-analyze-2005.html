Summary

Written by the founder of a hedge fund advisory and fund-of-funds firm, this is an investor-facing (not trader-facing) guide to the hedge fund industry circa 2005. It explains what hedge funds are, how their fee and legal structures differ from traditional managers, surveys the major strategy categories (global macro, managed futures, event-driven, arbitrage/relative value, equity long-short, market neutral), and devotes substantial space to due diligence: transparency, valuation practices, operational risk, and regional coverage of European and Asian hedge fund growth. It is written for allocators deciding whether and how to invest in funds, not for traders building systems.

Key points

  • Defines hedge funds as private, largely unregulated pooled vehicles restricted to accredited/qualified investors (net worth ≥$1M or income ≥$200K/$300K couple; up to 499 investors if all are “qualified purchasers” with ≥$5M liquid net worth).
  • Standard fee structure: 1% annual management fee plus 20% of net returns (“2 and 20”).
  • Contrasts traditional managers (relative returns, regulated, limited strategies) with hedge funds (absolute returns, wide mandate, manager-skill-driven “alpha”).
  • Global macro funds run the largest asset bases in the industry, are described as resilient to the “size hurts performance” pattern seen in other strategies, and use leverage/derivatives more than most (leverage/derivatives used by under 5% of hedge funds generally).
  • Citing a Capco study: operational risk factors (fraud, misrepresentation, unauthorized trading, resource/infrastructure gaps) account for roughly half of catastrophic hedge fund failures; valuation problems specifically contributed to about 35% of studied failures.
  • Distinguishes investment due diligence (strategy, performance) from operational due diligence (back-office, controls, valuation practices) and argues both are required.
  • Minimum investments typically range $250K–$10M, most commonly $500K–$1M.
  • Covers regional chapters on the expansion of European and Asian hedge fund industries and the challenge of building a hedge fund index/benchmark.

Actionable rules

None given as trading rules — this is an allocator’s due-diligence framework, not a trading system. Closest actionable takeaways: (1) treat operational due diligence (valuation controls, back-office capacity, style-drift monitoring) as equally important as track-record review before allocating; (2) watch for red flags — misappropriation, misrepresented performance, unauthorized trading outside guidelines, thin infrastructure; (3) scrutinize how illiquid or complex positions are priced, since broker quotes on instruments like mortgage-backed securities can vary 20–30%.

Caveats

Written for capital allocators evaluating hedge fund managers, not for retail traders — there are no entry/exit rules, indicators, or position-sizing formulas. Figures (fee norms, minimums, accreditation thresholds) reflect 2005 U.S. securities regulation and may be dated. The author’s firm (LJH Global Investments) provides hedge fund advisory services, so strategy framing carries an industry-promotional tone in places.

Who it is for

Investors, allocators, or advisors deciding whether to add hedge funds to a portfolio and how to vet managers; not useful for someone looking for a tradable system.
