Default Rates on Structured Finance Securities

Douglas J. Lucas, Laurie S. Goodman, and Frank J. Fabozzi · 2004 · Tier B — concise note paper advanced

A methodological critique recalculates structured-finance default rates using transition-matrix multiplication and issue cohorts.

Summary

Lucas, Goodman, and Fabozzi ask why rating-agency default statistics for structured-finance tranches can exceed market intuition and corporate-bond benchmarks. They review six S&P and Moody’s studies, focusing on rating transitions, withdrawn ratings, heterogeneous collateral, and cohort construction (pp. 1–4). The paper’s central method multiplies average six-month transition matrices ten times to estimate five-year transitions, using more recent observations and avoiding the exclusion of newer tranches. For BBB RMBS, this produces a 1.62% five-year transition to D versus S&P’s 4.00%; CMBS is 0.65% versus 0.93%, while ABS is higher at 13.80% versus 1.23% because recent ABS performance worsened (pp. 4–6). The authors argue S&P’s exclusion of withdrawn ratings can inflate rates, and Moody’s “material impairment” definition is broader than an uncured payment default (pp. 4, 7–9). Using Moody’s original-issue cohorts, they estimate Baa Resi B&C impairment at 2.11%, versus 3.47% from an earlier all-ABS adjustment and 1.62% from S&P’s BBB RMBS method (pp. 7–9). Their blended historical estimate for BBB Resi B&C is 1.9% over five years, compared with corporate BBB 2.7% in their final table (p. 10).

Key points

  • Default statistics depend materially on transition horizon, rating withdrawals, asset mix, and rolling versus original-issue cohorts (pp. 2–4, 7–10).
  • Matrix multiplication uses more recent data but implicitly assumes rating transitions are not serially correlated (p. 10).
  • Default frequency is only half of credit loss; severity is not analyzed (p. 2).

Actionable rules

  1. The paper gives no security-selection or position-sizing rule; it audits historical default estimates.
  2. Before comparing default rates, inspect the denominator, cohort construction, withdrawn-rating treatment, and definition of default or material impairment (pp. 2–4, 7–9).
  3. Separate default frequency from loss severity and avoid transferring a broad asset-class rate to a specific collateral type or rating without an explicit adjustment (pp. 2, 6–10).

Caveats

The calculations are historical estimates built from rating-agency data and several judgmental adjustments. The authors acknowledge “Kentucky windage,” heterogeneous collateral, sponsor-specific events, and the matrix method’s no-serial-correlation assumption (pp. 2, 10). Results do not forecast future crises.

Who it is for

Useful for structured-credit investors, CDO analysts, and researchers auditing default statistics.
