Overview

Ken Wolff, with co-authors Chris Schumacher and Jeff Tappan, wrote this in 2002 as a post-crash update to the online-momentum-trading methods he taught in the Mtrader.com and RealityTrader.com chat rooms during the late-1990s bull market. His core claim: the predictable, months-long momentum patterns of 1996–2000 broke down after the March 2000 Nasdaq crash, taking out roughly 40% of short-term traders who kept applying stale tactics; the book is aimed at traders willing to adapt setups daily via a tracking-diary process rather than trade a fixed system. It covers order-routing mechanics (SOES, SelectNet, ECNs), the psychology of “smart/informed/dumb money,” four repeatable intraday chart patterns, tape-reading, and — the section Wolff says to read if nothing else — his stop-loss and money-management rules.

Core thesis

Short-term stock moves are driven by predictable, repeating cycles of fear and greed among inexperienced traders reacting to news, not by company fundamentals. A disciplined momentum trader doesn’t forecast a stock’s future — only reads what the tape is doing right now, exploits a small number of catalogued setups (gainers, dumpers, gap plays, news alerts) that a personal “tracking diary” shows are currently working, and survives long enough to keep exploiting them by treating every stop-loss as insurance rather than failure.

Key concepts

  • Momentum trader vs. investor — a momentum trader trades symbols and crowd reactions on a timescale of seconds to days, indifferent to the underlying business; conflating this with buy-and-hold psychology (“stocks always come back”) is, per Wolff, the primary cause of blown accounts.
  • Smart / informed / dumb money — a three-tier model of participants; smart money buys/sells ahead of the crowd, dumb money chases late and pays “tuition,” and dumb money slowly turns into informed money after enough painful cycles.
  • Tracking diary — a daily log of gap size, open/close, half-hour price levels, and a written description of how each pattern group (first/second-day gainers and dumpers) behaved; used to form the next day’s expectations.
  • Ax — the Market Maker or ECN whose size is currently constraining a stock’s move; identifying it helps read whether a pause is real resistance or order-filling noise.
  • Front-running / “sell the news” — buying ahead of an expected announcement and exiting into the spike the news itself produces, which is why good news often coincides with a stock opening down.
  • Level 1 vs. Level 2 screens — Level 1 shows best bid/ask/last only; Level 2 shows every participant and size at each price level, used to assess execution risk before entering.
  • Mid-range rule — (day’s high − low) ÷ 2, added to the low; closes above it across several sessions signal continuation, below it signal more selling pressure.
  • Chasing — buying/shorting materially away from the real reversal price out of impatience; the single biggest cause of turning a good setup into a loss.
  • Clearing the spread — the point where enough buying moves the bid up to your entry ask, making an exit there breakeven; the minimum bar for a trade “working.”
  • Trailing stop — moving the stop price in the direction of a winning trade to lock in profit as the position runs.

Rules and setups

Full entry/exit mechanics for the four core intraday setups (gainer pattern, dumper pattern, end-of-day gap plays, breakout plays) are in the companion system page Momentum Day-Trading Setups (Gainers, Dumpers, Gap Plays, Breakouts). Recurring numeric filters:

  1. Gainer/dumper qualification: stock up (gainer) or down (dumper) 20%+ from the previous close on news; below 20% predictability degrades.
  2. Pre-market volume filter: at least 50,000 pre-market shares across multiple prints (not one block) by open; under 10,000 shares, drop the name.
  3. Level 2 execution filter: trade only names with roughly five participants near the inside price and a spread no wider than 0.15 point; caution above 0.25.
  4. News-alert entry: enter within 0.15 point of the alert price or skip the trade — do not chase.
  5. Breakout play: on a new intraday high, enter expecting a 0.25–0.50 point move; exit half the position on the move, small predefined stop if the level fails.
  6. Two-stop rule (dumper bottoms): take at most two stop-losses hunting a reversal bottom, then stand aside.
  7. End-of-day gap entry: assess only in the final few minutes; exit at or just before next morning’s open unless conditions clearly favor holding longer.

Risk and money management

Wolff’s money management is dollar/percentage-of-portfolio based rather than fixed-share-count based: pick a stop distance (e.g., 0.15–0.40 point) and size the position so the dollar loss at that stop is an acceptable fraction of total account equity — his worked example: a $50,000 account trading 1,000 shares at $50 with a 0.25-point stop risks $250 (0.5% of equity); the same $250 loss on a $25,000 account is a much larger percentage, so share size should shrink accordingly. He distinguishes mental stops (faster, requires execution confidence), auto/hard stops (better for stocks moving too fast to react manually or when stepping away from the screen), and trailing stops (locking in profit as a winner runs). Golden Rule #10 is explicit: keep stops and adhere to money-management principles; Golden Rule #6 recommends paper trading until a system proves it trades above a 90% win rate before risking real capital, then trading small share size at first specifically to build execution confidence.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s centerpiece is Chapter 12’s “Golden Rules of Trading” (14 numbered rules, detailed in full in the system sub-page), built around one idea repeated throughout: a stop-loss is insurance, not a personal failure, and the trader’s job is to exit the instant “the reason you got into a trade is no longer valid” regardless of ego, hindsight (“I should have known it would do that”), or a room leader’s hype. Wolff frames chat-room culture explicitly as adversarial — leaders and members routinely post fabricated or after-the-fact entry/exit prices to look good, and readers are told to distrust any unverifiable, real-time-stamped claim. He argues roughly 70% of day traders fail primarily from an inability to hold a tight stop loss, not from picking bad setups.

Ch 7 — Indicators — using “indicator stocks” that lead the broader tape. Ch 8 — Market Dynamics — the trading day’s phases and how macro tone modifies the Ch. 6 patterns. Ch 9 — Tracking — building and analyzing a daily tracking diary; forming next-day expectations; the mid-range rule. Ch 10 — Time of Day — delayed quotes, chasing risk, pre-/after-hours trading. Ch 11 — Identifying Trading Opportunities — tape reading and the Market Maker/”ax” effect. Ch 12 — Rules of a Trader — stop-loss discipline, trailing stops, money management, and the 14 Golden Rules. Ch 13 — Personal Accountability and Trading Attitude — psychology of discipline and self-blame vs. process focus. Ch 14 — Momentum Investing — a longer-horizon hybrid of momentum trading and investing, including pre-market gap and closing-combination exits.

Strengths and caveats

Strengths: the stop-loss/psychology chapters (12–13) and the tracking-diary methodology (Ch. 9) are concrete, numbered, and largely timeless regardless of instrument or era. The four pattern setups are described with real entry triggers and stop placement, not vague “watch for momentum” advice.

Caveats: heavily dated market-microstructure content — SOES, SelectNet, the pre-decimal 1/16-point tick, “tier sizes,” and the 1990s Nasdaq Market Maker structure were substantially changed or eliminated by later regulation (decimalization in 2001, Reg NMS in 2005); a modern reader must mentally substitute today’s routing/ECN landscape. The pattern statistics (e.g., “20% gap” thresholds, point-potential figures) come from Wolff’s own late-1990s/2000 chat-room tracking, not a rigorous backtest, and he repeatedly notes the patterns’ own reliability decayed within the book’s timeframe — a warning against assuming today’s numbers still hold. There is no discussion of algorithmic/HFT competition, which now dominates the fast-momentum setups described.

Who should read it

Aspiring or early-stage intraday equity day traders wanting a structured, rules-based approach to reading tape and enforcing stop-loss discipline; the microstructure/routing chapters are historical rather than actionable today, but the setup taxonomy, tracking methodology, and Golden Rules remain a useful discipline template.
