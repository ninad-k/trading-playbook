The C Wave Method

Myles Wilson Walker · 1996 · Tier A — deep note system advanced

A catalog of eleven Elliott Wave 'C wave' end-of-correction patterns, each with a fixed one-tick entry-stop and stop-loss rule.

What it is

A pattern-recognition method that trades only the final leg (wave C) of an Elliott Wave A-B-C correction, using eleven defined pattern shapes to recognize when wave C is about to complete. Rather than counting a full wave sequence from its start, the trader watches for one of the eleven shapes to form, places a stop order to enter on a break of the pattern’s second-to-last sub-wave, and protects the trade with a stop beyond its final sub-wave. All patterns share the same entry/stop mechanic and differ only in the wave-count and price/time relationships that identify which one is forming. Designed for daily and intraday bar charts across futures, currencies, and indices, intended as a standalone signal.

Rules

  1. Universal entry/stop mechanic (all eleven patterns): label the pattern’s final two sub-waves “d”/4 and “e”/5. Place an entry stop one tick beyond the extreme of bar d/4 (above for a long, below for a short). When triggered, place a protective stop one tick beyond the extreme of bar e/5.
  2. Pattern identification — any one qualifies: Pattern 1 (waves 1/4 don’t overlap; larger wave A/B clearly visible ahead of the impulse-like wave C); Pattern 2 (waves b/d overlap in price; wave e brief or a small five-wave triangle); Pattern 3 (five-wave triangle, wave a longest, b–e similar range/subdivision — frequent and reliable); Pattern 4 (five clean waves; a and b each subdivide into three, d into three, e a single wave); Pattern 5 (only three-wave a-b-c pattern; entry on a break of wave b, stop beyond wave c); Patterns 6–9 (strong/running B wave extending past the start of wave A; wave C is a triangle, retraces ≥50% of B, is a Pattern-3-style structure, or breaks strongly in wave A’s direction respectively; entry is a break of wave d for all four); Pattern 10 (zigzag, wave B retraces ≤61.8% of A, ends in a five-wave triangle, wave 4 smaller than wave 2 in price and time; entry on a break of wave D); Pattern 11 (false impulse — combined time of waves 3–5 from the end of wave 2 is ≤ combined time of waves 1–2; entry on a break of the 2–4 trendline, stop beyond wave 5).
  3. Trade management: once three waves of the resulting move can be counted (third leg roughly ≥300% of the second), move the stop beyond the second leg. If stopped out and the next wave group subdivides into threes rather than fives, treat it as a new correction and look for a fresh C wave pattern (6, 7, or 8) instead of assuming the move is over. Once five waves are countable, move the stop beyond the most recent fourth-wave extreme.
  4. Pyramiding: add to the position when a new, independent C wave pattern completes in the trend’s direction; each add is its own trade with its own entry-stop and stop-loss.
  5. Profit-taking: close or partially close once profit reaches a target set as a percentage of the market’s exchange margin (roughly 50% of margin for the S&P, 100% for currencies, smaller intraday). Recommended practice: run two contracts per signal — one exits at the margin-based target, the other rides the trailing stop for a longer-term position.

Risk

  • No fixed percent-of-capital rule; risk is defined by pattern geometry (entry one tick beyond bar d/4, stop one tick beyond bar e/5).
  • Stops tighten progressively: beyond the second leg once three waves are visible, then beyond the most recent fourth wave once five waves are visible.
  • If the entry-stop is never triggered, no position is taken; the pattern is reassessed and may reclassify (e.g., into Pattern 6, 7, or 8).
  • Profit targets scale with volatility because they are set as a percentage of margin.
  • No portfolio-level risk rule (max concurrent positions, correlation limits, total heat) is given.

Caveats

Pattern identification still requires subjective judgment about wave degree even though the entry/stop trigger is mechanical — two traders can disagree on which pattern is forming before it triggers. No win rate, sample size, drawdown, or backtested performance figures are given anywhere in the source; the edge is asserted from the author’s experience, not demonstrated with data. The one-tick entry offset was specified for 1980s–1990s futures/currency tick sizes and may need adjustment for modern decimalized markets. The book explicitly says to skip ambiguous setups, so this is not a fully mechanical, always-in-the-market system.
