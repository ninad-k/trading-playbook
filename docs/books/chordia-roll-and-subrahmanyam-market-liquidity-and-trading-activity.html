Market Liquidity and Trading Activity

Tarun Chordia, Richard Roll, and Avanidhar Subrahmanyam · 2001 · Tier B — concise note paper advanced

Journal of Finance (2001) study of 11 years of NYSE data finding aggregate market liquidity and trading volume are highly volatile, decline sharply in down markets, and show strong day-of-week patterns (weak on Fridays, strong on Tuesdays).

Summary

Question: how does aggregate (market-wide) stock liquidity and trading activity vary over time, and what drives daily changes? Prior research studied liquidity only at the individual-security level over short windows (a year or less); this is the first study of market-wide liquidity over a long span. Data: quoted/effective bid-ask spreads, market depth, volume, and transaction counts for a comprehensive NYSE sample, daily, over 1988-1998 (~2,800 days, ~3.5 billion transactions from ISSM/TAQ). Method: time-series description plus regressions of daily liquidity/activity changes on interest rates, market returns/volatility, and day-of-week, holiday, and macro-announcement dummies. Finding: aggregate spreads, depth, and volume are more volatile than returns and negatively serially correlated day to day; liquidity worsens sharply in down markets but only marginally improves in up markets; strong weekly and announcement patterns exist.

Key points

  • Measures examined: quoted/effective spread ($ and %), quoted depth, dollar depth, a composite liquidity measure (%spread/$depth), share volume, dollar volume, and transaction count.
  • Secular trend: spreads declined and depth/volume rose over 1988-1998, with a structural break when NYSE’s minimum tick dropped from 1/8 to 1/16 in mid-1997.
  • Daily liquidity/volume changes are negatively serially correlated — a large move tends to partially reverse the next day.
  • Down markets hurt liquidity asymmetrically: spreads widen sharply on down days but narrow only slightly on up days — the single most significant regressor found.
  • Counter to intuition, recent volatility tends to reduce (not widen) spreads in this sample.
  • Short-term rates and the term spread significantly affect both liquidity and trading activity.
  • Strong day-of-week effects: liquidity/activity decline on Fridays and around holidays; Tuesdays show the opposite (elevated) pattern.
  • Trading activity and depth rise just before scheduled macro announcements (GDP, unemployment), falling back to normal on the announcement day itself.
  • The regression model explains only 18-33% of daily variation — a large residual is unexplained. Authors flag the sample as a persistent bull market and note liquidity dynamics could differ in a sustained bear market.

Actionable rules

None given as trading rules — a descriptive academic study, not a system. Takeaways: (1) expect spreads to widen disproportionately on down days — trade more cautiously with limit orders in falling markets; (2) expect thinner liquidity into Friday close and holidays, better liquidity on Tuesdays; (3) expect elevated volume/depth ahead of scheduled macro releases, normalizing on release day — useful for timing large orders.

Caveats

A peer-reviewed paper (The Journal of Finance, Vol. LVI, No. 2, April 2001) built entirely on 1988-1998 NYSE data — a fractional-tick, pre-decimalization, pre-algorithmic-trading era whose patterns may not transfer directly to modern markets. The paper is explicitly “atheoretical” (empirical, not testing one formal model), and the authors flag as an open question whether these dynamics hold in bear markets, since their sample was a prolonged bull run.

Who it is for

Quantitatively-minded traders and researchers interested in the empirical behavior of market-wide liquidity, execution cost timing, and day-of-week/announcement effects on the NYSE.
