The Impact of Regulation Fair Disclosure: Trading Costs and Information Asymmetry

Venkat R. Eleswarapu, Rex Thompson, Kumar Venkataraman · 2003 · Tier B paper advanced

Event study finding that SEC Regulation FD (2000) lowered information-asymmetry-driven trading costs around NYSE earnings announcements, most for small, less-liquid stocks, without raising volatility.

Source coverage: partial review. PDF pages inspected: 10-11, 23, 26 (the descriptive statistics and the spread comparisons around Reg FD). Page references in these notes identify source locations, not independently verified trading results.

Summary

Question: did the SEC’s Regulation Fair Disclosure (effective October 2000), which bars firms from selectively briefing analysts/large investors ahead of the public, actually reduce the informational advantage of “informed” traders around earnings announcements, and did it increase return volatility as critics feared (on the theory that less selective guidance means bigger surprises)? Data/method: a stratified sample of 300 NYSE common stocks (from a 1,153-stock screened universe), split into six [firm size × analyst-following] groups, comparing trading-cost and volatility measures in the period January-September 2000 (pre-regulation) versus November 2000-May 2001 (post-regulation), using TAQ intraday data. Cost measures were percentage effective spread (a Lee/Huang-Stoll-style adverse-selection proxy) and percentage price impact (price move 30 minutes after a trade); volatility was measured via a cumulative-information-flow measure (CIM) around announcement windows.

Key points

  • Effective spreads and price impact around earnings-related announcement windows (days −2 to 0) fell by 3.25 and 5.90 basis points, respectively, after Regulation FD.
  • The reduction in information asymmetry was concentrated in smaller, less liquid stocks, where declines reached as large as 14 basis points — large, heavily-analyst-covered stocks saw little change.
  • Return volatility around mandatory earnings announcements showed a marginal decrease (not increase) post-regulation, contradicting the critics’ fear that restricting selective disclosure would create bigger surprise-driven price swings.
  • When mandatory and voluntary announcements are combined (to test whether firms shifted information release into voluntary channels), total announcement-period information flow showed no significant change — consistent with firms substituting more public voluntary disclosure for the selective channels closed by the rule.
  • Descriptive data: large/high-analyst-following firms averaged $62.7B market cap, 31 analysts, and 0.25% quoted spreads, versus small/low-analyst-following firms averaging $106M market cap, 0 analysts, and 2.38% quoted spreads — illustrating the liquidity gradient the paper’s cross-sectional results run along.
  • Findings are described as more moderate than a contemporaneous study (Heflin et al.) that found larger volatility reductions around mandatory announcements.

Actionable rules

For a trader: this is an empirical regulatory event study, not a trading system, and gives no entries/exits. Its practical takeaway is a liquidity/information observation: adverse-selection-driven trading costs around earnings announcements are structurally higher in small, thinly-covered stocks than large ones, and that gap is what a regulatory change like Reg FD most affects — relevant context for anyone estimating expected slippage or spread costs around earnings in small vs. large caps.

Caveats

This is a peer-reviewed academic microstructure paper (published research, SMU authors) using intraday TAQ data and formal statistical tests (t-statistics, cross-sectional regressions) — it requires comfort with event-study methodology to fully evaluate. The sample window is short (roughly one year each side of the October 2000 rule change) and specific to NYSE-listed common stocks circa 2000-2001; results may not generalize to other exchanges, later periods, or non-US disclosure regimes.

Who it is for

Researchers or advanced traders interested in how disclosure regulation affects transaction costs and information asymmetry, particularly around earnings events — useful as background on why small-cap spreads/impact costs run structurally wider, not as a source of trade signals.
