Summary

A 277-page political-economy book by Kenneth S. Friedman (Algora Publishing, 2003), organized in three parts — Economy, Polity, and a closing “Finality?” section. The Economy section argues, using historical GNP/productivity comparisons and case studies (Reagan-era tax cuts, 1990s Mexico/Russia privatization, executive-compensation trends), that pure laissez-faire economics has a “long but undistinguished” track record and consistently underperforms well-managed mixed economies. The Polity section extends the argument into political philosophy (humanism, libertarianism, democracy), and the book closes with a chapter on the free market’s relationship to the environment. This is not a trading or investing manual — it contains no entries, exits, indicators, or position-sizing guidance — but is included here as macro/historical background reading on markets and economic cycles.

Key points

  • Central thesis: laissez-faire (minimal government intervention) economies have historically underperformed well-focused mixed economies (cites 19th-century Meiji Japan and Bismarck’s Germany outperforming free-market Britain/France; post-WWII U.S. growth was faster under New Deal-era mixed policy than under a “purer” free market since).
  • 1973–1992 average annual productivity growth table cited: Taiwan 5.3%, South Korea 5.2%, Japan 3.1%, U.S. 1.1% — used to argue U.S. productivity lagged more interventionist economies over that period.
  • Argues a recurring historical pattern (classical Athens, late Roman Empire, medieval Europe) links widening wealth disparity to social instability and eventual economic decline, and narrower disparity to periods of stability and growth.
  • Case study: Reagan-era and Kennedy-Johnson-era top-marginal-tax-rate cuts are followed, in the author’s reading of the data, by slower GNP growth, declining productivity growth, and a falling (eventually negative) net savings rate, contrary to the “supply-side” prediction that cuts would spur investment.
  • Case study: 1990s Mexican and Russian rapid transitions toward free-market policy (privatization, tariff elimination) are presented as producing currency instability, falling real wages, and broader economic disruption rather than the promised gains.
  • Discusses a sharp rise in average U.S. CEO compensation relative to average worker pay (cited at roughly 400x by the early 2000s vs. 10–25x in Japan/Europe) and gives examples (AT&T, Lucent, Qwest) of executives being paid large sums despite poor company performance, as evidence that “free market discipline” does not consistently apply to executive pay.
  • Argues free trade has historically had asymmetric winners and losers rather than being universally mutually beneficial (citing Ricardo-era England/Portugal textiles-for-wine trade and the 1786 Anglo-French Eden Treaty).
  • Polity section applies similar critique to libertarian political philosophy and democratic theory, arguing pure non-interventionism is incompatible with functioning democracy.
  • Final section extends the argument to environmental policy, arguing free-market mechanisms alone are inadequate to address ecological externalities.

Actionable rules

None given — this is not a trading manual, system, or strategy guide; it contains no entries, exits, stops, indicators, or position-sizing rules. The closest practical relevance to a trader is contextual: the book’s account of how wealth concentration, executive-incentive misalignment, and credit/short-termism cycles have historically preceded market instability can serve as background reading for macro/regime-awareness rather than as a source of tradable rules.

Caveats

This book is explicitly a work of political economy and social criticism, not investing or trading literature; readers looking for market strategy content should look elsewhere in this library. Many of its statistics and case studies (Reagan-era tax data, 1990s Mexico/Russia, dot-com/telecom-era executive pay scandals like Qwest and Lucent) are now dated by two decades and reflect the author’s stated political viewpoint (explicitly critical of laissez-faire and libertarian economics) rather than a neutral survey — readers should treat its historical claims and causal arguments as one side of a long-standing, contested debate in economics rather than settled fact. No trading, technical-analysis, or market-timing content of any kind appears in the book.

Who it is for

Readers interested in economic history and the political debate over free-market versus mixed-economy policy as background context; not relevant for traders seeking strategy, indicators, or risk-management technique.
