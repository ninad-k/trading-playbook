Trade Breakouts for Bigger Profit Potential

Summary

This short manual from Windsor Advisory Services argues that trading breakouts — entering when price moves decisively out of a trading range — outperforms the conventional “buy low, sell high” instinct of waiting for pullbacks. It leans on Alexander Elder’s writing and the Richard Dennis/Bill Eckhardt “Turtles” experiment (an ~80% average compound return) as evidence that following breakouts, not pullbacks, is how large trends are actually captured, because capital sits idle less and probability favors trend continuation over reversal.

Key points

  • A breakout occurs when price exceeds the prior high or low of an established trading range, of any duration.
  • Waiting for a pullback creates competition from five groups of traders (per Elder) crowding the same entry zone, and a deep pullback may itself signal reversal rather than continuation.
  • The Turtles’ success (from the Dennis/Eckhardt bet on whether trading could be taught) is presented as proof that a simple, disciplined breakout-based rule set works even for novices.
  • Bill Eckhardt (via “Market Wizards”): buying retracements is “psychologically seductive” but risks missing the trade entirely or buying at a worse price if the trend has already turned.
  • Breakout trades use capital more efficiently since a trader only commits when a trend is already confirmed in motion, rather than sitting in a range waiting.
  • Breakout validity increases with (1) more prior tests of the support/resistance line (the article’s rule of thumb: never trade fewer than three tests) and (2) a longer time period over which that level has held.
  • Longer-term (monthly/weekly) charts should be scanned first to identify significant levels; daily charts are then used for entry timing.

Actionable rules

  1. Only trade breakouts of support/resistance levels tested at least three times.
  2. Prefer breakout levels that have held for months, not weeks — longer-held levels produce more valid breaks.
  3. Identify significant levels on monthly/weekly charts first, then time entry using the daily chart.
  4. Place stops at or near the breakout point itself; if price reverses back through the breakout level rather than continuing, treat the breakout as false and exit.
  5. Once a strong trend is underway, use pullback breakouts of smaller consolidation ranges (3–5 tests) within that trend as continuation entries.

Caveats

This is a promotional/educational essay, not a rigorous backtest — it relies on anecdote (the Turtles, Market Wizards quotes) rather than the author’s own statistics on breakout win rates, sample size, or false-breakout frequency. No specific instrument, timeframe, or position-sizing framework is given; “trading range” and “significant level” are described qualitatively rather than with precise rules a system could code directly.

Who it is for

Traders new to trend-following who are instinctively drawn to buying dips and need a conceptual case for why entering strength (rather than waiting for a pullback) can be the lower-risk approach, especially into major trend changes.
