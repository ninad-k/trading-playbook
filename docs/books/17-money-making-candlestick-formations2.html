Summary

This is a promotional glossary distributed by INO Global Markets, reproducing graphics and definitions from Steve Nison’s Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques. It defines and illustrates 17 individual and multi-candle formations used in Japanese candlestick charting, giving one short definition and diagram per pattern with no surrounding trading system, backtest, or risk-management discussion.

Key points

  • Belt-hold line — bullish opens on its low (white); bearish opens on its high (black); read as directional at extremes.
  • Counterattack lines — a gap against the prior trend that closes back at the prior session’s close, signaling a stalemate.
  • Dark-cloud cover — bearish reversal: a black candle opens above the prior white candle’s high but closes well into its body.
  • Doji — open and close nearly equal; variants include gravestone and long-legged doji; a building block of larger patterns.
  • Engulfing patterns — bullish (large white body engulfs a small black body in a downtrend) or bearish (mirror image); major reversal signals.
  • Doji star / evening star / evening doji star — three-candle bearish top reversal: tall white body, a gapped small body or doji (“star”), then a black candle closing well into the first body.
  • Hammer / hanging man — same shape (small body, lower shadow ≥2x body, little/no upper shadow); bullish as a hammer in a downtrend, bearish as a hanging man in an uptrend (needs confirmation).
  • Harami / harami cross — a small body (or doji) contained within the prior session’s large body, signaling trend exhaustion; the cross version is a “petrifying pattern.”
  • Inverted hammer / morning star / morning doji star — bullish bottom-reversal mirror images of the shooting star and evening star.
  • Piercing pattern — bullish reversal: a long black candle followed by a gap-down open closing more than halfway into the prior black body.
  • Tweezers top/bottom — sessions testing the same high or low; gains importance combined with another signal (e.g., a harami cross) on the same two candles.

Actionable rules

  1. Classic hammer/hanging man requires the lower shadow to be at least twice the height of the real body, with little or no upper shadow.
  2. A hanging man signal requires bearish confirmation the next session (a black candle with a lower close or weaker open) before acting.
  3. An inverted hammer bottom-reversal requires next-session confirmation (a white candle with a higher close or higher open).
  4. A dark-cloud cover requires the second candle’s close to land “well into” (commentary elsewhere in Nison’s work: more than halfway into) the first candle’s real body — the same halfway rule applies to the piercing pattern in reverse.
  5. Tweezers gain significance when the same two candles also form a separate recognized pattern (e.g., harami cross) — treat as a confluence signal, not standalone.

Caveats

This is a definitional glossary excerpt, not a tested trading system: it gives no win-rate statistics, no entry/exit/stop rules beyond the confirmation notes above, and no discussion of which market conditions or timeframes the patterns are reliable in. It assumes familiarity with basic candlestick anatomy (real body, upper/lower shadow) and should be paired with a fuller source (e.g., Nison’s own books) for context on reliability and false-signal rates.

Who it is for

Traders learning to read candlestick charts who need a quick illustrated reference to the most common reversal formations before studying a fuller candlestick text or building pattern-based entry rules.
