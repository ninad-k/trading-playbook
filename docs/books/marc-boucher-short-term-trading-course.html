Overview

This is a 10-week course originally delivered on TradingMarkets.com by hedge fund manager Mark Boucher (the file’s internal metadata spells the name “Marc,” the text consistently uses “Mark”). Each week is a standalone lesson building toward one integrated approach: find “runaway markets” (stocks or futures moving explosively in one direction), confirm them with relative strength and fundamental “fuel,” time entries with a specific breakout pattern vocabulary the author calls TBBLBG, and control risk with a fixed money-management framework built around protective stops and position sizing. The course opens with a graded quiz on money management before teaching any patterns, which sets the tone: Boucher treats capital preservation as prerequisite knowledge, not an afterthought bolted onto a signal-generation method.

The ten weekly topics are: Money Management, Relative Strength, Runaway Markets, Liquidity Cycles, Indicators, Market Selection, Pattern Recognition, Market Timing, Trading Psychology, and a closing review. The course is written for swing and short-to-intermediate-term traders in stocks and futures, using daily bar charts as the primary timeframe.

Core thesis

Consistent, moderate returns compound into more wealth than sporadic spectacular ones once drawdown is accounted for, so the discipline of protecting capital (money management) has to be established before any entry method is taught. On top of that foundation, the highest-probability trades are found not by timing market tops and bottoms but by market selection: identifying the small number of stocks or futures already in an explosive, well-“fueled” trend (a “runaway market”) and joining that trend early using an objective breakout-pattern vocabulary, rather than trying to anticipate reversals.

Key concepts

  • Open Protective Stop (OPS) — Boucher’s term for a resting stop-loss order; theoretical risk is the distance between entry price and OPS.
  • Trailing OPS — a stop that is walked toward the market as a trade moves favorably, using each new consolidation’s support/resistance as the new stop level.
  • Creeping Commitment — adding to a winning position once the trailing stop has been moved above (or below, for shorts) the original entry, so the initial risk is already eliminated.
  • Runaway Market — a stock or futures contract moving with such momentum that it appears “out of control”; Boucher’s central trading opportunity.
  • TBBLBG — an acronym for Thrust Breakout, Breakaway Lap, and Breakaway Gap, the three interchangeable breakout-bar patterns used to enter and confirm runaway moves.
  • Equity Fuel — a fundamental or narrative reason (earnings growth, new product, sponsorship) that a trend has room to continue, used to filter runaway-pattern candidates.
  • Relative Strength (RS) — the percentage price change of an instrument ranked against all others (or against the S&P 500); used for market selection, not the RSI oscillator.
  • Swing chart / major swing chart — a chart reduced to only its pivot highs and lows, used to make classical chart-pattern recognition (double tops/bottoms, head-and-shoulders) objective and repeatable.
  • Liquidity Cycle — Boucher’s macro framework linking Federal Reserve policy and money supply changes to the onset and end of bull/bear phases.
  • Meteors and Fixed Stars — Boucher’s two categories of big trending stocks: Meteors run up then collapse back to obscurity; Fixed Stars sustain earnings growth and keep advancing at a slower pace.

Rules and setups

  1. Risk per trade: cap Theoretical Risk (entry price minus OPS) at 2% of account capital or less per position (e.g., $20,000 max risk on a $1,000,000 account).
  2. Stop placement: OPS levels are set at market-determined support/resistance from the price action itself, not arbitrary dollar or percent distances.
  3. Trailing stops: move the OPS to the most recent consolidation’s support level each time price breaks out of that consolidation in the trade’s favor; tighten stops further in more volatile conditions.
  4. Entry pattern — Thrust Breakout Up: breakout bar range is 2x or more the average range of the preceding 20 days; volume exceeds the prior day; close is in the top third of the bar’s range; low of the breakout bar is below the prior day’s close.
  5. Entry pattern — Breakaway Lap: breakout bar’s low is above the prior close but below the prior high (a partial gap/”hole” in trading).
  6. Entry pattern — Breakaway Gap: breakout bar’s low is above the prior day’s high (full price gap).
  7. Creeping Commitment sizing: add to a position only once the trailing stop has moved beyond the original entry price, so incremental size carries no risk to initial capital.
  8. Market-selection filter for runaway candidates (simplified version given in the course): 5 or more runaway patterns within a 21-day window; price has not closed below its 200-day moving average since the uptrend began; Relative Strength rank of at least 65 on pullbacks or 80 on new highs; IBD-style EPS rank of 80 or higher; institutional ownership under 16% (start taking profits above 40% sponsorship); long-term debt under 50% of capitalization; a clear fundamental reason (“fuel”) for continued earnings growth.
  9. Classical pattern confirmation, not standalone entries: e.g., a Double Bottom is valid when the first low (A) exceeds the second (C), and the B-to-C distance is within 20% of the C-to-D distance; used to add confidence to a TBBLBG breakout, not traded alone.
  10. Reaction/pullback trades: as a secondary approach when no breakout is available, entries are taken on corrections that retrace no more than 38% of the prior move within a still-confirmed trend.
  11. Trade management default: hold with a trailing stop until stopped out (“let profits run”); exit and stay in cash when no A-grade setup is present (“when in doubt, stay out”).

Risk and money management

Money management is Week 1 of the course and is treated as the primary determinant of long-run success, ahead of any entry method. The framework: (1) minimize losses, because recovering from a large drawdown requires a disproportionately larger gain (a 70% loss needs roughly a 230% gain to recover); (2) judge strategies by compounded/consistent annual return rather than flashy average annual return, since volatile high-average-return strategies compound worse than steady ones; (3) cap theoretical risk per trade at 2% of capital via the OPS; (4) use trailing stops to lock in profit and eliminate risk on the initial capital before adding size (Creeping Commitment); (5) understand markets well enough to know when a given method’s edge has stopped working, rather than trusting any system as infallible — illustrated with a story of two traders using an identical system, one of whom overrode it (and survived) going into the October 1987 crash.

Psychology and discipline

Week 9 is devoted to discipline mechanics rather than general market psychology: Boucher describes using a trading journal, a written daily action plan, visualization, and a “mental trading account” (a practice, credited in part to a joint session with Laurence Connors, of separating a trade’s psychological weight from the physical account) to keep emotional decisions from overriding the plan. Money management concepts throughout the course double as psychological guardrails — for example, the “when in doubt, stay out” rule is framed as protection against the trader’s own impatience, and the “price makes news, not vice versa” rule (markets are said to discount news roughly 6–12 months ahead) is used to argue against trading off headlines and sentiment.

Chapter map

  • Week 1 — Money Management: 20 numbered rules; OPS, trailing stops, Creeping Commitment, “trend is your friend,” market selection over prediction.
  • Week 2 — Relative Strength: RS as the course’s preferred market-selection tool over market timing; IBD-style and S&P-relative RS variants.
  • Week 3 — Runaway Markets: Meteors vs. Fixed Stars; introduces the TBBLBG pattern vocabulary and the “early identification” pattern frequencies (e.g., swing breakouts on TBBLBG present in 85% of a 1,300-stock sample).
  • Week 4 — Liquidity Cycles: macro framework linking Fed policy/money supply to bull and bear market onset.
  • Week 5 — Indicators: technical indicators used for confirmation, introduced after the market-selection foundation is set.
  • Week 6 — Market Selection: the full runaway-market filter criteria (RS rank, EPS rank, institutional ownership, debt, fuel) and gap/continuation/corrective pattern catalog.
  • Week 7 — Pattern Recognition: swing charts and major swing charts; classical chart patterns (double tops/bottoms, head-and-shoulders) as confirmation, not standalone signals.
  • Week 8 — Market Timing: entry/exit timing layered on top of the market-selection and pattern framework.
  • Week 9 — Trading Psychology: journal, daily action plan, visualization, mental trading account.
  • Week 10 — Review: a self-test (multiple choice/true-false with answers) recapping all prior weeks, closing on money management as “the real Holy Grail.”

Strengths and caveats

The course is unusually concrete for a discretionary swing-trading text: pattern definitions are stated in terms a programmer could translate directly into code (bar range multiples, close-location rules, moving-average and RS-rank thresholds), and the money-management rules carry specific numbers (2% risk, 20% pattern-symmetry tolerances, 38% pullback limits) rather than vague guidance. The frequency statistics behind the runaway-pattern catalog (e.g., “85% of 1,300 runaway markets”) are asserted from the author’s own research and are not independently reproducible from the text — treat them as descriptive claims, not audited backtest results. The course predates decimalization and modern electronic markets (written in 1999, referencing Quote.com QCharts and the dot-com-era bull market), so liquidity and slippage assumptions may not transfer directly to current markets. The RS variants and IBD-style EPS ranks depend on data services (Investor’s Business Daily, TradingMarkets.com) that a modern reader would need to replicate independently.

Who should read it

Suited to swing and short-to-intermediate-term traders in stocks or futures who want an integrated system (market selection plus a specific breakout pattern plus a numeric risk framework) rather than a loose collection of indicators. Less useful for pure day traders or for anyone seeking options- or fixed-income-specific material.
