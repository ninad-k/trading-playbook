Overview

Written as a first-person narrative by journalist Edwin Lefèvre but universally read as a thinly veiled biography of Jesse Livermore (“Larry Livingston” in the text), the book traces a trading life from a fourteen-year-old quotation-board boy beating bucket shops on pure tape reading, through repeated fortunes made and lost on the New York Stock Exchange, to a mature operator who manipulates stock distributions for banks and syndicates. There is no worked-out mechanical system; the book is a sequence of anecdotes (the 1907 panic, the July 1907 corn-corner escape, the July cotton corner, the Percy Thomas cotton disaster, the Imperial Steel and Consolidated Stove manipulations) from which the narrator repeatedly extracts the same handful of lessons about reading price action, sizing positions, and controlling one’s own psychology. Because it is narrative rather than a manual, this page extracts Livermore’s stated principles as rules rather than treating any one anecdote as a codeable system.

Core thesis

Speculation is won or lost on two axes that are almost independent of stock-picking cleverness: reading what prices are actually doing right now (the tape, not tips, opinions, or “explanations”) and controlling one’s own hope and fear, which push traders to do the opposite of what a cold reading of the tape recommends. The narrator states repeatedly that no one beats the market itself, only individual opportunities at individual times, and that the money is made “by sitting tight” through a correctly diagnosed trend, not by cleverness in and out.

Key concepts

  • Line of least resistance — the tape’s evident tendency (up, down, or “getting nowhere”) at a given moment; the trader’s job is to wait for it to define itself, then trade with it, not to guess it in advance.
  • Tape reading — inferring the balance of buying versus selling pressure from price and volume action itself, independent of news, opinions, or “why.”
  • Sitting tight — holding a correctly diagnosed position through the whole move instead of trading in and out of it; the narrator states plainly that the big money is made this way, not from being clever about entries.
  • Pyramiding — adding to a position only after it has already shown a profit, never averaging into a loser.
  • General conditions — studying the overall market and business cycle rather than only the individual stock; a stated turning point in the narrator’s development from tape-only trading to conditions-based trading.
  • Playing a lone hand — trading strictly on one’s own reading, refusing partnerships, tips, or another trader’s conclusions, because dependence on someone else’s information leaves you unable to act when they are unavailable or wrong.
  • The public’s hope/fear inversion — ordinary traders hope when they should fear (holding losers, waiting for a bad position to “come back”) and fear when they should hope (selling winners too soon); the professional trains himself to do the reverse.
  • Manipulation as an extension of trading, not a separate skill — the narrator’s stock-distribution campaigns (Imperial Steel, Consolidated Stove) succeed because they follow the same tape-reading and line-of-least-resistance principles as ordinary trading; when a manipulated stock stops responding to buying, he stops, exactly as he would with any position.
  • Trading to meet a need — using the market to pay for a specific expense (the “sable coat” anecdote) is identified as a reliable way to lose, because it forces trades on a timetable set by need rather than by the tape.

Rules and setups

The book offers no fixed entry/exit mechanics with specific percentages beyond the anecdotes below; the numbers given are the ones actually stated in the text.

  1. Do not anticipate a breakout from a narrow, “getting nowhere” range. Wait for price to actually cross the range’s resistance or support before trading it (the wheat example: wait for $1.20 to be crossed rather than buying at $1.14 in anticipation).
  2. Build a position in stages, not all at once. Buy an initial partial line (Livermore describes buying about one-fifth of an intended full line to start, or splitting a cotton line into 10,000-bale tranches with further tranches added only after each prior one is profitable).
  3. If the initial partial position does not show a profit, it is wrong — get out; do not add to it and do not wait for it to “come back.”
  4. Never average down into a loser. The Percy Thomas cotton episode (accumulating roughly 440,000 bales while a loss grew because another man’s opinion had displaced the narrator’s own reading) is presented as the era’s costliest illustration of this rule.
  5. Sell what shows a loss; keep what shows a profit — stated explicitly as the reverse of what the narrator actually did in the Percy Thomas episode, to his own detriment.
  6. Never trade at a fixed limit price when you need to exit; sell (or buy) at the market when the position needs to be closed, because a limit order can fail to fill during exactly the move you were trying to escape.
  7. In a large position, look for the market’s own capacity to absorb your line before trying to exit; forcing size into an unready market moves the price against you (the July cotton corner was unwound cleanly only because a newspaper story happened to manufacture buying demand).
  8. As a manipulator or large trader, keep buying only as long as the buying itself moves the price up (or selling only as long as it moves the price down); when the market stops responding, stop and reassess — do not force it.
  9. Treat a friend’s stop-loss discipline as a system to be followed exactly, not improvised on: the Pat Hearne anecdote describes buying 100 shares of an active stock, adding another 100 on each 1-point advance, and placing a stop 1 point below the last purchase, moved up with the price — abandoning this exact discipline (letting a loss run “as though profits”) is shown wiping out a trader who had proven it worked.

Risk and money management

The book gives no percentage-of-equity risk rule. Its money-management logic is entirely about when to add size and when to walk away: accumulate only into strength (never into weakness), treat the cost of small “feeling out” losses on partial positions as cheap insurance against loading up at the wrong time, and cut a losing position completely rather than partially. The narrator states his one-point trailing-stop story (Pat Hearne, above) as the clearest numeric illustration of a hard stop in the text. He also warns that trading in size changes the game: a large line cannot be exited at will the way a small one can, so an oversized position carries hidden liquidity risk beyond the price risk of the trade itself. Separately, he identifies trading to cover a specific cash need (rather than trading only when a real opportunity is read from the tape) as a disguised way of taking on excess risk.

Psychology and discipline

This is the book’s real subject. Named psychological failure modes, all illustrated with the narrator’s own losses: relying on someone else’s conviction instead of one’s own tape reading (Percy Thomas); being talked into a purchase by a persuasive personality even against one’s own judgment (the book-agent anecdote, told as a warning that precedes the costlier Percy Thomas episode); trading out of impatience or a need for excitement rather than a genuine signal; refusing to accept a loss because doing so feels like admitting error; and taking profits too early out of fear after a real trend has only begun. The counterexample offered as the ideal is Mr. Partridge, nicknamed “Old Turkey,” a fellow customer who answers every stock tip and every suggestion to trade in and out with only “well, you know, this is a bull market” — the narrator singles him out as embodying the rare discipline of being right and sitting tight, which he calls harder to learn than any technical skill in the book. The narrator’s own summary is that hope and fear are backwards in most traders: they hope when a loss is growing (and so let it grow further) and fear when a profit is growing (and so cut it short); the professional inverts both.

Chapter map

  • Ch I — Boy Plunger — Learns tape reading as a bucket-shop board boy; first trades and the bucket shops’ retaliation.
  • Ch II — Cornered by a Bucket Shop — The rigged Sugar trade that finally drives him out of bucket shops into a real brokerage.
  • Ch III — Learning the Real Game — Losing badly at Fullerton’s; realizes bucket-shop tape reading does not transfer to real execution and slippage.
  • Ch IV–VIII — Early New York Years — Repeated stakes, losses, and comebacks; first exposure to insider tape manipulation, tipsters, and general-conditions thinking.
  • Ch IX — Anaconda and the Line of Least Resistance — A Florida vacation interrupted; introduces the line-of-least-resistance concept explicitly through an Anaconda trade.
  • Ch X — The Tape-Reading System — Pat Hearne’s stop-loss/pyramiding method; the hope/fear inversion stated as the speculator’s core psychological task.
  • Ch XI — The 1907 Panic and the Corn Corner — Covering shorts at Morgan’s request during the 1907 panic; escaping a cornered corn position via a manufactured oats sell-off.
  • Ch XII — Percy Thomas and the Cotton Disaster — Abandoning his own judgment for a persuasive partner’s; the book’s central cautionary tale on averaging losers.
  • Ch XIII — Broke Again — Rock bottom after the cotton losses; rebuilding discipline.
  • Ch XIV — The Comeback (Bethlehem Steel, 1915) — A patient six-week wait for one high-conviction trade to rebuild a stake.
  • Ch XV–XIX — Tips, Insiders, and Cornered Stocks — Chapters on the psychology of tip-giving and tip-seeking, and further insider/corner episodes (coffee, Tropical Trading).
  • Ch XX–XXII — Manipulation Campaigns — How he manipulates Imperial Steel and Consolidated Stove for syndicates, using the same tape-reading logic as ordinary trading.
  • Ch XXIII–XXIV — Closing Reflections — Warnings to the public about insider tip-planting, market letters, and press manipulation.

Strengths and caveats

The prose is vivid and the psychological observations (hope/fear inversion, the danger of a persuasive personality, the discipline of sitting tight) have aged extremely well and are widely cited by later authors. However, the book is entirely anecdotal: no statistics, win rates, or backtests are offered anywhere, and the “rules” above are the narrator’s own retrospective generalizations from a small number of remembered trades, which may be flattered by hindsight and survivorship. The market-manipulation chapters (Imperial Steel, Consolidated Stove) describe practices — cornering, pool operations, deliberately alternating buying and selling to build an illusion of demand, feeding stories to floor traders — that are illegal under modern securities law and are of historical rather than practical interest today. Prices, share counts, and margin terms throughout reflect pre-1929, pre-SEC, often pre-decimal markets and do not translate directly to modern position sizing.

Who should read it

Any discretionary trader looking for the classic articulation of trading psychology and tape-based trend-following; useful as a motivational and psychological companion to more mechanical texts rather than as a source of testable rules. Less useful to someone looking for a specific, codeable entry/exit system — for that, see the companion system page below, drawn from a later book that reconstructs Livermore’s methods more explicitly.
