Summary

This is a reference glossary, not a trading strategy book. The author, publisher of the Portfolio Crafter site, explains that most beginning investors don’t know what CPI, PMI, or ECI mean, and sets out to define the twenty U.S. economic indicators most followed by investors so readers can judge their likely effect on interest rates and portfolios. Each entry follows an identical template: Definition, Importance (how the Fed/market interprets it), Source, Availability (release day/time), Frequency, and Revisions.

Key points

  • Beige Book: Fed’s anecdotal district-by-district summary, released the Wednesday before each FOMC meeting, eight times a year.
  • ISM Manufacturing / Services Indices: readings above 50 signal expansion, below 50 contraction; Manufacturing is the “king” index, released first business day of the month, Services the third.
  • Consumer Price Index (CPI): core CPI (ex food/energy) is the key inflation gauge; larger-than-expected core gains signal inflationary pressure.
  • Employment Situation: the single most closely watched release; full employment is treated as roughly 5.5-6.0% unemployment, with healthy GDP growth at 2.0-2.5% in that regime.
  • Initial Claims: released weekly; a move of 30,000+ is flagged as a substantial labor-market shift.
  • Capacity Utilization: above 85% is flagged as generating inflationary pressure.
  • GDP, Durable Goods, Housing Starts, Home Sales, Personal Income, Retail Sales, PPI, Philadelphia Fed, Chicago PMI, ECI, Trade Balance: each covered with the same template; consumer spending is repeatedly noted as roughly two-thirds of the economy.

Actionable rules

None given as trading rules — this is a reference glossary for reading the economic calendar, not a system. The implicit rule for a trader is: know the release date and threshold level (the 50 line for ISM, the 5.5-6.0% band for unemployment, 85% for capacity utilization, a 30,000 move for claims) for whichever indicators are scheduled ahead of a trade, since these are the levels the book identifies as market-moving.

Caveats

Written in 2002 with data sources, contact URLs, and release-time conventions (e.g., “Raw Data” links to government sites) that are dated and may no longer resolve or match current release schedules; several data series discussed (e.g., Chicago PMI as an ISM preview) have institutional changes since publication. It has no discussion of price reaction patterns, only definitions — pair it with a source on actual market response to surprises.

Who it is for

Beginner-to-intermediate macro or forex traders who need a plain-language reference for what each U.S. economic release measures and why it matters, to read alongside an economic calendar.
