Overview

Written by William J. O’Neil, founder of William O’Neil & Co. and Investor’s Business Daily, this is the foundational text for the CAN SLIM growth-investing system, built from a 40-year (1953–1993) statistical study of the market’s biggest stock-price winners before their major advances. The book’s structure mirrors that research process: Part 1 lays out the seven-factor CAN SLIM checklist letter by letter with historical case studies (Xerox, Syntex, Cisco, Wal-Mart, Amgen); Part 2 covers practical mechanics (brokers, margin, shorting, options, mutual funds) and — at length — exactly when to sell, both to cut losses and to take profits; Part 3 covers reading charts, using the daily financial pages and tape, picking industry groups, and a final list of the 18 most common investor mistakes. Historical price/earnings models of past winning stocks and their exact chart patterns at the moment they broke out are used throughout as the evidentiary backbone for each rule.

Core thesis

The market’s biggest winners share a small, repeatable, and identifiable set of fundamental and technical traits just before their major price advances begin — the acronym CAN SLIM (Current earnings, Annual earnings, New products/management/highs, Supply and demand, Leader or laggard, Institutional sponsorship, Market direction) — and a disciplined investor who screens for all seven factors together, buys at the correct technical entry point (a “pivot” breaking out of a proper price base), and follows strict, predetermined, numeric sell rules will outperform investors who rely on valuation metrics (low P/E), personal opinion, or “buy and hold regardless” strategies. Selling is treated as equally important as buying: cutting every loss at a small, fixed percentage and being willing to take profits systematically is what allows a trader to be wrong most of the time and still make money, because losses stay small while a minority of correctly identified winners are allowed to run.

Key concepts

  • CAN SLIM — the seven-letter screening acronym: Current quarterly earnings, Annual earnings growth, New products/management/highs, Supply and demand, Leader or laggard, Institutional sponsorship, Market direction.
  • Relative strength (RS) rating — a 1–99 scale measuring a stock’s price performance against all other stocks over the trailing 6–12 months; the 500 best-performing stocks from 1953–1993 averaged an RS of 87 just before their major advance began.
  • Earnings stability rating — a 1–99 scale (lower is more stable) measuring how consistently a company’s quarterly earnings have tracked their five-year trend line; stable growers typically score below 20–25.
  • Base / pivot buy point — a sideways price-consolidation pattern (7–8 weeks to 15 months) a stock builds after a prior advance and correction; the pivot is the exact price at which the stock breaks out of that base on rising volume, and the correct buy point.
  • Floating supply — shares available for purchase after excluding stock closely held by management/insiders; a smaller floating supply combined with rising demand produces sharper price moves.
  • Follow-through day — the market-direction confirmation signal: on roughly the 4th–7th day (as early as the 3rd, weakness if after the 10th) of an attempted rally off a low, a major index closes up 1% or more on volume higher than the prior day, confirming a new uptrend has begun.
  • Sympathy stock — a secondary, weaker company in the same industry as a genuine group leader, which rises “in sympathy” with the leader’s move but underperforms it; O’Neil advises buying only the true leader.
  • Cup-with-handle and other base patterns — the price-base chart shapes (covered in the charting chapter) used to identify a proper base and its exact pivot point, as opposed to a “wide and loose,” faulty base.
  • Distribution — heavy-volume selling by institutions visible as stalled or falling prices on high volume, used both to spot a stock topping and to spot the general market topping.
  • Pyramiding (averaging up) — adding to a winning position as it moves in the anticipated direction (a technique O’Neil credits to Jesse Livermore), as opposed to averaging down into a loser.

Rules and setups

  1. C — Current quarterly earnings. Require current-quarter EPS up at least 20–50% (or more) versus the same quarter one year earlier; the best winners often show 100–500%. A mediocre 8–10% increase is explicitly called insufficient. Compare like-for-like quarters (e.g., December quarter vs. prior December quarter, never vs. the immediately preceding quarter).
  2. A — Annual earnings growth. Require annual EPS growth of roughly 25–50% (or more, sometimes 100%+) compounded per year over the last 3–5 years, with no more than one down year in five (and a quick recovery to new highs if there is one). Prefer an earnings-stability rating below 20–25.
  3. N — New factor plus new high. Look for a new product, service, or management change, and buy only as the stock approaches or breaks out to a new price high after a proper base (7–8 weeks to 15 months of consolidation) — not stocks still well off their highs. Do not buy more than 5–10% above the exact pivot/breakout price.
  4. S — Supply and demand. Favor smaller share counts outstanding (the 40-year winners study found the median winning stock had about 4.6 million shares outstanding, average 11.8 million, and more than 95% had fewer than 25 million shares before their big move) over large-cap, heavily supplied stocks; require breakout volume at least 50% above normal (often 100%+); favor companies buying back their own stock and reducing debt-to-equity.
  5. L — Leader vs. laggard. Buy only the #1 or #2 stock (by relative strength and price action) in a leading industry group, never a “sympathy” play trading on the leader’s coattails. Require a relative strength rating of at least 70 (avoid below 70), prefer 80+, with the strongest winners historically near 90+ just before breakout.
  6. I — Institutional sponsorship. Favor stocks with at least a few, but not an excessive number of, institutional owners, with the trend in the number of sponsors increasing quarter-over-quarter and recent new positions taken by funds with strong performance records; avoid stocks with no institutional following (poor liquidity) and avoid extremely overowned mega-caps.
  7. M — Market direction. Determine overall market trend daily from the price/volume action of a major index (Dow, S&P, or a broad index); confirm a new bull leg only after a “follow-through day” (index up 1%+ on higher volume, typically the 4th–7th day of an attempted rally); do not fight the market’s own signal with personal opinion.
  8. Stop-loss rule. Cut every loss at 7–8% below the purchase price, without exception; this is described as “the absolute best protection” against catastrophic losses and requires no forecasting skill, only discipline.
  9. Profit-taking rule. Take profits at 20–25% gains as a default target; exception — if a stock gains 20% in fewer than 8 weeks (a sign of unusual strength), hold it at least 8 weeks before reassessing rather than selling immediately.
  10. Pyramid, don’t average down. Add to positions that move in your favor after purchase (Livermore-style pyramiding); never add to a losing position to lower the average cost.
  11. 36 supplementary sell signals (selected, numeric ones): sell if a stock declines 8% or more from its peak and shows a deteriorating pattern; treat a 15% pullback as material and 20%+ as a likely trend change; sell if relative strength drops below 70; sell if a stock runs up 25–30% (rarely 50%) in one to two weeks around a stock split, since this often marks a top; sell into “climax” runs of rapid advance over two to three consecutive weeks (8–12 trading days).
  12. Market-top defensive rule. On recognizing a general market top, raise at least 25% cash quickly, more aggressively (and faster) if on margin — since a 50%-margined portfolio down 20% in price has lost 40% of equity.

Risk and money management

Risk control centers on one non-negotiable numeric rule — cut every loss at 7–8% below cost, always — which the book frames as more important than being right on stock selection, because it caps the downside on the majority of trades that will be wrong. O’Neil illustrates the asymmetry directly: being wrong twice at an 8% loss each and right once at a 20% gain still nets a profit, and a documented backtest in the book shows an approach of buying market leaders and cutting losses near 7% averaging about a 20% gain on winners versus roughly a 7% average loss — producing a strongly positive expectancy despite a sub-100% win rate. Leverage risk is called out explicitly: a fully margined (50%) portfolio suffers double the percentage loss of an unmargined one on any given decline. Diversification is treated cautiously — O’Neil favors concentrated positions in a few thoroughly researched leaders over broad diversification, on the view that over-diversification dilutes the effect of correctly identifying a true winner. Market-level risk is managed by tracking the general market’s daily trend and raising cash (25%+ immediately, more if margined) at the first credible signs of a market top, rather than waiting for confirmation across many indicators.

Psychology and discipline

O’Neil frames his own selling discipline as forged through a specific personal failure: in 1961 he was “dead right” on stock selection for a year but gave back all his profits by holding winners (including Certain-teed, which later tripled after he sold too early on a shakeout, then also failed to have any stop-loss plan on other positions) without predetermined sell rules, prompting a six-month post-mortem analysis of every trade that produced the 7-8%/20-25% profit-and-loss plan. Recurring psychological themes: sell your worst-performing stock first and let your best performer run longer; avoid “falling in love” with a stock or refusing to sell because of a sentimental or sunk-cost attachment; treat market letters, opinions, and forecasts skeptically — “you do not need to know what the market is going to do, only what it has actually done.” The book closes with a chapter of 18 named common investor mistakes (buying on tips, averaging down, buying cheap low-quality stocks, lacking a selling plan, being over-diversified, trading options/futures without expertise, among others) intended as a checklist against self-sabotage.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s core strength is that every CAN SLIM criterion is backed by a specific number (20-50% current EPS growth, 25-50% annual growth, RS rating ≥70/80/90, float under ~25 million shares, 50%+ volume surge, 7-8% stop, 20-25% profit target) and grounded in a stated 40-year historical study, making the system unusually easy to convert into a checklist or screen. The main caveat is survivorship bias in the underlying research: the CAN SLIM traits were reverse-engineered from stocks already known to have become the era’s biggest winners (Xerox, Syntex, Wal-Mart, Cisco, Amgen), and the book does not report the false-positive rate — how many stocks met the same CAN SLIM criteria at the time and failed to perform. Historical examples and market anecdotes (1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, 1973-74 bear market, 1987 crash, early-1990s biotech and computer names) are specific to those market eras, and some mechanics (fixed brokerage commissions history, margin rules, capital-gains holding periods referenced in the sell rules) reflect pre-2000 U.S. market structure and tax law. The system is also fairly labor-intensive to run by hand (daily market averages, weekly relative-strength updates, chart-pattern review), historically mitigated by the Investor’s Business Daily and Daily Graphs products O’Neil’s own firm published.

Who should read it

Best suited to a swing-to-position-term equity investor (holding periods of weeks to well over a year) who wants a rules-based growth-stock system combining fundamental screens (earnings growth) with technical entry/exit timing (chart bases, relative strength, market-direction confirmation), and who is willing to apply strict, mechanical stop-losses. Less suited to value investors (the system explicitly favors high-growth, often high-P/E stocks over “cheap” ones), to buy-and-hold long-term investors uninterested in active chart monitoring, or to short-term day traders (the holding periods and base-building timeframes described run into weeks and months).
