Summary

A classroom handout from MarketWise Trading School covering option fundamentals: contract mechanics, symbology, settlement, and moneyness; the six basic positions (long/short stock, long/short call, long/short put) and their synthetic equivalents; pricing models (Black-Scholes, binomial, and adjustments for American-style options); the option Greeks; and a large catalog of strategies from covered calls through complex spreads and repair strategies. A trade-planning and trade-management section closes the book with concrete stop-loss and profit-taking guidance.

Key points

  • Explains the basics: 100-share contract standardization, expiration/exercise, open interest, opening rotation, and rights vs. obligations for calls and puts.
  • Covers pricing models: Black-Scholes, the binomial model, and American-option adjustments (Roll-Geske-Whaley, Barone-Adesi-Whaley).
  • Defines all five Greeks (delta, gamma, vega, theta, and position-level delta/gamma) and their role in position risk.
  • Strategy catalog includes covered call, covered put, married put/call, long calls/puts, debit spreads (bullish/bearish, horizontal/time), credit spreads, ratio spreads, straddles/strangles (long and short), and complex trades (long/short butterfly, iron butterfly, condor, risk collar/fence).
  • Includes a stock-and-option repair-strategy section for salvaging a losing position.
  • Frames trading as a business: define time frame (short/medium/long term), select underlyings by technical or fundamental analysis, and set money-management rules before entering a trade.
  • Distinguishes stop-loss placement by option price (tighter, but subject to widening bid/ask when the stock moves fast) versus by stock price (“catastrophe insurance,” less prone to option-price lag).
  • Worked profit example: a $10,000 account taking a 25% profit once every two weeks (without reinvesting/compounding) would net roughly $62,500 over a year.

Actionable rules

  1. Before entering any trade, define the exact dollar or percentage loss exit in advance; “lose” means sell for a loss, not hold and hope.
  2. Set a limit sell for a predetermined profit target immediately after entering (example given: buy a $3 option, place a $6 limit sell for a 100% profit).
  3. Use option-price stops for exits close to the current price; use stock-price stops as catastrophe insurance for larger, less frequent risk of a fast intraday move.
  4. Example stock-price stop: a $65 stock backing a $3-to-$6 option position could carry a stop at $58.50 (about a 10% drop) to guard against a disaster move without reacting to every small tick.
  5. Re-entering a position after a stopped-out loss must be justified as a fresh, independent decision — not a re-buy driven by attachment to the prior trade.

Caveats

This is a strategy/mechanics reference, not a backtested system — no win-rate or edge statistics are given for any of the listed spreads. The 2002 vintage means some platform references (e.g., named brokers for stock-price stop orders) are outdated. Pricing-model coverage (Black-Scholes, binomial) is descriptive only; no worked numerical examples are given for either model in the excerpted material.

Who it is for

Traders who already understand stock trading and want a single-document reference to option mechanics, the Greeks, and the standard strategy menu, plus basic trade-management and stop-placement rules for option positions.
