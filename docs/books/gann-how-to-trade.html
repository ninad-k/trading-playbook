How to Trade

W. D. Gann · 1935 · Tier B article intermediate

A November 1935 lesson linking trend alignment, preplanned stops, paper practice and additions to profitable stock positions.

Summary

This five-page lesson, signed in November 1935, explains Gann’s preparation, risk and pyramiding rules. It assumes the reader already knows his forecasting, angle and time-cycle lessons. Its durable study questions concern trend alignment, where a trade becomes wrong, and when adding exposure increases aggregate risk. References use PDF pages, corresponding to printed F-17 through F-21.

Key points

  • Preparation: compare monthly, weekly and daily trends, support/resistance, volume and previous reaction sizes before entry (PDF pp. 1-2).
  • Defined invalidation: identify the stop location before taking a position. Gann repeatedly prefers historical stock-price stops no more than three points away, with closer stops where feasible (pp. 1-3).
  • Waiting: remain out while price is trapped within an unbroken range or between important angles; paper-trade until the rules can be applied consistently (p. 2).
  • Capital rule: the lesson divides capital into ten parts and limits initial trade risk to one part. Following three successive losses, it calls for a smaller trading unit based on remaining capital (p. 3).
  • Pyramiding: add after favorable movement and move stops on the combined position, rather than adding simply because the original view remains unchanged (pp. 3-4).
  • Exhaustion risk: avoid new additions near double tops when long, double bottoms when short, or unresolved narrow ranges (p. 5).

Actionable rules

  1. Record the trend evidence, proposed entry and protective stop before execution; if the direction or stop cannot be identified, the lesson says to wait (pp. 1-2).
  2. In its worked stock example, add a second lot only after a five-point favorable move, then place the stop on both lots no more than three points from the second entry. A stop-out then combines a two-point gain on the first lot with a three-point loss on the second: a one-point net loss across equal lots, before costs (pp. 3-4).
  3. A separate smaller-addition sequence starts with 100 shares, then adds 50, 30 and 20 shares at successive five-point favorable intervals (p. 4). This is a historical illustration, not a volatility-adjusted sizing model.

Caveats

Risking 10% of capital per trade is aggressive, and the text’s assurances of success are unsupported. Fixed stock-price points do not transfer directly across prices, assets or leverage. The missing angle/cycle lessons prevent reproduction of the complete entry method. No backtest, slippage treatment or gap-loss protection is supplied. All five scans were read visually; their absent text layer did not make them unreadable.

Who it is for

Readers studying Gann’s historical trading process and the arithmetic of adding to winners, with attention to the assumptions behind his examples.
