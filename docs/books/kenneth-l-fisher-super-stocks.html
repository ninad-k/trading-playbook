Overview

Kenneth Fisher (founder of Fisher Investments) wrote this in 1984 to introduce a valuation tool he pioneered: the Price/Sales Ratio (PSR), later paired with a companion Price/Research Ratio (PRR) for technology names. His argument is that earnings- and asset-based valuation (P/E ratios, Ben Graham-style book value screens) systematically miss the biggest winners, because fast-growing companies routinely pass through a period of little or negative earnings — a “glitch” — during which conventional analysts abandon them. Sales, unlike earnings, rarely go to zero or negative, making the PSR a more stable lens. The book builds the theory, then works two extended real case studies (Verbatim Corporation and California Microwave) to show the method applied.

Core thesis

A “Super Stock” is the stock of a “Super Company” (a business with specific, identifiable qualitative traits) bought at a price appropriate to an inferior company — almost always right after a glitch has scared conventional, earnings-focused investors away. Fisher’s target: 3–10x return in three to five years, a 25–100% annualized rate. The PSR is the primary tool for finding this mispricing because it stays calculable and comparable even when earnings are negative or meaningless.

Key concepts

  • Glitch — a temporary earnings/growth setback that nearly every young, fast-growing company suffers as it matures (bad inventory, mistimed product cycles, quality problems); it depresses the stock far more than it depresses the underlying business, creating the buying window.
  • Price Sales Ratio (PSR) — total market value ÷ trailing 12-month corporate sales; Fisher’s core valuation metric, usable even when a company is losing money.
  • Price Research Ratio (PRR) — total market value ÷ trailing 12-month R&D expense; a secondary cross-check specific to technology companies.
  • Super Company — a business combining growth orientation, marketing excellence, an “unfair advantage,” positive personnel culture, and superior financial controls.
  • Unfair advantage — a durable competitive edge, usually being the lowest-cost producer and/or holding a proprietary market position.
  • Margin analysis — forecasting a company’s profit margins several years out to translate a given PSR into an implied future P/E (Fisher provides a lookup table relating PSR, margin, and implied P/E).
  • Anteaters — Fisher’s term for large, resource-rich competitors that crush small companies that stray into markets big enough to attract them; Super Companies deliberately compete in niches too small to interest anteaters.
  • Future-loss coverage — balance-sheet strength measured in years of survivable losses, not just a debt ratio.
  • Smokestack stock — a mature, cyclical, basic-industry company (steel, chemicals, autos) valued with its own, lower PSR bands rather than the Super Company bands.
  • Turkey Market — Fisher’s term for the phase of excess and reversal that follows a bull-market top, distinct from an ordinary bear market.

Rules and setups

Fisher gives explicit numeric thresholds rather than vague guidance:

  1. PSR entry (Super Companies): buy at a PSR of 0.75 or less. Avoid anything with a PSR above 1.5; never buy above 3.0.
  2. PSR exit: sell when the PSR rises to between 3.0 and 6.0; a risk-averse investor sells at 3.0.
  3. Smokestack/basic-industry stocks: a separate band — buy under a PSR of 0.4, sell as the PSR approaches 0.8 (or as low as 0.6 if the company’s prospects are mediocre).
  4. Huge (multi-billion-dollar sales) companies: buy near a PSR of 0.4, sell as it approaches 0.8, scaled to future margin potential — big companies structurally carry lower PSRs than small ones.
  5. PRR entry (technology Super Companies): buy in the 5–10 range; never buy above 15. PRRs below 5 are rare because another company or investor has usually already bid the stock up.
  6. PRR as a cross-check: a low PRR (~5) alongside a marginally high PSR (~1.0) suggests underpriced R&D worth investigating further; a high PRR (25+) alongside a deceptively low PSR (~0.75) signals a company starving its future product pipeline — a value trap, not a Super Stock.
  7. Balance-sheet filter: less than 40% of total assets financed by debt; free working capital sufficient to cover at least five years of the worst plausible losses and at least three years of negative cash flow.
  8. Management-ownership filter: management’s stock ownership should be at least 10x its combined annual salary; prefer auditing by a top-tier accounting firm.
  9. Holding period: hold a genuine Super Stock indefinitely as long as its PSR stays low and the qualitative traits persist — a company compounding sales at 25%/year at a constant PSR of 0.6 could roughly triple in 5 years, 9x in 10 years, and 85x in 20 years.
  10. Timeframe: this is a multi-year fundamental holding strategy (three to five years to realize the target 3–10x), not a trading system — Fisher explicitly warns against using PSR bands to actively trade in and out of a stock.

Risk and money management

Fisher’s risk control is qualitative and balance-sheet based, not a position-sizing or stop-loss system — no per-trade percentage-of-capital rule is given anywhere in the book. The controls are: avoid competitive (“anteater”) risk by favoring companies in niches too small to attract giants; require a balance-sheet cushion (five years of worst-case losses, three years of negative cash flow, under 40% debt-to-assets); require management-ownership alignment (≥10x salary); and treat concentrated “controlling interest” ownership as a flag requiring extra scrutiny of the individuals involved, since a board cannot remove an underperforming controlling owner. There is no scaling of position size to conviction or volatility; the entire position is exited when the PSR becomes “excessively high” (roughly 2–5x, tighter for larger companies) or when the company loses its Super Company traits.

Psychology and discipline

The book’s psychological thread is that Wall Street punishes a stumbling company emotionally as much as financially: years of good results build “superhumanlike” faith in management that later blinds investors to real deterioration, and the same overreaction runs in reverse when a glitch hits, driving the price down far more than the business has actually weakened. Fisher’s prescription is to buy into pessimism — into a stock the Street calls a “turkey” — and to ignore market-timing and forecasting entirely (“opportunities are seldom labeled”), staying process-driven around PSR/PRR and Super Company traits rather than reactive to headlines or short-term earnings.

Strengths and caveats

Strengths: the PSR/margin-analysis framework is genuinely durable — it is still cited in valuation work decades later — and the case studies (Verbatim, Datapoint, Alcoa, PPG, Universal Leaf) are concrete and numeric rather than anecdotal.

Caveats: written in 1984, with dated references to $2,000–4,000 home computers, dial-up data services (“The Source”), Big Eight accounting firms (long since consolidated to the Big Four), and fractional (pre-decimalization) share pricing throughout. The absolute PSR/PRR thresholds are calibrated to early-1980s valuation levels and inflation; Fisher himself notes they must scale with company size and inflation, so applying “0.75” or “5–10” literally in a very different market regime requires judgment rather than rote application. The evidentiary base is thin — essentially two detailed case studies plus a handful of historical illustrations — for what is presented as a general rule. There is no discussion of taxes, shorting, options, or portfolio-level diversification.

Who should read it

Fundamental and value-oriented investors wanting a sales-based (rather than earnings-based) valuation framework for growth and distressed-growth companies, especially small- and mid-cap technology names; not useful for short-term traders or anyone seeking a mechanical, backtestable system.
