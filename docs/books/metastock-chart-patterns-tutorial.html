Summary

This tutorial presents the author’s visual definitions of eleven classical stock-chart formations: cup and handle, flat base, ascending triangle, parabolic curve, wedge, channel, symmetrical triangle, descending triangle, flags and pennants, head and shoulders, and inverted head and shoulders (pp. 1-12). It links trend changes to supply-and-demand imbalances and repeatedly uses volume behavior to describe a formation or breakout. The document makes broad claims about forecasting reliability but supplies no cited study, probabilities, measured-move targets, stop method, or risk-management procedure.

Key points

  • The cup-and-handle description starts after a two-to-four-month advance, gives a possible 20%-35% correction and eight-to-twelve-week cup, and describes a handle lasting about four days to three weeks near 5% below the old high (p. 2).
  • A flat base is horizontal action with drying volume; the stated buy point is a break above its upper trendline accompanied by increasing volume (p. 3).
  • Ascending triangles have flat tops and rising lower boundaries and are described as most reliable in uptrends; descending triangles have flat bottoms and falling upper boundaries and are usually associated with downtrends (pp. 4, 9).
  • Wedges have converging, slanted boundaries: the source calls falling wedges generally bullish and rising wedges generally bearish (p. 6).
  • Channels, flags, pennants, and symmetrical triangles are presented as continuation patterns, usually resolving with the existing trend; volume is generally expected to expand at the breakout (pp. 7-10).
  • Head and shoulders is described as an uptrend reversal completed by a neckline break, while the inverted form appears in downtrends with its own stated volume sequence (pp. 11-12).

Actionable rules

  1. Apply the source’s trend context: ascending triangles are framed in uptrends, descending triangles in downtrends, and head-and-shoulders patterns as reversals of the preceding trend (pp. 4, 9, 11-12).
  2. For the flat-base setup, draw its upper trendline and use the source’s stated trigger only when price breaks it as volume increases (p. 3).
  3. For triangle, wedge, channel, flag, pennant, and head-and-shoulders examples, compare actual volume behavior with the pattern-specific description before labeling the breakout (pp. 4, 6-12).

Caveats

The authorship and publication year are not identified in the available pages. Claims that “research has shown” certain patterns to be reliable have no citations or performance tables. The source offers no universal stop, invalidation distance, target projection, timeframe, or position-size rule, and it does not discuss failed breakouts. Its visual examples do not establish future expectancy.

Who it is for

Beginners learning classical chart-pattern vocabulary and the limits of visual signals.
