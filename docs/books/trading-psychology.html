Trading Psychology

Colin Nicholson · 2001 · Tier B — concise note article beginner

Explains why treating unrealized gains as expendable 'market money' can distort decisions and weaken risk discipline.

Summary

Nicholson presents trading as the combination of analysis, risk management and psychology. His main example concerns mental accounting: a trader protects money originally deposited but treats an unrealized gain as if it belongs to the market and can be lost casually. The article argues that this distinction can protect the trader’s ego while undermining decisions about the capital currently at stake (PDF pp. 1-2).

Key points

  • Better market analysis is only one part of the task; exposure and repeated decisions also affect outcomes (p. 1).
  • New information presents a sequence of decisions to buy, sell or do nothing, rather than a single prediction made once (p. 1).
  • Emotional framing can change the way identical price changes are perceived (p. 2).
  • An unrealized gain contributes to current marked-to-market wealth, even though it is not a realized cash profit (p. 2).
  • A purchase price can become an emotional reference point that obscures the present risk of a position (p. 2).
  • The article uses explanation and a teaching anecdote, not a controlled study establishing the size of a psychological effect.

Actionable rules

  1. In the author’s example, a share bought for $1 rises to $2 and is later sold at $1.80. The realized gain remains $0.80, but marked-to-market wealth has fallen $0.20 from the earlier $2 value. Keep both statements visible instead of denying the decline because the trade is still profitable (p. 2).
  2. Apply consistent care to current capital whether it came from a deposit or an open-position gain. Nicholson’s point is consistency of decision-making, not a rule to sell every profitable trade immediately (p. 2).
  3. Recognize when a decision is being defended by its effect on self-image rather than the market evidence. The article provides no numerical stop or exit threshold (pp. 1-2).

Caveats

Paper gains and realized gains differ in execution and taxation; the example addresses psychological framing, not their legal equivalence. It supplies no empirical performance test or complete strategy. The reproduction labels the article January 2001, while its embedded web address contains 2002; the exact publication date is uncertain. OCR recovered the previously garbled text.

Who it is for

Traders who notice that they tolerate a loss of open profit more casually than a loss below their purchase price.
