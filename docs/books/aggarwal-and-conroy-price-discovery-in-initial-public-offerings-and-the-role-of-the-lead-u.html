Price Discovery in Initial Public Offerings and the Role of the Lead Underwriter

Reena Aggarwal & Pat Conroy · 2000 · Tier B — concise note paper advanced

Journal of Finance study showing the lead underwriter's first preopening quote explains most of an IPO's initial return, even for the hottest deals.

Summary

Question: how does an IPO’s price move from the offer price (set the night before) to the first trade price, and what role does the lead underwriter play? Data: a proprietary SEC quote-by-quote dataset covering the five-minute Nasdaq preopening window (plus SDC New Issues data) for 188 Nasdaq IPOs, May–October 1997. Method: descriptive statistics and event-study analysis of quote revisions during the preopening, comparing offer-to-open, offer-to-close, and offer-to-first-quote returns. Finding: the lead underwriter’s very first preopening quote explains a large share of the IPO’s eventual initial return — for the hottest deal in the sample (a ~140% offer-to-open runup), the underwriter’s first quote alone captured ~105 points of it — and substantial further price discovery (up to ~190 quote revisions) occurs in the remaining seconds before trading opens.

Key points

  • Mean IPO offer price $12.33; mean offer-to-open return 17.66% (median 13.76%); mean offer-to-close return 19.47% — nearly all initial return is captured by the opening/first trade, not later in day 1.
  • On average 6.71 market makers enter quotes during the 5-minute preopening (max 14); most syndicate members never quote in the preopening at all.
  • Preopening quotes are non-binding, yet strongly predictive — the lead underwriter’s first quote alone explains a large share of eventual initial return, even for “hot” deals.
  • The lead underwriter enters a bid at the offer price for weak IPOs, effectively providing price support (consistent with known underwriter stabilization behavior).
  • 19 of 188 IPOs had no preopening quotes; 83 of 188 used 3+ minutes of the window (generally the ones with the largest price runups).
  • Nearly half of IPOs start trading after 11:00am rather than at the 9:30am open, with later start times associated with bigger opening pops.
  • Worked example: the Amazon.com IPO (May 15, 1997) — offer price $18, 116 quotes from 8 market makers during a 4:38 preopening window, first trade at $29.25.

Actionable rules

None given — this is empirical microstructure research on IPO mechanics, not a trading system. Takeaway for anyone trading IPO opens: the lead underwriter’s earliest preopening quote is highly informative about where a new issue will open, and quoting activity concentrated late in the preopening window (with a later trading-start time) signals a larger expected opening pop.

Caveats

Findings are specific to 1997-era Nasdaq market-maker mechanics (five-minute non-binding preopening window, human market makers) which have since been substantially changed by exchange rule changes and electronic opening auctions; the paper does not address direct-listing or auction-based IPO opening mechanisms used more commonly today.

Who it is for

Traders and researchers interested in the microstructure of new-issue openings and underwriter price-setting behavior, particularly anyone trying to trade around IPO opens.
