Summary

A 129-page book by Tom Williams introducing Volume Spread Analysis (VSA) — a Wyckoff-derived method of reading bar charts by combining volume (amount of activity) with price spread (range of the bar) to infer the actions of professional operators (market makers, specialists, large “smart money” players) rather than the crowd. The book covers market overview and how professionals move price on supply and demand, core VSA signals (no demand, no supply, testing, effort vs. result, buying/selling climaxes, shake-outs, up-thrusts), trend lines and trend clusters, the anatomy of bull/bear market campaigns, and practical trading hints, closing with a chapter on stock selection and a companion VSA software tool.

Key points

  • Volume Spread Analysis (VSA) — reading price spread and volume together to identify supply/demand imbalances caused by professional activity.
  • No Demand — an up bar on narrow spread, low volume: a weak rally likely to fail.
  • No Supply — a down bar on narrow spread, low volume: sellers have exited, higher prices likely.
  • Testing — a down move on low volume probes for remaining supply; passing on low volume implies none left.
  • Effort versus Result — high volume (“effort”) with a poor price outcome (“result”) signals absorption and a likely reversal.
  • Selling/Buying Climax — a very wide bar on very high volume that exhausts selling or buying pressure, often marking a low or high.
  • Shake-Out — a sharp, high-volume down move (often on bad news) that forces out weak longs before a genuine advance.
  • Up-thrust — a false new-high breakout on narrow spread, high volume: a rally failing on top.
  • Trend clusters and trend lines mark zones where support/resistance should be read alongside volume signals.
  • A “Trading Hints and Tips” chapter covers having a plan, planning for being wrong, and not relying on others’ opinions.

Actionable rules

  1. Treat an up bar with narrow spread and low volume as “No Demand” — exit or avoid new longs.
  2. Treat a down bar with narrow spread and low volume as “No Supply” — downside likely exhausted.
  3. Watch a Selling Climax (wide down bar, very high volume) as a potential buy zone; a Buying Climax as a potential short zone.
  4. After a Selling Climax, a low-volume test holding above the low confirms buying; a high-volume test warns of continued weakness.
  5. An Up-thrust (failed new high on narrow spread, high volume) is a sell/short signal, not a breakout to buy.
  6. Compare effort (volume) to result (spread/close) on every significant bar; a mismatch flags likely reversal.

Caveats

The book is built on Richard Wyckoff’s early-20th-century supply/demand framework and overlaps conceptually with other VSA/Wyckoff texts in this library. Thresholds for “high” or “low” volume are left to visual judgment rather than defined mathematically, limiting mechanical replication. No formal backtest statistics are given; the method is presented as a discretionary skill, and the book promotes companion VSA scanning software.

Who it is for

Discretionary chart readers who want a volume-and-price-spread framework for spotting professional accumulation/distribution and trend turning points, building on (or as an alternative entry point to) classic Wyckoff method material.
