The Right Stock at the Right Time — Dow Industrials/Utilities Seasonal Rotation System

Larry Williams · 2003 · Tier A — deep note system intermediate

A mechanical annual rotation between low-priced/high-yield Dow Industrial stocks and Dow Utility stocks timed to the October-to-April seasonal window, backtested at 24.1%/year since 1985.

What it is

Williams calls this the “Williams Way to Beat Wall Street” / “Larry Williams High-Yield Investment” (Chapter 11). It is a fully mechanical, twice-a-year portfolio rotation that combines two of the book’s core findings: (1) the Dow Jones Industrial Average has a strong seasonal tendency to rally from late October into spring and to stagnate or decline from spring into October, while (2) the Dow Jones Utility Average tends to do the opposite, rallying from around the first week of April into the first week of October because money seeking safety and yield rotates into utilities exactly when industrials are entering their weak season. The system holds industrial stocks during the strong industrial season and utility stocks during the strong utility season, always holding the lowest-priced, highest-current-yield names within whichever index is in season.

Rules

  1. Stock selection within each rotation. At each entry point, select the five lowest-priced, highest-current-dividend-yield stocks from the relevant Dow average (Industrials for the October entry, Utilities for the April entry). Optionally cross-check with a price-to-sales ratio below 1.0 as an additional value filter (Williams notes this ratio produced the widest spread between best and worst quintiles in his broader ratio backtest).
  2. October entry. On the last trading week of October, buy the five lowest-priced/highest-yield Dow Jones Industrial Average stocks.
  3. April exit/rotation. On the first trading week of April, sell the Industrial positions and buy the five lowest-priced/highest-yield Dow Jones Utility Average stocks with the proceeds.
  4. October exit/rotation. On the first trading week of October, sell the Utility positions and roll back into a freshly re-screened set of the five lowest-priced/highest-yield Dow Industrial stocks (repeat from step 2).
  5. Continuous repetition. The system runs every year without a market-timing filter beyond the calendar — it does not skip a cycle based on valuation, sentiment, or trend condition; the calendar rotation itself is the entire timing mechanism.
  6. Week-ending data discipline (general rule borrowed from Williams’ other systems, applicable here): use Thursday’s close for signal generation to avoid Friday/Monday volatility around the rotation dates; if a rotation signal would fall on a Thursday close, execute Friday morning; if it falls on Friday’s close, execute the following Monday morning.

Risk

Backtested results (Table 11.7, 1985–2001): the rotation system averaged 24.1%/year versus 13.8%/year for a Dow-30 buy-and-hold benchmark over the same period; a longer look-back (1976–2001) showed the Dow Industrials returning 8.3%/year buy-and-hold versus roughly 30%/year for the continuous rotation. Annual results in the 17-year table ranged from a loss of roughly −7.8% (1987, Utilities leg) to gains above 35% (2000, Industrials leg), so single-year variance is substantial even though the multi-year average is strong. The stated risk-reduction mechanism is structural, not a stop-loss: by design the system is out of Industrials (the more volatile, more rate-sensitive leg) during the historically weaker April–October stretch and holds Utilities instead, which Williams notes carry a higher yield and typically less downside volatility than industrials during market corrections — the Utilities leg is explicitly a “safer harbor,” not a return driver on its own. No stop-loss, position-sizing formula, or maximum-drawdown rule is specified for this system beyond the calendar rotation itself.

Caveats

The backtest period (1985–2001) includes a strong secular bull market for both Dow averages, and Williams does not show results for the Utilities leg during a rising-rate environment where utility stocks (which behave partly like bond proxies) would be expected to underperform; the system’s logic assumes falling or stable interest rates are the “reason” money flows into utilities each spring, so a persistently rising-rate regime would weaken or invalidate the utility half of the rotation. Trading costs, taxes, and dividend reinvestment mechanics are not detailed in the worked results. The exact “five lowest-priced/highest-yield” screen has no explicit tie-breaking or minimum-liquidity rule beyond the general book-wide filter of adequate trading volume, and stock composition of both Dow averages has changed materially since the original test period, so the specific names selected today will differ from those in the backtest sample.
