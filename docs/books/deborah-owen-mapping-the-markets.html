Overview

Mapping the Markets (Deborah Owen and Robin Griffiths, Bloomberg Press/The Economist, 2006) is a compact guide that connects macroeconomic cycle theory to practical stock selection. Part 1 builds the “tools for mapping the markets”: historical business-cycle research (Kondratieff, Juglar, Kitchin), the four-year U.S. electoral stockmarket cycle, sector rotation through a bull-market cycle, and a technical-analysis primer for individual stock selection (moving averages, RSI, support/resistance, Fibonacci, chart patterns, volume). Part 2 covers long-term secular growth drivers (emerging-market catch-up, demographics, energy, biotechnology); Part 3 covers downward-phase risks (global imbalances, external shocks); Part 4 applies the framework to specific stock markets and sectors circa the mid-2000s.

Core thesis

Stock markets move in nested, overlapping cycles — a roughly 50–60-year Kondratieff long wave, an approximately 9–11-year Juglar/business cycle, and a 4-year Kitchin cycle tied closely to the U.S. presidential election calendar — and investors who understand where the market sits within these cycles, combined with sector-rotation patterns and individual stock technical signals, can improve both asset allocation and stock-picking timing. The authors argue markets are not perfectly efficient/random (citing Mandelbrot’s leptokurtic, fat-tailed distribution of price changes rather than a normal bell curve) because human crowd psychology creates short-term momentum and recurring, tradable patterns.

Key concepts

  • Kondratieff wave — a ~50–60-year long economic cycle (Nikolai Kondratieff, 1920s) driven by clusters of technological innovation and capital-equipment replacement; the third wave (from 1896) accurately anticipated the 1930s slump.
  • Juglar cycle — a ~9–10-year business cycle identified independently by Jevons and Juglar in the 19th century, still visible in U.S. recession timing.
  • Kitchin cycle — a ~40-month (later associated with the 4-year U.S. electoral cycle) inventory/interest-rate-driven cycle.
  • Schumpeter’s “creative destruction” — capital and labor released from failing firms are redeployed into more productive, innovative sectors, powering the cycle’s upward phase.
  • Four-year electoral cycle — U.S. stockmarkets tend to rally for roughly 30 months and correct for roughly 18 months around the presidential term, skewed up or down by the prevailing secular trend.
  • Elliott wave — bull markets in three up-legs/two corrections, bear markets in a fall-rally-fall pattern, tied to the perception lag between actual and perceived trend.
  • Sector rotation — from interest-rate-sensitive financials at the cycle low, to consumer/retail stocks in early recovery, to speculative small caps at the “impulsive” peak, to property and laggard cyclicals late-cycle, to defensive sectors (tobacco, beverages, pharmaceuticals, utilities) after the peak.
  • Relative strength — a stock’s price performance against a benchmark index; relative strength often turns before the stock’s own price does.
  • Golden cross / dead cross — a shorter MA crossing above (golden) or below (dead) a longer MA while both are trending in that direction.
  • Leptokurtic distribution — Mandelbrot’s finding that real market returns are more peaked and fat-tailed than a normal distribution, evidence against pure random-walk efficiency.

Rules and setups

  1. Moving averages: 200-day MA defines the primary trend; 25-day or 50-day MA defines the secondary trend. Price typically tests a moving average with a frequency proportional to its length (25-day tested roughly monthly, 200-day roughly annually).
  2. Golden/dead cross: a shorter MA (e.g., 50- or 90-day) crossing above a rising longer MA (e.g., 200-day) = golden cross (bullish); crossing below a falling longer MA = dead cross (bearish).
  3. Overbought/oversold via MA deviation: fluctuations up to 20% above/below the 200-day MA are normal; most strong trends consolidate once deviation reaches roughly 30%.
  4. RSI (Wilder): 14-day period; readings of 70+ = overbought, 30 or below = oversold; treat as a guide, not a firm signal, since strong trends can stay overbought/oversold for extended periods. Divergence between price and RSI (new price high not confirmed by a new RSI high) flags slowing momentum.
  5. Quantum/Fibonacci retracements: common retracement levels are 25%, 33%/67%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, and 100% of the prior move; a break below half of an index’s all-time high suggests a secular downtrend.
  6. Support/resistance: a broken resistance level tends to become new support (and vice versa) because investors who missed the breakout buy the retest, while investors trapped at the old support level sell into any return rally.
  7. Chart patterns (with the standard measured-move target = height of the pattern projected from the breakout): head and shoulders / reverse head and shoulders, double and triple tops/bottoms, rounded bases/tops, symmetrical/ascending/descending triangles (breakout typically occurs 50–75% of the way to the apex; reliability drops past 75%), rectangles (need at least 3 touches of support and resistance to validate), and flags (should resolve within about 4 weeks or the pattern loses reliability).
  8. Volume confirmation: any breakout or pattern completion is stronger on rising volume; a new high on falling volume signals fading momentum.

Risk and money management

The book gives no explicit position-sizing or stop-loss percentage rules; its risk framework is cycle-level rather than trade-level. It frames technical analysis itself as a risk-management discipline because moving-average and trendline signals create “automatic triggers” to review a position if price fails to behave as expected — contrasted with fundamental “value” investing, where a falling price is reinterpreted as a better buying opportunity with no natural stop. The authors caution that overbought/oversold readings should be used to trim a portion of a position, not to exit entirely, and that reversal patterns should be treated as invalid if price recrosses back through the pattern’s neckline/boundary.

Psychology and discipline

Elliott wave psychology is explained through a perception-lag model: investors judge the trend from only the last two periods of price action, so their perceived trend lags the actual trend by about one period — explaining why they buy late in rallies and sell late in declines, and why bullish/bearish sentiment surveys move in a skewed wave rather than a symmetric sine wave (bull phases running roughly 30 months, bear phases roughly 18 months within the four-year cycle). At market tops, “irrational exuberance” produces phrases like “it’s different this time” and small speculative stocks attract disproportionate buying; at bottoms, capitulation selling accelerates into the final low as psychological denial gives way to forced selling.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Economic cycles — Kondratieff, Juglar, Kitchin waves and Schumpeter’s three-cycle synthesis.
  • Ch 2 — Stockmarket cycles — four-year U.S. electoral cycle, Elliott wave and its crowd-psychology basis.
  • Ch 3 — Sector rotation — which sectors lead at each stage of the economic/market cycle.
  • Ch 4 — Stock selection — the book’s technical-analysis primer (moving averages, RSI, relative strength, S/R, Fibonacci, chart patterns, volume).
  • Ch 5 — Economic catch-up — growth potential and risks in China, India, and other emerging economies.
  • Ch 6 — Demographic trends — population-driven growth differentials across the US, Europe, and Asia.
  • Ch 7 — Energy — long-term oil-price and alternative-energy drivers.
  • Ch 8 — Biotechnology — the genome-driven innovation wave as a Kondratieff-style growth driver.
  • Ch 9 — Global imbalances — current-account deficits and savings-rate risks.
  • Ch 10 — External events — shocks (e.g., terrorism, geopolitical) and how markets price them.
  • Ch 11 — Stockmarkets — cycle-based reads on the Dow, S&P 500, Nikkei, FTSE, Shanghai, and Bombay Sensex circa the mid-2000s.
  • Ch 12 — Sectors — long-term commodity (copper, aluminum) cycle case studies.
  • Conclusion — synthesis of the cyclical framework.

Strengths and caveats

The book’s chief value is connecting long-horizon macro cycle theory to a genuinely practical, well-illustrated technical primer — useful as an accessible on-ramp to both. Its regional market analysis (Ch. 11–12) and specific data (oil prices, demographic tables, index levels) are explicitly dated to the mid-2000s and should be read as historical illustration rather than current market commentary. Kondratieff- and Elliott-wave-based long-cycle theories remain contested among economists and technicians (the book itself notes some technicians can fit any move into an Elliott pattern “with the benefit of hindsight”), and the four-year electoral cycle is presented largely as a U.S.-specific, historically observed pattern rather than a causally proven mechanism. No backtested performance statistics are given for any of the technical rules; all examples are illustrative single-stock/single-index charts.

Who should read it

A good entry point for beginner-to-intermediate investors who want the standard technical-analysis toolkit (moving averages, RSI, Fibonacci, classic chart patterns) explained alongside the macro cycle context that shapes when those tools tend to work, and for readers interested in market history and cycle theory as a complement to (not a replacement for) trade-level technical systems.
