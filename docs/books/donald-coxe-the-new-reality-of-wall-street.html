Summary

Written by strategist Donald G. M. Coxe in 2003, in the aftermath of the Nasdaq crash, this book argues that major market manias and crashes follow a repeatable pattern he calls a “Triple Waterfall”: a cascading three-stage collapse, interrupted by two false-hope rallies, following a long build-up of collective over-belief. Coxe traces this pattern through seven historical episodes — the 1929 crash, the 1970s gold/silver/oil manias, Japan’s bubble, and the dotcom Nasdaq crash — then pivots to prescriptive portfolio construction, walking the reader through a diversified “Survival Pak” across ten asset classes as insurance against the next cycle.

Key points

  • Triple Waterfall — a six-stage cycle: Optimism (~2 yrs), Faith (~2 yrs), Fanaticism (1-2 yrs), Sudden Shock/First Cascade (6-12 mo), Last Chance/Second Cascade (2-10 mo), Long-Term Collapse/Third Cascade (12-20 yrs); the two intervening rallies lure investors back to “average down” before the final collapse.
  • Seven historical Triple Waterfalls identified: Nifty Fifty/1929-style crashes, 1970s-80s gold/silver/oil-stock manias, Japan’s bubble economy, and the 1997-2002 Nasdaq/telecom crash.
  • Two features separate a Triple Waterfall from an ordinary bear market: active promotion of the mania’s belief system by trusted institutions, and a “Shared Mistake” — a collectively held, ultimately false conviction (not simple fear/greed cycling).
  • “Di-worsification” (borrowed from Peter Lynch) — owning many overlapping equity funds across styles, which just recreates an index fund at a much higher fee.
  • Survival Pak — a portfolio diversified across 10 buckets: U.S. large/small-cap equities, U.S. REITs, foreign equities, emerging-market equities, U.S. short/medium and long tax-exempt bonds, U.S. short/medium and long taxable bonds, and gold (mining shares and/or bullion).
  • True diversification means always holding meaningful exposure to at least one asset class you currently dislike; expecting good returns from everything you own means you aren’t diversified.
  • Distinguishes durable “growth stocks” (Walgreens, Microsoft, Johnson & Johnson) from capital-spending cyclicals mistaken for growth stocks during the tech mania.
  • Momentum investing relies on the “Greater Fool Theory” and is self-reinforcing during a mania but fails once new buyers are exhausted; capital preservation, not high average returns, drives long-run outcomes.

Actionable rules

None given as tradable entry/exit rules — a macro/portfolio-construction book, not a trading system. Closest guidance: (1) build a portfolio spanning all ten Survival Pak buckets rather than concentrating in equities; (2) avoid “di-worsification” by consolidating overlapping equity-style funds; (3) treat the six-stage Triple Waterfall pattern, especially its two “relief rally” traps, as a warning against averaging down into a structurally broken mania.

Caveats

Written in 2003 just after the dotcom crash; specific market levels, rates, and named securities (Nasdaq levels, ~5% Treasury yields, the dollar-bear-market thesis) are dated to that period and read as historical case studies, not current guidance. The author discloses a conflict of interest — his firm sells analytics-based investment management, which he favors in the text. “Triple Waterfall” is Coxe’s own pattern-recognition thesis, not an academically validated model, and some statistics are cited without clear sourcing.

Who it is for

Intermediate investors and traders interested in market-cycle history and long-horizon, multi-asset-class portfolio construction rather than short-term trading technique.
