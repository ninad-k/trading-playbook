Gimmee Bars

Joe Ross · Tier B — concise note book intermediate

A Bollinger-band reversal setup for sideways markets uses a one-tick breakout trigger and exclusion filters.

Summary

Ross defines a Gimmee bar as a reversal bar that forms after price touches an outer Bollinger Band while the market is broadly consolidating (pp. 1–2). The example uses a 20-bar moving average of the close with bands at two standard deviations; the text says approximately 96.5% of closes fall within the bands under that setting (p. 1). At the upper band, a bar that closes below its open after a rise becomes a short candidate, with a sell order one tick below its low (p. 3). At the lower band, a bar that closes above its open after a fall becomes a long candidate, with a buy order one tick above its high (p. 4). The qualifying bar may be the touch bar or the immediately following bar. Ross advises standing aside when the Gimmee extreme is at or near the middle moving average, when the bar is unusually large relative to recent bars, or when the next bar gaps beyond the Gimmee range (p. 5).

Key points

  • The setup is explicitly for range conditions; a trending market changes the premise (pp. 1–2).
  • The one-tick trigger is a precise entry convention, while “near” the middle band is discretionary.
  • No stop, target, expectancy, or performance sample is supplied.

Actionable rules

  1. Use 20-bar, two-standard-deviation Bollinger Bands and accept the setup only in an overall trading range (pp. 1–2).
  2. After an upper-band touch, sell one tick below the low of a bearish reversal bar; after a lower-band touch, buy one tick above the high of a bullish reversal bar. The reversal may be the touch bar or the next bar (pp. 3–4).
  3. Skip the trade if the trigger extreme is near the middle average, the reversal bar is unusually large, or the following bar gaps beyond the Gimmee bar’s range (p. 5).

Caveats

The 96.5% containment statement follows a normal-distribution intuition and does not imply a reversal edge. The method can fail in breakouts, and the source provides no risk or cost model.

Who it is for

Short-term traders researching a simple mean-reversion hypothesis.
