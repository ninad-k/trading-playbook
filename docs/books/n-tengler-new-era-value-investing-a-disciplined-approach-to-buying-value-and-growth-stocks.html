Overview

Nancy Tengler, a professional large-cap value manager, argues that classic Graham-and-Dodd value investing — built around absolute dividend and P/E screens — became too narrow once the U.S. market shifted toward low- and non-dividend-paying growth sectors (technology, health care, communications) in the 1980s–1990s. Her firm’s response, developed with colleagues over two decades, is “Relative Value Discipline”: two valuation screens — Relative Dividend Yield (RDY) for dividend payers and Relative Price-to-Sales Ratio (RPSR) for low/no-dividend growth names — that measure cheapness relative to a stock’s own history and to the market, rather than in absolute terms. Every stock that screens as cheap must then clear a Twelve Fundamental Factors qualitative/quantitative checklist before purchase, and the whole process feeds a concentrated, rules-based portfolio-construction and stop-loss framework. The book is explicitly a professional manager’s playbook, illustrated throughout with real named-stock case studies (Exxon Mobil, Coca-Cola, Johnson & Johnson, Intel, Qwest, Lucent) rather than backtested statistics.

Core thesis

Dividends and, by extension, sales are reliable proxies for a corporate board’s own view of sustainable long-term earnings power, because boards rarely change payout or pricing policy casually. Comparing a stock’s current yield (or price-to-sales ratio) to its own trading history and to the market’s, rather than using an absolute cutoff, lets a value discipline stay applicable across changing market regimes and expand from traditional dividend payers into growth stocks that have temporarily fallen out of favor (“fallen angels”). Valuation cheapness alone is not sufficient — a rigorous fundamental checklist is required to screen out stocks that are cheap for good reason (“terminally cheap”).

Key concepts

  • Relative Dividend Yield (RDY) — a stock’s dividend yield divided by the market index’s dividend yield, tracked over time to flag over/undervaluation relative to the stock’s own history.
  • Relative Price-to-Sales Ratio (RPSR) — the equivalent metric for low/no-dividend stocks: a stock’s price-to-sales ratio divided by the S&P 500’s price-to-sales ratio.
  • Twelve Fundamental Factors — a 3-qualitative/9-quantitative checklist (Buggy Whip obsolescence risk, Franchise/Niche Value, Top Management and Board; Sales Growth, Operating Margins, Relative P/E, Positive Free Cash Flow, Dividend Coverage and Growth, Asset Turnover, Investment in Business/ROIC, Equity Leverage, Financial Risk) applied after a valuation screen signals a buy.
  • Terminally cheap stock — a stock that screens as statistically cheap on RDY or RPSR but fails enough fundamental factors that its cheapness reflects real, lasting impairment rather than a temporary sentiment gap.
  • Loss of constituency — the situation where a stock’s natural investor base (e.g., growth managers) abandons it before a new base (e.g., value managers) has discovered it, producing a valuation gap that can persist for quarters or years.
  • Fallen-angel growth stock — a former growth darling that has become statistically cheap enough to enter RDY or RPSR buy range while its underlying franchise remains intact (Coca-Cola and Johnson & Johnson are the book’s central examples).
  • Growth-at-a-reasonable-price (GARP) positioning — the resulting portfolio sits between pure value and pure growth indices because it holds both dividend-paying value names (via RDY) and undervalued growth names (via RPSR).
  • Covariance-aware sector weighting — deliberately pairing sectors with historically negative return correlation (e.g., technology vs. pharmaceuticals, technology vs. financials) to dampen portfolio volatility.
  • “It’s rarely different this time” — the book’s recurring behavioral warning against extrapolating current trends (dividend irrelevance in the late 1990s, “financial supermarket” bank models) indefinitely into the future.

Rules and setups

Relative Dividend Yield (dividend-paying stocks): 1. Yield = indicated annual dividend rate / current stock price; Market yield = index dividend rate / index value; RDY = stock yield / market yield. 2. Never buy a stock when its RDY is below 125% (an absolute floor stated in the text); the default sell trigger is when the stock’s yield falls back to the market’s yield (RDY near 100%). 3. Buy/sell threshold lines are otherwise set individually per stock from its own RDY history, and are moved only when the company’s fundamental profile changes materially (M&A, growth-rate shift, divestiture). 4. RDY is a long-term signal: the book notes it can take one to three years or more for a stock to travel from the buy range to the sell range.

Relative Price-to-Sales Ratio (low/no-dividend growth stocks): 5. PSR = stock price / sales per share; RPSR = stock’s PSR / S&P 500’s PSR. 6. Buy line = the mean RPSR over the trailing period (anchored at 12/31/1992 for data-quality reasons) minus one standard deviation; sell line = that mean plus one standard deviation. Recalculate monthly. 7. Do not apply RPSR to companies with no meaningful sales history (early-stage growth names) or to a merged entity whose combined pre-merger sales history is not representative of its new business mix.

Twelve Fundamental Factors gate (applied to any stock that clears RDY or RPSR): 8. Require passing scores on at least 2 of 3 qualitative factors (Buggy Whip/obsolescence, Franchise or Niche Value, Top Management and Board) and at least 5 of 9 quantitative factors (Sales/Revenue Growth, Operating Margins, Relative P/E, Positive Free Cash Flow, Dividend Coverage and Growth, Asset Turnover, Investment in Business/ROIC, Equity Leverage, Financial Risk). 9. For bank stocks, substitute Overhead/Efficiency, Liquidity/Funding Mix, Asset Quality, Asset-Liability Management, and Capital Adequacy for the standard quantitative factors that don’t apply to lenders.

Portfolio construction and exits (see the system sub-page below for full position-sizing and stop-loss mechanics): concentrate in roughly 25–35 names, initiate positions at up to 0.5% of the portfolio and build toward a 3–5% target weighting (6% hard cap), and apply two explicit stop-loss triggers on relative price decline.

Risk and money management

Position sizing is staged, not immediate: initial purchases are capped at about 0.5% of portfolio value, built up toward a 3–5% target weighting per name, with a hard ceiling of 6% of the portfolio for any single holding. No sector is allowed to exceed roughly twice the S&P 500’s weighting in that sector without a deliberate, conditions-based override. Two numeric stop-loss triggers, added after a costly experience (the Qwest merger investment), govern exits: Trigger 1 — a relative price decline of 10% in one day or 15% in two days automatically trims the position to half its pre-decline size, followed by a fundamental reassessment and a decision to exit fully or add at a newly identified “rock-bottom” level; Trigger 2 — a 20% relative decline over the trailing week, one, three, six, or twelve months triggers an immediate Twelve Fundamental Factors re-review, and an additional 5% decline from there triggers the same automatic half-size trim. The book explicitly identifies two stock types to avoid regardless of how cheap they screen: newly merged companies combining high-growth and slow-growth segments (their combined sales/earnings history is not statistically meaningful for RDY/RPSR) and companies with too short a public operating history (RPSR ranges are not statistically reliable without several years of data).

Psychology and discipline

The book’s central discipline argument is that a value manager’s greatest risk is abandoning a sound methodology exactly when it is most out of favor — “when you just can’t take it anymore, it’s time to double down your bet if your discipline is sound.” Named behavioral traps: extrapolating current trends indefinitely (dividends “no longer mattering” in the late 1990s, banks as permanent “financial supermarkets”); trusting sell-side analyst buy/sell calls, which the book shows (via Cisco, Oracle, and Intel charts) tend to peak in bullishness near price tops; and clinging to loved stocks past the point they remain good investments. The recommended discipline is mechanical and pre-committed: fixed valuation thresholds, a fixed fundamental checklist requiring a minimum passing score, and automatic (not discretionary) stop-loss trims triggered by specific percentage declines, precisely so that emotion cannot override the process at turning points.

  • Ch 2 — A Short History of Fundamental Analysis and the Dividend — Traces value investing from early 1900s insurance-stock analysis through Graham and Dodd’s 1934 “Security Analysis.”
  • Ch 3 — The Development of Relative Dividend Yield — Introduces RDY’s formula, origin, and buy/sell thresholds (with Coca-Cola and Johnson & Johnson examples).
  • Ch 4 — The Challenges of the 1990s — Explains why the growth-dominated 1990s market required a new tool; introduces RPSR’s formula and mechanics.
  • Ch 5 — The Twelve Fundamental Factors of RDY and RPSR Research — Full qualitative and quantitative checklist, including the bank-sector variant.
  • Ch 6 — RDY Case Studies — Sector-by-sector RDY examples (oil, pharma, fallen angels, consumer, banks) and RDY “failures.”
  • Ch 7 — RPSR Case Studies — Technology-bubble names and cases where RDY and RPSR signals intersect or diverge.
  • Ch 8 — Constructing a Value-Driven Portfolio — Concentration, position sizing, covariance-based sector pairing, and the two stop-loss triggers.
  • Ch 9 — What Is Value Investing Today? — Reconciles the discipline with competing growth/value index definitions.
  • Ch 10 — Seven Critical Lessons We Have Learned as Disciplined Investment Managers — Behavioral maxims (don’t extrapolate trends, it’s rarely different this time, go with your discipline at turning points, etc.).
  • Appendices A–D — Composite disclosure and three fully worked Twelve Fundamental Factors valuations (Estée Lauder, EMC, Walt Disney).

Strengths and caveats

The RDY and RPSR mechanics, the Twelve Fundamental Factors checklist, and the position-sizing/stop-loss rules are stated with unusual precision for an investing book and are genuinely codeable. However, all buy/sell thresholds and case studies are calibrated to data through 2002 and to that era’s market structure (dot-com bust, post-Enron accounting scrutiny); the RPSR anchor date of 12/31/1992 and its standard-deviation bands would need to be recalculated with current data before use. The book relies on Compustat-sourced institutional data that individual investors may not have easy access to. Several conclusions are drawn from a small number of the author’s own named holdings (Qwest, Lucent, Reader’s Digest) rather than a systematic backtest, so the stated success rate of the discipline is anecdotal rather than statistically validated. The framework also assumes continued availability of a “dividend-paying culture” and reliable sales reporting; the book itself flags that both dividend policy and sales figures can be distorted by managements (e.g., Enron-style off-balance-sheet financing, telecom capacity swaps).

Who should read it

Fundamentals-driven equity investors and analysts who want a systematic, rules-based value-investing framework that extends beyond dividend payers into growth stocks, and who want explicit position-sizing and stop-loss rules rather than only qualitative valuation philosophy. Less useful for traders looking for short-horizon setups (RDY and RPSR are explicitly multi-year, buy-and-hold signals) or for readers without access to historical price-to-sales and dividend-yield data series.
