Overview

Written just after the 2000–2002 tech-stock collapse, this book is a set of first-person profiles of eleven professional money managers — growth, value, and sector-fund specialists — describing how they actually pick stocks. Financial journalist Daniel Strachman frames the book as a corrective to the late-1990s belief that anyone could day-trade their way to riches: instead of teaching the reader a personal stock-picking system, it argues that consistent, disciplined managers with clearly articulated, repeatable criteria are the real “what works on Wall Street,” and shows what several very different disciplined approaches look like in practice. A short opening chapter covers stock-market basics and history; a closing “Resources” chapter lists financial websites and publications, and is dated and non-substantive today.

Core thesis

Following a strict, well-defined, and consistently applied investment discipline over long periods matters more than intelligence, prediction, or chasing the current market fad; the specific discipline (growth, value, or a sector focus) matters less than the manager’s consistency in applying it and their willingness to say “no” to companies that don’t meet their own numeric criteria. Strachman explicitly argues that most individual investors are better served hiring a manager who has demonstrated this discipline than picking their own stocks.

Key concepts

  • Return on equity (ROE) floor — several profiled value managers (e.g., Leon Black of Delphi Management) require a minimum ROE (Black’s floor: 15%, average holding 17–18%) before a company is even considered.
  • P/E ceiling / cutoff multiple — a hard maximum price-to-earnings multiple a manager will pay regardless of the company’s quality (Black’s cutoff: 12.9× his own forward-earnings estimate).
  • Margin of safety — Graham’s concept, used explicitly by Black and by Tweedy, Browne: buying at a conservative discount to a calculated intrinsic or breakup value so that being wrong about the business still limits the loss.
  • Intrinsic value vs. market price — Tweedy, Browne’s two-tier framework (book value, net asset value, earnings value, private market value, versus the quoted price); they target stocks at 50–60% of estimated intrinsic value.
  • Free cash flow analysis — adding back depreciation/amortization/deferred taxes to net income and subtracting capital and working-capital needs, used by value managers to identify companies that can self-fund through downturns.
  • Position and industry diversification caps — Tweedy, Browne caps any single position at 3–4% of portfolio assets and any single industry at 15–20%.
  • Bottom-up vs. top-down sector screening — some managers (e.g., health-care specialist Sam Isaly) screen an entire sector for candidates rather than scanning the whole market.
  • Qualitative management assessment — several managers require direct meetings with company management, checking loss-reserve tables (insurers), accounting conservatism, and executive compensation structure, before finalizing a purchase.
  • Growth-at-a-reasonable-price (GARP) blending — the recurring view, stated by multiple managers, that “value” and “growth” are not opposites: the goal is a growing business bought at a below-average multiple.

Rules and setups

The book does not present one unified system; each profiled manager states their own numeric criteria in their own words. Representative, concrete examples given in the text:

  1. Leon Black (Delphi Management, value) — minimum 15% return on book value (portfolio average 17–18%); 3–5 year revenue/profit growth faster than inflation (portfolio average 11–13%); pay no more than 12.9× the firm’s own forward-earnings estimate, regardless of the company; prefer companies with no debt or more cash than debt (40%+ of the portfolio); avoid companies practicing “financial engineering” (meeting per-share estimates via buybacks/accounting rather than real growth); portfolio of 60–65 names, concentrated by industry (historically ~20% media).
  2. Chris Browne (Tweedy, Browne, global value) — buy at 50–60% of calculated intrinsic value (book value, net asset value, earnings power, or private-market/takeover value); no position larger than 3–4% of assets; no industry larger than 15–20% of assets; hedge foreign-currency exposure back to the dollar; portfolios of roughly 100 (domestic) to 190 (global) names.
  3. General screening-first process (several managers) — run quantitative screens (ROE, P/E, price-to-book, debt levels, growth rate) across a database of thousands of names to build a short list, then perform qualitative research (management calls, 10-K/10-Q review, site visits for manufacturers) before buying.
  4. Author’s own target — Strachman states a personal goal of 8–15% annual return net of fees (average ~11.5%) as the realistic, sustainable benchmark against which to judge any manager’s performance, rather than chasing 20–30%+ annual returns.

There are no entry/exit price triggers, stop-loss rules, or position-sizing formulas given for an individual retail trader to execute mechanically — the “rules” are each manager’s company-selection screens, applied by that manager’s own team over a multi-week research process.

Risk and money management

Risk management in this book means fundamental risk control (diversification by position and industry, avoiding overleveraged or opaque companies, requiring a margin of safety before purchase), not trade-level stop-losses or position sizing. Tweedy, Browne’s per-position and per-industry caps (3–4% / 15–20%) are the only explicit portfolio-construction limits given. No manager profiled describes using price stops; the operative risk control is not overpaying in the first place (Black’s fixed P/E ceiling; Browne’s discount-to-intrinsic-value requirement).

Psychology and discipline

The recurring psychological theme across managers is willingness to be unfashionable: buying “flawed merchandise” and unglamorous, low-profile companies (Black explicitly contrasts this with the excitement of chasing IPOs), sticking to a numeric cutoff even for a company everyone loves (Black: “I don’t care what the company is; if it does not sell just below the 13-multiple cusp, we are not interested”), and tolerating long stretches of underperformance versus hot sectors. The author’s own psychological framing for readers is humility: accepting that professional managers have an informational and analytical edge that individual investors following news and tips do not.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — Investing 101 — what a stock is, a brief history of the NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq, basic market mechanics.
  • Ch 2 — Growth and Value Managers — the book’s core: individual profiles including Leon Black (Delphi Management), Chris Browne (Tweedy, Browne), and several other growth/value managers, each covering firm history, philosophy, and specific numeric criteria.
  • Ch 3 — Sector Fund Managers — profiles of specialist managers, including health-care manager Sam Isaly, and other single-sector fund managers.
  • Ch 4 — Resources — a dated list of financial websites (Yahoo! Finance, Bloomberg.com, TheStreet.com, Morningstar.com) and print publications (Investor’s Business Daily, Financial Times, Barron’s).
  • Ch 5 — Conclusion — restates the book’s core argument that discipline and consistency, not prediction, separate successful managers from the rest.

Strengths and caveats

The manager-specific numeric criteria (ROE floors, P/E ceilings, discount-to-intrinsic-value targets, diversification caps) are concrete and give a genuine sense of how real institutional value/growth screening works, which is the book’s real strength. However, it is fundamentally a journalistic profile collection, not a trading or investing manual: there is no unified, testable system, no price-action or timing rules, and no guidance translatable into a retail trader’s day-to-day execution. The Resources chapter (websites, print media commentary) is entirely dated. Several anecdotes and firm asset sizes are specific to 2001–2002 and some profiled funds, firms, or personnel may no longer exist in the same form. The book’s implicit conclusion — that most readers should hire a manager rather than pick stocks themselves — sits in tension with its own title.

Who should read it

Beginner-to-intermediate investors who want to understand how professional fundamental managers actually think about stock selection (screening criteria, valuation discipline, qualitative management assessment) before either evaluating a fund manager or building their own long-term equity screens. Not useful for traders seeking entry/exit rules, technical setups, or a testable trading system.

Related books in this library

  • The Intelligent Investor — the margin-of-safety and intrinsic-value framework that Tweedy, Browne and other profiled value managers explicitly trace back to Graham.
  • The Warren Buffett Way — a fuller treatment of the Buffett/Graham value lineage referenced repeatedly in the Tweedy, Browne profile.
  • Stock Market Wizards — a comparable interview-profile format applied to traders rather than long-term stock pickers.
  • Pick Stocks Like Warren Buffett — a more systematized, retail-oriented distillation of the value-investing criteria discussed here.
  • New Era Value Investing: A Disciplined Approach to Buying Value and Growth Stocks — covers the same growth-vs-value blending theme with a more structured, rules-based approach.

Related in this library

  • The Intelligent Investor — Graham's framework for value investing: treat market quotes as a manic partner (Mr. Market), demand a margin of safety, and pick a defensive or enterprising posture with explicit numeric screens.
  • The Warren Buffett Way — Distills Warren Buffett's investment decisions into twelve tenets across business, management, financial, and value criteria, plus his focus-portfolio approach.
  • Stock Market Wizards — Fourteen interviews with top U.S. stock/derivatives traders plus a trading psychiatrist, distilled into 65 cross-cutting 'Wizard Lessons' on edge, discipline, and risk control rather than one method.
  • Pick Stocks Like Warren Buffett — Distills Buffett's value-investing principles and profiles a dozen like-minded fund managers who apply the same approach.
  • New Era Value Investing: A Disciplined Approach to Buying Value and Growth Stocks — Extends Graham-and-Dodd value investing to non-dividend growth stocks via two relative-valuation screens: Relative Dividend Yield and Relative Price-to-Sales.
