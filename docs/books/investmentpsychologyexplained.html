Overview

Pring’s central claim is that beating the market is secondary to “beating ourselves” — mastering fear, greed, pride, and hope well enough to execute a chosen methodology consistently. The book has three parts: Part I (Knowing Yourself) diagnoses the psychological traps that destroy traders — chasing gurus, pride of opinion, impatience, abandoning a system mid-drawdown; Part II (The Wall Street Herd) applies contrary-opinion theory to crowd behavior, news reactions, and broker relationships; Part III (Staying One Step Ahead) turns diagnosis into practice with 19 numbered trading rules, a written-plan framework, and a curated anthology of classic rules from historical traders (Baruch, Wyckoff, Gann, Sperandeo, and others).

Core thesis

There is no “Holy Grail” — no guru, indicator, or back-tested system that works reliably at all times, because any edge that becomes popular gets arbitraged away once the crowd discovers it, and because market conditions change underneath a fixed system. Sustainable profit comes not from finding a perfect method but from selecting a reasonable, tested approach and supplying the psychological discipline — objectivity, patience, humility, rule-following — that most traders lack. Pring argues 90% of new futures traders are wiped out in their first year not because good methods don’t exist, but because pride of opinion, impatience, and emotional decisions cause traders to abandon sound rules at exactly the wrong moment.

Key concepts

  • Holy Grail myth — no advisor, indicator, or system delivers consistent perfect timing; advisory-sentiment and moving-average systems both fail at specific historical turning points despite long-run edges.
  • Pride of opinion — stubbornness and inability to admit a mistake; illustrated by a case study (“Jack and Bill”) of a joint trading account destroyed when one partner refused to cut losses out of ego.
  • Contrary Opinion (Humphrey Neill) — the crowd is right most of the time but wrong at turning points; “when everyone thinks alike, everyone is likely to be wrong.” Going contrary is an art, not a mechanical rule, requiring genuine independent reasoning.
  • Guru cycle — market forecasters (Edson Gould, Joe Granville) gain fame after a few correct calls, become overconfident, and are eventually discredited when conditions shift underneath their fixed approach.
  • Staying the course — abandoning a tested methodology mid-drawdown (shown via a discount-rate timing system and a 25-day moving-average crossover) destroys most of the edge; the worst losses come from quitting right before the system’s next big winning signal.
  • “This time it’s different” — the rationalization crowds use to justify abandoning discipline at manias (1920s stocks, 1970s “Nifty Fifty”); Pring treats it as a reliable warning sign, not a legitimate exception.
  • Risk/reward ratio discipline — size and enter a trade only with a predetermined, favorable ratio of acceptable loss to expected gain (Pring’s rule of thumb: at least 3-to-1).
  • Written trading/investment plan — a four-part framework (set objectives, adopt a philosophy matched to temperament, build a plan to maximize objectivity, establish a review process) drawn partly from Van Tharp’s “ten trading tasks.”
  • Peak performer traits (Charles Garfield) — commitment to a mission, results in real time, self-mastery, team building, course correction, change management — common to top traders and top performers generally.

Rules and setups

Pring’s own numbered rules (Chapter 12, “Nineteen Trading Rules for Greater Profits”), split into psychological management and money management:

Psychological management: (1) When in doubt, stay out. (2) Never trade or invest based on hope. (3) Act on your own judgment or else entirely on another’s — never a mix. (4) Buy low into weakness, sell high into strength (i.e., act opposite to rising/falling confidence). (5) Don’t overtrade. (6) After a profitable campaign, take a trading vacation (6–8 weeks). (7) Take a periodic mental inventory. (8) Constantly analyze your mistakes. (9) Don’t jump the gun ahead of your system’s signal. (10) Don’t try to call every market turn.

Money management: (11) Never enter a position without first establishing a risk/reward ratio — Pring’s rule of thumb is at least 3-to-1. (12) Cut losses, let profits run. (13) Place numerous small bets on low-risk ideas — as a general rule, risk no more than 5% of available capital on any one trade. (14) Look down, not up: assess the worst case before the best case. (15) Never trade or invest more than you can reasonably afford to lose. (16) Don’t fight the trend. (17) Wherever possible, trade liquid markets (a rule of thumb: under ~2,000 contracts/day average volume is illiquid). (18) Never meet a margin call by sending more money — treat it as a signal to reduce exposure. (19) If you place a stop, put it at a logical (technical) point, not a convenient dollar-loss point.

Pring adds the self-deprecating “Rule 20/21”: follow the other 19 without question.

A fully specified example system appears in Chapter 6 (originally from Pring’s Technical Analysis Explained) trading British pound futures — see the linked system page for exact entry/exit rules.

Risk and money management

Beyond Rules 11–19 above, Pring frames money management as the discipline that keeps a trader in the game long enough for a positive-expectancy method to pay off: cap risk per trade (≤5% of capital, 3:1 minimum reward:risk), never add to a losing position or meet a margin call with fresh funds, and treat a margin call driven by a string of losses as a signal to stop trading entirely for an extended period (at least three weeks) rather than re-fund the account. He separates two margin-call causes — an adverse market move versus a slow account bleed — and argues the latter calls for a longer break. Leverage magnifies psychological as well as financial risk: futures traders are warned that small price moves can wipe out equity, so risk tolerance must be assessed before, not during, a fast market.

Psychology and discipline

This is effectively the whole book’s subject. Pring frames psychological failure as the primary cause of trading losses, ahead of any flaw in analytical method. Key patterns he documents: traders are risk-averse about taking profits (cashing out too early) but risk-seeking about losses (holding too long, hoping for a rebound) — the reverse of Rule 12. Pride of opinion causes traders to hold losing positions to avoid admitting error. The “greener pastures effect” causes investors to chase recently-top-performing funds just as they mean-revert. Overconfidence after a winning streak leads to abandoning risk discipline exactly when a reversal is most costly. Van Tharp’s research found losing traders share traits of high stress, pessimism, externalizing blame, crowd-following, and a failure to follow rules — while winners show risk control, patience, and freedom from internal conflict about money itself.

Chapter map

  • Ch 1 — There Is No Holy Grail — no guru, indicator, or system provides consistent perfect market timing.
  • Ch 2 — How to Be Objective — maintaining flexibility of opinion against confirmation bias.
  • Ch 3 — Independent Thinking — the guru cycle (Gould, Granville) and dangers of outsourcing judgment.
  • Ch 4 — Pride Goes Before a Loss — case study of an account destroyed by refusal to admit error.
  • Ch 5 — Patience Is a Profitable Virtue — waiting for genuine low-risk setups rather than forcing trades.
  • Ch 6 — Staying the Course — why abandoning a tested system mid-drawdown destroys its edge; includes the Pound Sterling system.
  • Ch 7 — A New Look at Contrary Opinion — Humphrey Neill’s theory: the crowd is right except at turning points.
  • Ch 8 — When to Go Contrary — practical guidance on timing contrarian positions.
  • Ch 9 — How to Profit from Newsbreaks — avoiding emotional reaction to news-driven volatility.
  • Ch 10 — Dealing with Brokers and Money Managers the Smart Way — keeping independent judgment in advisory relationships.
  • Ch 11 — What Makes a Great Trader or Investor? — traits of top performers (Tharp, Garfield, Train research).
  • Ch 12 — Nineteen Trading Rules for Greater Profits — Pring’s own numbered psychological and money-management rules.
  • Ch 13 — Making a Plan and Sticking to It — a written-plan framework including Van Tharp’s ten trading tasks.
  • Ch 14 — Classic Trading Rules — curated rule lists from Baruch, Nelson, Wyckoff, Gann, Frank Williams, Wolf, Hoyne, Sperandeo.

Strengths and caveats

  • Concrete, numbered, and specific rules (Ch. 12) are unusually actionable for a psychology-focused book, avoiding vague platitudes.
  • The Pound Sterling and discount-rate example systems use 1970s–1990s data and pre-decimalization/pre-electronic-market conditions; Pring himself notes some indicators (e.g., short-interest ratio) were later distorted by structural changes like program trading.
  • Chapter 14 is explicitly a compilation of other authors’ historical rule lists (some dating to 1903–1930), included for their enduring relevance rather than as Pring’s own original contribution — treat attribution accordingly.
  • The book’s illustrative “systems” (moving-average crossover, discount-rate signal, Pound Sterling MA/ROC system) are used to teach discipline, not promoted as current, optimized trading strategies; Pring flags the moving-average crossover explicitly as “not a recommended system.”
  • Anecdotal case studies (Jack and Bill, various guru profiles) are illustrative rather than statistically representative.

Who should read it

Traders and investors who already have (or are choosing) an analytical method but struggle to execute it consistently — the book targets the discipline and behavioral side of trading rather than teaching new technical or fundamental analysis.
