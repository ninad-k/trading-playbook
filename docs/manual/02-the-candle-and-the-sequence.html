The Candle and the Sequence

Read the anatomy of a single candle for what it says about the fight between buyers and sellers, then read three candles in a row as a sequence — without reaching for a memorised catalogue of pattern names.

123456789

What you will be able to do

Look at one candle and state, in your own words, who was in control by the close and how convincingly. Look at a run of two or three candles and describe what happened between them as a small story rather than by naming a pattern. Explain why a candle’s location matters more than its shape, and why you should be suspicious of any book — including some of the material this course draws on — that hands you forty named shapes to memorise.

The lesson

The anatomy, properly understood

You already know, from the last stage, that a candle packs four prices — open, high, low, close — into one shape. Now look at what that shape is actually built from.

The thick part of the candle is the body, running from the open to the close. Its color tells you the outcome: if the close is above the open, buyers pushed the price up over the period and the body is usually drawn in a lighter or green color; if the close is below the open, sellers won and it’s usually darker or red. The size of the body tells you the margin of victory. A long body means one side dominated from start to finish with barely any pushback. A tiny body — open and close almost identical — means the period ended close to a draw, whatever happened in between.

The thin lines extending above and below the body are wicks, sometimes called shadows or tails. They mark the high and low — the furthest the price traveled during the period before it was pulled back. This is the part beginners most often misread, so be precise about it: a wick is not a small, less important version of the body. A wick is a record of failure. Price went there, and then the market rejected it and pulled back the other way before the period closed. A long upper wick means buyers pushed price higher, and lost that ground — sellers took it back. A long lower wick means sellers pushed it lower, and lost that ground — buyers took it back. The longer the wick relative to the body, the more decisive that rejection was.

Put the two together and a candle is answering three questions every time: who closed in control (the body’s color), how convincingly (the body’s size), and did anyone get pushed back and rejected along the way (the wicks, and which side they’re on). Train yourself to ask those three questions of every candle you look at, out loud if it helps, before you ask anything else.

The handful of shapes worth actually knowing

There is a small set of single- and multi-candle shapes that recur often enough, and describe a clear enough situation, that it’s worth being able to name them. The reason to learn these few is not that the name matters — it’s that each one is a shorthand for a specific answer to the three questions above, and having the shorthand saves you re-deriving it every time.

A doji is a candle where the open and close are essentially equal, so the body all but disappears and you’re left looking mostly at wicks. Read literally: neither side won. It represents indecision or a genuine standoff between buyers and sellers over that period, nothing more mystical than that.

A hammer is a small body sitting near the top of the period’s range, with a long lower wick and little or no upper wick. Read it as: sellers pushed price down hard during the period, and buyers rejected that entire move and dragged the close back up near where it opened. The identical shape appearing near the top of an advance, after a run higher rather than after a decline, is usually called a hanging man instead — same shape, opposite context, which is exactly the point this lesson is building toward.

An engulfing candle is a two-candle shape where the second candle’s body completely swallows the first one’s body, closing beyond where the first one opened, in the opposite direction. A bearish engulfing candle, for instance, opens above the prior candle’s close and then closes below the prior candle’s open — a full reversal of the previous period’s outcome inside one period. Read it as: whoever was in control a moment ago has just been decisively overrun.

The star family — morning star and evening star — are three-candle shapes: a strong candle in the prevailing direction, then a small-bodied candle that gaps or drifts away showing indecision, then a strong candle back in the opposite direction that erases most of the first candle’s move. Read it as a three-act version of the same story a single reversal candle tells in one act: control, hesitation, control changing hands.

That is close to the complete list worth memorizing by name. Notice what all of them have in common: each name is just a label for a specific pattern of who-won-and-by-how-much across one to three candles. You have already been building the ability to derive that reading from the anatomy directly. The names are a convenience, not a separate skill.

Why the catalogue approach is a trap

If you pick up almost any beginner trading book — several of the source books behind this course among them — you will find a candlestick chapter listing thirty, forty, sometimes more named patterns: three white soldiers, dark cloud cover, piercing line, abandoned baby, tweezer top, tweezer bottom, and on. Each gets a picture, a name, and a one-line verdict: bullish or bearish. It looks like a complete alphabet you can learn once and then apply forever.

Be blunt with yourself about two problems with that approach. First, it is genuinely the wrong way to learn this, even setting aside whether the patterns work. Memorizing forty shapes trains you to pattern-match against a mental flashcard deck — you see a candle, flip through the deck, find the closest match, apply its verdict. That is slow, it is error-prone the moment a real candle doesn’t quite match the textbook picture, and worse, it trains you to look at a candle in isolation and ask “what is this called” instead of asking “what does this tell me about who’s in control, here, right now.” You already have the second skill from the anatomy section above. The catalogue approach actively gets in its way by giving you a shortcut that skips the reasoning.

Second, and this needs to be said plainly rather than hedged: the evidence that most of these individually named patterns carry a standalone statistical edge is weak. Testing across markets and time periods generally does not find that spotting a hammer or an evening star, on its own, reliably predicts what happens next with any consistency worth trading on. Some of that is because the patterns are common enough to appear constantly in both directions; some is because a pattern’s usefulness — to the extent it has any — depends enormously on context that the name itself doesn’t capture. This doesn’t mean the shapes are meaningless. It means the name is not where the value is.

Where the value actually is: location, not shape

Here is the central point of this lesson, and it’s worth being direct about it: a candle means almost nothing by itself. The same hammer shape that looks like a meaningful bullish signal after a long, tired decline into a level that has held before is unremarkable — arguably noise — in the middle of a range with nothing else going on. The candle did not change. Its context did.

So instead of asking “what is this candle called,” build the habit of asking a different, better question: where did this happen, and what had to be true just before it for this to matter? A rejection candle at a price level nothing else has touched recently tells you very little. The identical candle at a level where price has reversed twice before, after a stretched multi-bar move in one direction, arriving on a spike in the range or volume of the bar itself — that is worth your attention, and it would be worth your attention with or without a name attached to the shape. The named shape is, at most, a flag that draws your eye to the right spot. The location and the run of bars leading into it is where the actual information lives.

This is also why reading a short sequence beats reading one candle. Three bars in a row is a small story: bar one shows sellers in firm control, bar two shows a stall — small body, long lower wick, indecision — and bar three shows a strong push back up that erases bar one’s ground. You do not need a name for that sequence to read it correctly. You need to ask, bar by bar: who was in control, how convincingly, and did anyone get rejected. Do that across three or four bars and you are reading the actual tug-of-war instead of pattern-matching a picture.

Worked example

A stock has been declining steadily for eight sessions, each day closing lower than the last, with mostly solid red bodies and small wicks — sellers clearly and consistently in control. On day nine, the stock opens near the prior close, sells off early to a new low for the move, and then buyers step in hard: the price is dragged back up and closes near the top of the day’s range, well above the open. The body is small, the lower wick is long.

Read it with the three questions. Who closed in control? Barely buyers — the close is above the open, though not by much. How convincing was it? The body itself is unremarkable, small. Did anyone get rejected? Yes, decisively — sellers pushed to a fresh low and then lost all of that ground within the same session, leaving a long lower wick.

Now add location, which is the whole point of this lesson. This candle arrives after eight straight days of sellers winning, at a fresh low for the move — exactly the kind of place where a rejection is worth noticing, because it suggests the selling pressure that has driven eight straight closes lower just failed to extend itself for the first time. If this same small-bodied, long-lower-wick candle had appeared on day three of a quiet sideways drift with no prior selling pressure to reject, it would tell you almost nothing — a minor, forgettable stall.

Note what this worked example does not do: it does not conclude the stock will now rally, and it does not name the candle. Naming it a hammer changes nothing about the reasoning above and adds a claim to a future outcome that the candle itself cannot support. What you can say honestly is narrower and more useful: control that had been one-sided for eight sessions was contested for the first time, at a level worth watching, in a candle worth remembering as you look at what comes next.

Practice

### Drill: Three-candle stories Pull up twenty recent candles on any chart. Working left to right, mark ten separate three-candle sequences (they can overlap). For each one, write a single sentence describing what happened using only the language of control, conviction, and rejection — no pattern names allowed. For example: "seller in control, contested hard on the second bar with a long lower wick, then buyer took over decisively on the third." Only after you've written all ten sentences, go back and note next to any of them whether it also happens to match a named pattern from this lesson. You should find the sentence was doing all the real work before you ever reached for the name.

Self-check

  1. What does a long wick actually tell you, and why is it a mistake to think of it as a minor detail compared to the body?
  2. Why does the same candle shape get called a hammer in one location and a hanging man in another, and what does that tell you about where the meaning of a candle actually comes from?
  3. What is wrong with learning candlesticks by memorizing a catalogue of thirty or forty named patterns, beyond the fact that it’s a lot to memorize?
  4. When you see a candle you don’t recognize as any named pattern, what three questions can you still ask to read it correctly?
