## Summary

An educational seminar handout on the fundamental (macro) drivers of currency prices, aimed at beginners. It classifies U.S. economic indicators into leading, coincident, and lagging categories, explains how each relates to the four phases of the business cycle, gives a condensed history of foreign exchange from the gold standard through Bretton Woods to the modern floating-rate era, and closes with a short FX glossary.

## Key points

- Leading indicators (anticipate future direction): average weekly manufacturing hours, initial unemployment claims, building permits, S&P 500, consumer expectations index, new orders for consumer/capital goods, vendor delivery speed, M2 money supply, and the 10-year Treasury/fed funds interest rate spread (yield curve).
- Coincident indicators (reflect current conditions): nonfarm payrolls, personal income less transfer payments, industrial production index, manufacturing and trade sales.
- Lagging indicators (confirm turns after the fact): average duration of unemployment, prime rate, inventory-to-sales ratio, consumer credit-to-income ratio, unit labor cost change, commercial/industrial loans outstanding, services CPI.
- Key market-moving releases highlighted: CPI, durable goods orders, employment cost index, GDP, industrial production, jobless claims, motor vehicle sales, personal income/outlays, PPI, trade balance — each explained with its typical short-term currency effect (e.g., rising CPI/PPI tends to be currency-supportive short term via rate-hike expectations, but undermines confidence if inflation persists).
- Historical arc: barter to metal coinage, gold-backed paper currency, the 1944 Bretton Woods fixed-rate system (USD pegged at $35/oz, other currencies pegged to USD), its 1971 collapse after Nixon suspended gold convertibility, the 1979 European Monetary System, and the 1997 Asian currency crisis.
- Estimated 1997-era daily FX turnover: over $3.2 trillion (later text also cites daily interbank volumes in the same range), dwarfing global stock and bond markets combined.
- Market participants: commercial/interbank banks (facilitating client flow and proprietary speculation, reportedly up to 70% of some banks' currency revenue), corporate/commercial hedgers, and central banks (intervening to stabilize their own currency).
- Interest rate differentials, trade flows, and political events are named as the main fundamental drivers of exchange-rate trends.
- Glossary terms defined: quote, pip, speculate, spot, stop (buy stop / sell stop / stop-loss), and historical vs. implied volatility.

## Actionable rules

None given as tradable entry/exit rules — this is a fundamentals/context primer, not a system. Practical takeaway: track the listed leading indicators (especially the yield curve, consumer expectations, and new orders) for early clues on economic turning points, and treat CPI/PPI/GDP/NFP releases as the highest-impact scheduled events for a given currency.

## Caveats

Undated seminar handout with no named author; internal text references "this year (2005)" and 1997-era daily volume figures, so it reflects mid-2000s and earlier market conditions — current FX daily turnover is many times larger. No specific trading strategy, entry rule, or risk parameter is given; it is pure background/reference material.

## Who it is for

Beginners who want a compact reference for what economic releases move currencies and why, before layering in a technical trading method.
