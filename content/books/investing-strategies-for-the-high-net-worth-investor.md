## Summary

Written by wealth advisor Niall J. Gannon (The Gannon Group), the book's thesis grew out of a 2008 CFA Institute speech arguing that mainstream investment advice — that stocks beat bonds over the long run, that index funds beat active management, and that alternative investments provide reliable diversification — is often wrong once an investor is in the top tax bracket and paying taxes on dividends, interest, and realized gains every year. The book walks through after-tax return studies (S&P 500 vs. municipal and taxable bonds, 1957–2009, at varying portfolio turnover rates), municipal bond history and default rates, estate and gift tax strategy, multigenerational wealth planning, and comparisons of how pensions, endowments (Yale, Harvard), and wealthy families actually allocate capital.

## Key points

- After-tax return modeling (1957–2009) shows the "stocks always beat bonds long-term" claim is materially weaker, and sometimes reversed, once dividends and capital gains are taxed annually at the top bracket rather than compounded tax-free.
- Portfolio turnover rate has a large, quantified after-tax cost: the book models turnover from 0–100% (average actively managed mutual funds run near 90% turnover vs. ~5% for the S&P 500 itself) and shows higher turnover materially erodes after-tax compounding.
- Municipal bonds get their own chapter (history of yields, defaults through the Great Depression, 1945–65 postwar period, and 1965–2008) as a systematically under-analyzed taxable-investor asset class.
- Alternative investments (commodities, currencies, hedge funds, private equity) are examined critically for whether they earn their fees and actually diversify in downturns — the book is skeptical, echoing Vanguard founder John Bogle's stated view (quoted at length) that hedge fund costs are "horrendous" and fail to diversify when it matters most.
- A "New Way to Look at Risk" chapter argues standard risk statistics mislead muni-fund investors and proposes stress-testing and valuation/leverage-risk measures instead.
- Compares the investing habits of institutions (pensions, PBGC, Yale and Harvard endowments) against wealthy families, finding families with concentrated, fewer-manager portfolios often outperform diversified institutional approaches after tax.
- Devotes chapters to estate tax, gift tax, and multigenerational planning, arguing a portfolio's after-tax structure — not just its pre-tax return — determines whether wealth survives across generations.
- Includes a "Train Wrecks" chapter of real (anonymized) case studies of wealthy business owners who lost fortunes through concentration, ego-driven decisions, and refusal to diversify or take advice.
- Closes by comparing wealth-management business models (family office, full-service, and direct wealth management) to help readers choose an advisor structure.

## Actionable rules

None given as mechanical trading rules — this is a tax-and-allocation strategy book for taxable high-net-worth portfolios, not a trading system. Its core actionable principle: always compute expected after-tax return (accounting for dividend tax rate, capital gains rate, state tax, and expected portfolio turnover) before comparing asset classes, since pre-tax comparisons can reverse the "correct" allocation decision for a top-bracket investor.

## Caveats

Written by a practicing wealth advisor whose firm's own historical case studies and calculators are used as evidence, which invites some selection bias; the After-Tax Returns table methodology (S&P 500 at assumed turnover rates) is model-based, not an independently audited dataset. Tax-rate figures, muni-bond yield history, and the "Train Wrecks" anecdotes reflect U.S. tax law and market conditions through 2009–2010 and predate significant later changes to estate, gift, and capital-gains tax law.

## Who it is for

High-net-worth individuals, family offices, and their advisors focused on after-tax portfolio construction, municipal bonds, and multigenerational estate planning — not a resource for active traders or short-term strategy.
