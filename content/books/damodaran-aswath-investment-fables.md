## Overview

Aswath Damodaran, the NYU valuation professor, takes on roughly a dozen recurring "stories" that brokers and the financial media use to sell stocks: buy high dividend payers, buy low PE stocks, buy below book value, buy "excellent" companies, buy growth, buy losers (contrarian), buy small unknown names, buy acquisitive companies, chase arbitrage, ride momentum, follow gurus, and trust that stocks always win long term. Each chapter repeats one format: a fictional vignette showing the story's typical failure mode, the financial theory that could justify it, a review of the academic evidence, a hands-on portfolio built from real market data (mostly an October 2002 snapshot of ~7,100 US stocks), and a "rest of the story" section on the hidden risks and screens needed to fix them. It is a myth-testing exercise, not a trading manual, aimed at investors pitched these stories as riskless formulas.

## Core thesis

No investment story is entirely false and none is a free lunch. Every story has "a kernel of truth" but also a specific failure mode the salesperson omits. Low PE stocks really do outperform on average, but low PE is often a symptom of low growth, high risk or poor earnings quality rather than mispricing. High dividend stocks resemble bonds, but dividends are not contractually guaranteed. Momentum exists over three-to-eight month horizons but reverses over multi-year horizons. Damodaran's method is consistent throughout: state the story, find the theoretical rationale, look at full-sample evidence rather than a cherry-picked backtest, build the actual screened portfolio, then identify the additional screens (risk, growth, quality, liquidity) needed to make the strategy survivable. The book closes with Ten Lessons for Investors that generalize across every chapter.

## Key concepts

- **PE ratio** — price per share divided by earnings per share (current, trailing, or forward); a "low PE" is only meaningful relative to the market/sector median, not an absolute cutoff.
- **Price-to-book ratio** — market price divided by book value of equity; low ratios can reflect undervaluation or genuinely low return on equity (ROE) and high leverage.
- **Beta and standard deviation** — the risk measures used throughout to test whether a "cheap" or "high dividend" portfolio is secretly a high-risk portfolio in disguise.
- **Serial correlation** — the statistical test for momentum/reversal: positive = price momentum, negative = mean reversion, zero = random walk.
- **Relative price strength** — (1 + stock return)/(1 + market return) over a lookback window; the basic momentum ranking measure.
- **Price drift** — prices continuing to move in the direction of a surprise (earnings, dividend, split) for days/weeks after the announcement, cited as evidence of slow-learning markets.
- **Earnings quality** — whether reported earnings reflect sustainable operations versus one-time gains or aggressive revenue recognition; a screen for both low-PE and high-dividend portfolios.
- **Signaling theory** — dividend changes, splits and buybacks are read by the market as management's private information about future prospects.
- **Conglomerate discount** — diversified firms trade at an estimated 5-10% discount to the sum of their parts, undercutting the "stable earnings via diversification" story.
- **Herd behavior models for momentum** — investors learn slowly from news (price drift), or learn by watching other investors trade (volume-reinforced moves).
- **"Hot hands" in mutual funds** — top funds repeating their ranking the following year; strong in the 1970s, weak afterward.

## Rules and setups

This is a valuation/screening book, not an entry-exit trading system - there are no stop-losses or intraday timeframes. The "rules" are portfolio-construction screens applied to real cross-sections of the market (vintage October 2002 unless noted):

1. **High dividend screen (Ch. 2)**: rank stocks by trailing yield (median among payers ≈2.54%). The top-100-yield portfolio concentrates ~40% in REITs and ~20% each in utilities/financials. Follow-up screens: payout ratio versus normalized earnings; exclude yields inflated by a recent price collapse (cuts drop price ~4.5% on announcement versus +1% for increases).
2. **Low PE screen (Ch. 3)**: absolute cutoff (e.g., PE < 8) or relative cutoff (10th-20th percentile of market/sector). Apply a risk filter (beta < 1.25, std. dev. < 80%, which cut a sample of 115 to 91), a minimum forward-growth filter (>5-10%, which removed 27-52 names), and a historical-growth confirmation (>5% trailing). Screen out likely earnings manipulation: repeated one-time charges, frequent restatements, revenue growth far outpacing earnings growth.
3. **Low price-to-book screen (Ch. 4)**: below a fraction of book value (e.g., <40% yielded 195 names from 5,883 firms). Composite risk screen (beta <1.5, std. dev. <80%, debt/capital <70%) cut the portfolio to 51. Separately exclude sub-$2 stocks, where commissions/spread/impact can eat 15-25%+ of the investment.
4. **Momentum screen (Ch. 12)**: rank by relative price strength over a 6-12 month lookback, strongest when confirmed by above-average volume; reverses at the 5-year horizon. No exit rule beyond recognizing this is a crowd trade you "live by... and die by."
5. **Growth, contrarian, small-cap, merger-arbitrage, and "expert-following" chapters (Ch. 6-11, 13)** repeat the same theory → evidence → portfolio → risk-screen pattern without adding numerically distinct rules beyond variants of the above.

## Risk and money management

Damodaran does not size positions or set stops; risk management here means screening out the hidden risk baked into each story before committing capital:
- **Concentration risk**: naive high-yield or low-multiple screens concentrate in a handful of sectors (REITs, utilities, financials) - diversify even within a "cheap stocks" mandate.
- **Leverage/distress risk**: cheap-looking stocks often carry higher debt/capital ratios; screen with a debt ceiling (50-70% of capital in the worked examples).
- **Liquidity/transaction-cost risk**: sub-$2 to sub-$5 stocks impose costs that can exceed 25% of the position - exclude them or amortize over a long holding period.
- **Tax cost**: dividends are taxed at a higher rate than capital gains, an explicit drag on the high-dividend strategy.
- **"Luck vs. skill" caveat**: short-term outperformance is dominated by luck; track records need long horizons before they are informative.

## Psychology and discipline

The book is framed around behavioral vulnerability: greed, fear, hope and envy are the emotional hooks that make each story sell. Momentum is explained via herd psychology - slow learning about news, and investors inferring information from other investors' trading. Chapter 15's "Ten Lessons" double as a discipline checklist: expect no guarantees, no return without risk, respect that markets are usually right before assuming you found a mispricing, and pass any strategy through an "acid test" (does it keep you up at night?) and a "patience test" (can you sit through its normal drawdowns?). The recurring moral-of-the-story vignettes counteract the sales-pitch framing investors are used to hearing.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Introduction — categorizes stories by investor psychology (risk-averse, risk-seeking, greedy, hopeful); lays out the theory-evidence-portfolio-screen method.
- Ch 2 — High Dividend Stocks — yield distributions, sector concentration, sustainability tests, price reaction to dividend changes.
- Ch 3 — Low Price Earnings Story — PE mechanics, accounting-earnings primer, empirical PE-return studies, growth/risk/quality screens.
- Ch 4 — Less Than Book Value — price-to-book across sectors, ROE/leverage drivers, risk and liquidity screens.
- Ch 5 — Stable Earnings, Better Investment? — utilities and conglomerates; the conglomerate discount against the diversification story.
- Ch 6 — In Search of Excellence — tests whether "quality"/excellent-company rankings predict returns.
- Ch 7 — The Growth Story — growth-stock valuation, historical growth-investing returns and risk.
- Ch 8 — The Contrarian Story — buying losers; overreaction evidence and serial-correlation tests.
- Ch 9 — The Next Big Thing — small-cap and IPO investing, neglected-stock effect.
- Ch 10 — The Acquisitive Company — evidence on serial acquirers and pooling-accounting-era growth illusions.
- Ch 11 — A Sure Thing — true vs. near vs. speculative arbitrage.
- Ch 12 — The Momentum Story — trend lines, relative strength, price/volume momentum, serial correlation, mutual-fund "hot hands."
- Ch 13 — Follow the Experts — analyst recommendations, insider trading, newsletter/guru track records.
- Ch 14 — Myths About Markets — the "stocks always win long term" claim and market-timing indicators.
- Ch 15 — Ten Lessons for Investors — no guarantees, no free risk, respect fundamentals, cheap is often cheap for a reason, know your risk tolerance, luck matters more than investors admit.

## Strengths and caveats

The empirical work is genuinely data-driven (real cross-sections of thousands of stocks, cited academic studies) rather than anecdotal, and the theory-then-evidence-then-portfolio structure is unusually rigorous for a retail-facing book. The main dating issue: portfolios are built from an October 2002 cross-section, so absolute cutoffs cited (PE of 8, yield of 2.5%, sub-$2 liquidity costs) are illustrations of method, not current thresholds. Some cited studies (pooling accounting, 1970s-80s "hot hands" data) reference rules or eras that no longer apply. Damodaran is explicitly skeptical rather than promotional - a strength for objectivity, but no single strategy gets a clean green light; every chapter ends in "it depends," which traders wanting a ready-made system will find frustrating.

## Who should read it

Value and quantitative investors who want to see how commonly touted screens (low PE, low P/B, high dividend, momentum) perform empirically, and who want a template for stress-testing a stock-picking story before adopting it. Less useful for short-term traders wanting execution rules, since risk management here is screening at portfolio-construction time, not trade management.
