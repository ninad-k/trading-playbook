## Overview

Aswath Damodaran, the NYU valuation professor, takes on roughly a dozen recurring "stories" that brokers, advisors and the financial media use to sell stocks: buy high dividend payers, buy low PE stocks, buy below book value, buy "excellent" companies, buy growth, buy losers (contrarian), buy small unknown names, buy acquisitive companies, chase arbitrage, ride momentum, follow gurus, and trust that stocks always win long term. Each chapter follows the same format: a short fictional vignette illustrating the story and its typical failure mode, a statement of the "core of the story" and the financial theory that could justify it, a review of the academic and empirical evidence for and against it, a hands-on construction of an actual portfolio from real market data (using an October 2002 snapshot of roughly 7,000-7,100 US stocks, supplemented with longer-run studies), and a "rest of the story" section laying out the hidden risks and the screens needed to fix them. The book is not a trading manual; it is a myth-testing exercise aimed at retail and institutional investors who are pitched these stories as if they were riskless formulas.

## Core thesis

No investment story is entirely false and none is a free lunch. Every one of the dozen stories has "a kernel of truth" - a piece of finance theory and some supporting data - but each also has a specific, identifiable failure mode that the salesperson omits. Low PE stocks really do outperform on average, but low PE is often a symptom of low growth, high risk or poor earnings quality rather than mispricing. High dividend stocks really do resemble bonds, but dividends are not contractually guaranteed and can be cut. Momentum really does exist over three-to-eight month horizons, but reverses over multi-year horizons and is a crowd trade that can end suddenly. Damodaran's method for evaluating any such story is consistent throughout: state the story, find the theoretical rationale, look at the full-sample evidence (not the cherry-picked backtest), build the actual screened portfolio, and then examine what additional screens (for risk, growth, quality, liquidity) are needed to make the strategy survivable. The book closes with Ten Lessons for Investors that generalize across every chapter - see Chapter map for Chapter 15.

## Key concepts

- **PE ratio (price-earnings)** — market price per share divided by earnings per share (current, trailing four-quarter, or forward); a "low PE" of under 8-12 is a common but arbitrary screen and must be judged relative to the market or sector median, not an absolute number.
- **Price-to-book ratio** — market price divided by book value of equity; low ratios can reflect either undervaluation or genuinely low return on equity (ROE) and high leverage.
- **Beta and standard deviation** — the two risk measures used throughout the book to test whether a "cheap" or "high dividend" portfolio is secretly just a high-risk portfolio in disguise.
- **Serial correlation** — the statistical test for momentum/reversal: positive serial correlation = price momentum, negative = mean reversion, zero = random walk.
- **Relative price strength** — (1 + stock return)/(1 + market return) over a lookback window; the basic momentum ranking measure used in Chapter 12.
- **Price drift** — the tendency of stock prices to continue moving in the direction of a surprise (earnings, dividend change, stock split) for days or weeks after the announcement, cited as evidence of slow-learning markets.
- **Earnings quality** — whether reported earnings reflect sustainable operations versus one-time gains, pension income, or aggressive revenue recognition; a screen recommended for both low-PE and high-dividend portfolios.
- **Signaling theory** — corporate actions (dividend increases/cuts, splits, buybacks) are read by the market as management's private information about future prospects.
- **Conglomerate discount** — diversified/multi-business firms trade at an estimated 5-10% discount to the sum of their parts, undercutting the "stable earnings via diversification" story.
- **Payout ratio / dividend sustainability** — dividends-to-earnings (or to normalized/potential earnings) used to test whether a high yield can be maintained.
- **Herd behavior models for momentum** — (1) investors learn slowly from news, so prices drift after announcements; (2) investors learn by watching other investors trade, so price moves are reinforced by volume.
- **"Hot hands" in mutual funds** — the tendency (found strongly in the 1970s, weakly afterward) for top-performing funds to repeat their ranking the following year.

## Rules and setups

This is a valuation/screening book, not an entry-exit trading system - there are no stop-losses or intraday timeframes. The "rules" are portfolio-construction screens Damodaran actually applies to real cross-sections of the US market (data vintage October 2002 unless noted):

1. **High dividend screen (Ch. 2)**: rank ~7,100 stocks by trailing dividend yield (median dividend yield among payers ≈2.54%, mean ≈3.32%, distorted upward by REITs/utilities). Building a portfolio of the 100 highest-yield stocks concentrates ~40% in REITs and ~20% each in utilities and financials. Required follow-up screens: check payout ratio against normalized earnings; exclude yields inflated by a recent price collapse (dividend cuts historically drop price ~4.5% on announcement, versus roughly +1% for increases).
2. **Low PE screen (Ch. 3)**: pick stocks below an absolute cutoff (e.g., PE < 8) or a relative cutoff (10th-20th percentile of market/sector PE). Apply a risk filter (beta < 1.25 and standard deviation < 80%, which cut a sample of 115 down to 91 in the book's worked example). Apply a minimum expected-growth filter (>5-10% forward EPS growth removed 27-52 names from the same sample) and a historical-growth confirmation filter (>5% trailing growth). Screen out likely earnings-manipulation candidates: repeated one-time charges, frequent restatements, revenue growth far outpacing earnings growth.
3. **Low price-to-book screen (Ch. 4)**: select stocks trading below a fraction of book value (example: <40% of book value yielded 195 names from 5,883 firms with positive book equity). Apply a composite risk screen (beta <1.5, standard deviation <80%, debt/capital <70%), which cut the 195-name portfolio to 51. Separately screen out very low-priced stocks (<$2/share) because commissions, bid-ask spread and price impact can eat 15-25%+ of the investment.
4. **Momentum screen (Ch. 12)**: rank stocks by relative price strength over a 6-12 month lookback; the momentum effect is strongest when price moves are confirmed by above-average trading volume, and reverses at the 5-year horizon (a 6-month winner in the book's data set tends to keep winning through ~8 months, then mean-reverts over multi-year windows). No stop-loss or explicit exit rule is given beyond the observation that this is a crowd trade that "you live by... and die by."
5. **Growth, contrarian, small-cap, merger-arbitrage, and "expert-following" chapters (Ch. 6-11, 13)** follow the identical pattern of theory → evidence → portfolio → risk screen but do not add numerically distinct entry rules beyond variants of the growth-rate, risk, and liquidity filters already listed above.

## Risk and money management

Damodaran does not size positions or set stops; risk management in this book means screening out the specific hidden risk baked into each story before committing capital:
- **Concentration risk**: naive high-yield or low-multiple screens concentrate in a handful of sectors (REITs, utilities, financials) - diversify across sectors even within a "cheap stocks" mandate.
- **Leverage/distress risk**: cheap-looking stocks (low PE, low P/B) often carry higher debt/capital ratios; screen with a debt ceiling (the book uses 50-70% of capital as cutoffs).
- **Liquidity/transaction-cost risk**: sub-$2 to sub-$5 stocks impose commission, spread and price-impact costs that can exceed 25% of the position for the cheapest names - either exclude them or amortize the cost over a long (10-year) holding period.
- **Tax cost**: dividend-based strategies are penalized because dividends are taxed at a higher rate than capital gains, an explicit drag noted in the Ten Lessons.
- **"Luck vs. skill" caveat**: Lesson 10 explicitly warns that short-term outperformance (of a strategy or a fund manager) is dominated by luck, and that track records need long horizons before they are informative.

## Psychology and discipline

The book is framed almost entirely around behavioral vulnerability: greed, fear, hope and envy are named as the four emotional hooks that make each story sell. Momentum is explained via herd psychology (slow learning about news, and investors inferring information from other investors' trading rather than fundamentals). Chapter 15's "Ten Lessons" double as a discipline checklist: expect no guarantees, no return without risk, respect that markets are usually right before assuming you found a mispricing, and pass any strategy you adopt through an "acid test" (does it keep you up at night?) and a "patience test" (can you sit through its normal drawdowns without abandoning it prematurely). The recurring moral-of-the-story vignettes (a retiree wiped out by a dividend cut, a value investor disillusioned by a "cheap" portfolio of distressed names, a momentum trader blindsided by a reversal) are used deliberately to counteract the sales-pitch framing investors are used to hearing.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Introduction — categorizes investment stories by which investor psychology (risk-averse, risk-seeking, greedy, hopeful) they target, and lays out the book's theory-evidence-portfolio-screen method.
- Ch 2 — High Dividend Stocks: Bonds With Price Appreciation? — dividend yield distributions, sector concentration, sustainability tests, price reaction to dividend changes.
- Ch 3 — This Stock Is So Cheap! The Low Price Earnings Story — PE mechanics, accounting-earnings primer, empirical PE-return studies, growth/risk/quality screens.
- Ch 4 — Less Than Book Value! What a Bargain? — price-to-book across sectors, ROE/leverage drivers, risk and liquidity screens.
- Ch 5 — Stable Earnings, Better Investment? — utilities and conglomerates; the conglomerate discount evidence against the diversification story.
- Ch 6 — In Search of Excellence! Are Good Companies Good Investments? — tests whether "quality"/excellent-company rankings predict stock returns.
- Ch 7 — U.S. Steel, IBM and Microsoft: The Growth Story! — growth-stock valuation, historical growth-investing returns and risk.
- Ch 8 — The Worst Is Behind You! The Contrarian Story — buying losers/beaten-down stocks; overreaction evidence and serial-correlation tests.
- Ch 9 — The Next Big Thing! New Businesses and Young Companies — small-cap and IPO investing, neglected-stock effect.
- Ch 10 — Mergers and Returns! The Acquisitive Company — evidence on serial acquirers and pooling-accounting-era growth illusions.
- Ch 11 — A Sure Thing! No Risk and Sure Profits — true vs. near vs. speculative arbitrage.
- Ch 12 — It's All Upside! The Momentum Story — trend lines, relative strength, price/volume momentum models, serial correlation and price-drift evidence, mutual-fund "hot hands."
- Ch 13 — Follow the Experts — analyst recommendations, insider trading, newsletter/guru track records.
- Ch 14 — In the Long Term... Myths About Markets — the "stocks always win long term" claim and market-timing indicators.
- Ch 15 — Ten Lessons for Investors — cross-chapter synthesis: no guarantees, no free risk, respect fundamentals, cheap is often cheap for a reason, know your own risk tolerance, and luck matters more than investors admit.

## Strengths and caveats

The empirical work is genuinely data-driven (real cross-sections of thousands of US stocks, cited academic studies with sources) rather than anecdotal, and the theory-then-evidence-then-portfolio structure is unusually rigorous for a retail-facing book. The main dating issue is the market snapshot: portfolios are built from an October 2002 cross-section (post dot-com-crash valuations, pre-decimalization-era transaction cost figures in places), so absolute PE/yield/price cutoffs cited (PE of 8, dividend yield of 2.5%, sub-$2 liquidity costs) will not transfer directly to other market regimes and should be read as illustrations of method, not current thresholds. Some cited studies (pooling accounting for acquisitions, 1970s-80s mutual fund "hot hands" data) reference rules or eras that no longer apply. Damodaran is explicitly agnostic/skeptical rather than promotional, which is a strength for objectivity but means the book gives no single strategy a clean, actionable green light - every chapter ends in "it depends" and "screen further," which traders looking for a ready-made system will find frustrating.

## Who should read it

Value and quantitative investors who want to see how commonly touted screens (low PE, low P/B, high dividend, momentum) actually perform empirically, and who want a template for stress-testing a stock-picking story before adopting it. Less useful for short-term traders wanting execution rules, since there is no timeframe, stop-loss or position-sizing framework - the "risk management" here is screening criteria applied at portfolio-construction time, not trade management.
