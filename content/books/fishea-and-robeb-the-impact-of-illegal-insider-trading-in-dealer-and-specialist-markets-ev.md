## Summary

Question: does illegal insider trading measurably damage liquidity in dealer (Nasdaq) versus specialist (NYSE/Amex) markets? Data/method: the authors exploit a real, prosecuted case in which five stockbrokers traded 116 stocks ahead of publication of Business Week's "Inside Wall Street" column, using advance copies obtained illegally. Because court records identify exactly which stocks were traded and when, this is a natural experiment isolating genuine informed (insider) trades from ordinary trading, with a non-traded control sample of other stocks mentioned in the same column. Finding: insider buying materially increases price and volume and forces market makers to cut quoted depth and widen spreads to manage adverse-selection risk, with the effect strongest on NYSE/Amex specialist markets; Nasdaq dealer depth falls much less. No comparable pre-release moves appear in the non-traded control sample.

## Key points

- Ask-side quoted depth falls roughly 38% for NYSE/Amex stocks during insider trading intervals, versus only about 3% for Nasdaq stocks.
- All identified informed trades were purchases, and it is specifically ask-depth (not bid-depth) that reacts.
- Average cumulative abnormal return per insider episode is large (about 6.85%), even though insider volume is only about 11.3% of a stock's total volume that day.
- Quoted and effective spreads increase significantly during insider-trading periods for exchange-listed (NYSE/Amex) stocks; the Nasdaq spread reaction is weaker.
- Significant abnormal price appreciation shows up specifically on the Friday following Thursday informed buying, consistent with the column's Friday publication schedule.
- Brokers were slow to fully size the opportunity, and one broker's trades were even unprofitable, showing informed trading isn't automatically riskless.
- Specialist (auction) markets detect and react to information-based order flow better than dealer (Nasdaq) markets, consistent with specialists seeing the full order book.

## Actionable rules

Not a trading system; practical takeaways: (1) unusually large ask-depth withdrawal alongside rising volume and price can signal informed/insider buying pressure, particularly on exchange-listed names; (2) expect wider effective spreads and thinner offers when informed flow is present, raising the cost of chasing a move; (3) informed-trading effects concentrate in a short window around the information event and are easier to detect on specialist-market stocks than dealer-market ones.

## Caveats

A rigorous academic microstructure paper, not a retail trading guide — its "rules" are statistical findings, not a system to code. It examines one historical natural experiment (one court case), so results may not generalize to modern all-electronic markets, which no longer resemble the NYSE specialist / Nasdaq dealer structure studied here.

## Who it is for

Quant researchers and advanced traders interested in market microstructure, liquidity provision, and how information-based trading shows up in spreads and depth — useful background for anyone building order-flow or liquidity-based signals.
