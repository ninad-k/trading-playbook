## Summary

A 2004 introductory course from "1st Forex Trading Academy" covering the structure and history of the forex market, who the major participants are, the practical differences between day trading and position trading, the mechanics of currency pairs and quotes, the benefits of retail online FX trading versus equities, a guide to reading and interpreting a weekly economic calendar, general risk-management principles, and a primer on fundamental analysis, followed by a glossary and FAQ. It is a broad orientation course rather than a specific trading system.

## Key points

- Forex market background: established after the 1971 collapse of Bretton Woods fixed exchange rates; a 24-hour, decentralized OTC/interbank market following the sun from Sydney through Tokyo, London, and New York.
- Major participants: central banks, commercial/interbank dealers, interbank brokers, commercial companies (trade hedgers), retail brokers, hedge funds, and speculators; speculation is estimated at over 95% of daily volume.
- Day trading vs. position trading contrasted: day traders hold minutes-to-hours and rely more on technicals/emotion management; position traders hold toward a delivery-style horizon (5–7 months) and enter mainly at key reversal points.
- Leverage described as ranging 50:1 to 400:1 in the retail market (example: control $250,000 with $5,000 margin); the course suggests 5:1–10:1 as more prudent for a newcomer, while calling 100:1 a "foremost suggested" balance of risk and reward — these two recommendations are in tension.
- Average daily trading ranges cited (in pips, era-specific): EUR/USD 76, USD/JPY 105, GBP/USD 96, USD/CHF 140.
- Currency pair mechanics: base currency vs. quote/counter currency, bid/offer quoting convention, pip definition (smallest price increment).
- Risk management section frames stop placement as a trade-off: too tight and normal noise triggers it; too far and there's no time/room to react; recommends having a trading plan first, then choosing entries deliberately.
- Stop-and-reverse (SAR) concept: if the market proves your directional read wrong, consider reversing rather than only exiting; not necessarily at the same instant/level.
- Fundamental analysis section explains reading Forex quotes and frames longer-horizon fundamental analysis as less suited to day trading due to the research time required.

## Actionable rules

1. Favor lower leverage (5:1–10:1) as a newcomer rather than the maximum available (up to 400:1); the course elsewhere suggests 100:1 as a "best" risk/reward balance, so treat this as a range to size down from rather than a fixed number.
2. Place stops meaningfully — not so close that normal price noise triggers them, not so far that there is no time to react — which requires defining entries according to a plan rather than ad hoc.
3. When a directional view is proven wrong by the market, consider a stop-and-reverse (SAR) into the opposite position rather than only flattening.
4. Use risk/reward from technical support/resistance levels to size the trade: require a larger pip distance to your profit target than to your stop before taking a trade.
5. Track the weekly economic calendar and understand each release's category (leading/coincident/lagging) before trading around it.

## Caveats

This is a 2004-era beginner marketing course (references pre-2008 leverage limits up to 400:1 that are no longer available to US retail traders after Dodd-Frank leverage caps, and cites average trading ranges and commission figures specific to that period). The leverage guidance is internally inconsistent (recommends both 5:1–10:1 for beginners and 100:1 as "foremost suggested" elsewhere in the same document) and should not be taken as a single coherent rule. No backtested system, specific entries/exits, or quantified track record is provided — it is oriented toward orientation and terminology rather than a tradable method.

## Who it is for

Complete beginners to forex who need grounding in market structure, participants, quoting conventions, and general risk-management vocabulary before moving to a specific trading system.
