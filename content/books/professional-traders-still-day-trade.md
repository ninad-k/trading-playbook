## Summary

Written for SFO Magazine, Linda Raschke (full-time trader since 1981, floor trader turned CTA, author of Street Smarts, featured in Schwager's The New Market Wizards) and partner Christopher Terry argue against treating day, swing, momentum, and position trading as separate, mutually exclusive disciplines. Historically, most professional traders — floor scalpers, specialists, option market-makers — have always made "day trades" as part of a broader practice, and the article traces how each style's popularity has shifted with volatility from the 1980s scalping era through the high-volatility late-1990s. Their practical question — can you still day-trade for a living as volatility trends back down — is answered with a framework: divide a market's price by its average daily range; the lower the ratio, the longer a trader must hold to overcome the spread and commissions ("the vig").

## Key points

- **Day trading is not one thing**: spans scalpers turning inventory constantly, traders who day-trade only their losers, and traders "probing" market strength with small trades.
- **Historical base rate**: a mid-'80s-to-mid-'90s clearing-firm study found only about 8% of retail accounts were profitable, with the most profitable accounts having the highest activity levels.
- **Four styles**: swing trading (reads prior swing structure), momentum trading (jumping on parabolic moves, trailing stops), position trading (breakouts from accumulation/distribution, only 2-3 good setups per market per year), and day trading/scalping.
- **Volatility rule of thumb**: divide price by average daily range; the lower the ratio, the more a trader must hold to clear noise and costs.
- **Regime shift noted (~2000)**: equity volatility (elevated since 1997) was starting to trend down, while currency and cash-commodity volatility was turning back up.
- **Nightly prep routine**: review daily charts for trend vs. range, flag potential trend days, note key pivots (20-day highs/lows, gaps), and keep a running list of 10-15 setups and 8-12 stock plays.
- **Closing citation (Schabacker)**: traders willing to commit time to technical study do better short-term; those unsuited to short-term stress do better, more slowly, trading the long swing.

## Actionable rules

1. Compute a market's price-to-average-daily-range ratio before choosing its trading timeframe; a low ratio implies holding longer to clear costs.
2. Prepare the night before: classify the next session as trending or range-bound, flag potential trend days, and note key pivots in advance.
3. Keep a running watchlist of 10-15 setups and 8-12 stock plays daily.
4. Use momentum-style entries only in markets showing a genuine strong trend; use scalping/probing entries in flatter conditions.
5. Do not force a single-style identity — allocate capital across timeframes based on what each market currently offers.

## Caveats

Short magazine feature (4 pages), written specifically around the volatility environment of the late 1990s/2000 and framed as a forward-looking opinion piece by the authors rather than a backtested study; the 8% retail-account profitability figure is attributed to a single unnamed clearing-firm study with no citation detail. No specific entry/exit/stop rules are given for any of the four styles.

## Who it is for

Traders who feel pressured to identify as strictly a "day trader" or "swing trader" and want a professional's case for blending timeframes based on market conditions rather than personal identity.
