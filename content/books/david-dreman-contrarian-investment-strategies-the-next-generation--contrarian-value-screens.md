## What it is

A set of four related, purely valuation-based stock-selection screens that Dreman backtested on the Compustat 1500 (large, well-established U.S. companies) over 1970–1996: low price-to-earnings (P/E), low price-to-cash-flow (P/CF), low price-to-book-value (P/BV), and high dividend yield (equivalently, low price-to-dividend). Each screen independently selects the cheapest (or highest-yielding) quintile of the universe by that single ratio; all four are shown to outperform both the market average and the corresponding "most expensive"/lowest-yield quintile over long holding periods. The strategy requires no forecasting of individual company turnarounds — it is a systematic bet that the market persistently overreacts to a stock's recent past and that reversion toward average valuations is itself the source of return.

## Rules

**Universe**: large, established companies — Dreman's tests use the Compustat 1500. He separately notes that small-cap and Nasdaq stocks behave differently and should not be assumed to follow the same statistics (treated as a distinct, higher-effort strategy elsewhere in the book).

**Screen construction (apply any or all of the four, independently)**:
1. Low P/E: rank the universe by trailing price-to-earnings; buy from the cheapest quintile (bottom 20% by P/E).
2. Low P/CF: rank by price-to-cash-flow-per-share; buy from the cheapest quintile.
3. Low P/BV: rank by price relative to book value, comparing book value to industry or market norms (not raw historical book value, since replacement costs of assets have risen well above balance-sheet carrying values since Graham's era); buy from the cheapest quintile.
4. High yield / low P/D: rank by dividend yield; buy from the highest-yielding quintile, favoring companies whose payout is sustainable and can grow (a supporting quality check, not a separate ranking).

**Preferred core**: Dreman's own firm uses low P/E as the primary/core screen (most current, most widely available data, updated quarterly) while running the other three as parallel/opportunistic screens, since attractive mispricings surface differently across the four measures at different times; the book explicitly recommends an eclectic approach over committing to a single ratio.

**Position construction**: diversify broadly rather than concentrate — Dreman cites research suggesting a well-diversified portfolio of roughly 16 stocks captures about 85–90% of the return achievable from the full quintile the stocks are drawn from.

**Holding period / rebalancing**: hold long-term rather than trade actively. Dreman's own reported odds of the low-P/E strategy beating the market rise with the holding period: roughly 87 out of 100 five-year periods, and roughly 94–95 out of 100 ten-year periods, in his historical sample; he explicitly discourages quarterly portfolio changes and favors annual (or longer) rebalancing.

## Risk

Risk is managed at the portfolio level, not per-position: there is no stop-loss or per-trade risk percentage in this system. The two controls given are (a) diversification across roughly 16+ names within the cheap quintile, so that any single company's disappointment does not dominate the portfolio, and (b) patience through the strategy's own statistically-expected "cold streaks" — Dreman frames underperforming stretches as expected variance around a favorable long-run edge (analogous to a casino's edge over many hands), not evidence the strategy has stopped working. Statistical-significance backing reported for the core screens: low P/E and low P/CF each show about a 1-in-200 probability of their historical outperformance being due to chance; low P/BV about 1-in-100; the corresponding most-expensive/lowest-yield quintiles show weaker (roughly 1-in-20, i.e., 95%-confidence-level) but still generally significant underperformance.

## Caveats

The backtest window (roughly 1970–1996) predates the late-1990s valuation extremes and their unwind, so out-of-sample performance in different valuation regimes is not demonstrated in the text. The book gives only one of its stated five "quality" indicators in the sampled OCR pages used to write this note (Indicator 5: an above-average, sustainable, and growing dividend yield) — Indicators 1 through 4, which the text says should also inform stock selection alongside the pure valuation quintile, were not recoverable from the sampled pages and are not included here; treat the four ratio screens above as necessary but, per the source, not sufficient on their own. No explicit sell/exit rule or valuation-based rebalancing trigger is given beyond "hold long-term, rebalance infrequently."
