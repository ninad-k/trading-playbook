## Summary

The course is organized as seven parts for trading S&P futures and Nasdaq 100 futures. It starts with mental preparation and choosing a style, then distinguishes trending from range-bound days, covers entries, exits, stops, scaling, support and resistance, pivots, breakouts and false breakouts, and ends with momentum, sentiment, stock-futures relationships, economic events, and direct-access questions (pp. 1-2). Its recurring premise is that a trader needs a written plan covering mental state, technical context, entry and exit points, and the day’s market character. The assigned extract is instructional and example-driven; it introduces the topics more reliably than it supplies a fully reproducible trading algorithm.

## Key points

- Part 1 puts self-knowledge and discipline before pattern selection (pp. 1-2).
- Trend and range days require different assumptions and tactics (p. 1).
- Execution lessons include entry, exit, stop placement, scaling in and out, and risk-reward assessment (pp. 1-2).
- Daily and intraday charts are used to locate support and resistance and assess breakout validity (p. 1).
- Economic releases named include PPI, CPI, and employment reports (p. 1).
- The course focuses on ES and NQ but says many principles can transfer to other markets (p. 2).
- The 200-day moving average is used to frame trend stage; the course describes widening, parallel, and narrowing price-to-average distance as early, middle, and late trend behavior (p. 11).
- Triangles are described as contracting ranges whose breakouts often occur around three-quarters of the distance to the apex; the first three to five breakout days are presented as especially vulnerable to fake-outs (pp. 11-12).
- The course's “Ready, Aim, Fire” example uses a long entry at 1513, a 1511 stop, and a 1519 objective, a 2:6 or 1:3 risk/reward illustration (p. 17); it separately states a preferred minimum of 1:2 (pp. 18-22).
- Intraday context includes key levels, volume, related indexes, divergence, retracements, and the opening or morning pivot; the author emphasizes observing behavior around a level rather than reacting to the level alone (pp. 23-29).
- The NQ section says the five largest holdings represented roughly 25% of index capitalization at the time, estimates NQ volatility at about three times ES, and describes thin liquidity and rapid “air ball” moves (pp. 34-37).
- A historical NQ example treats a limit-down failure, strength in four other technology stocks, and inability to break a recent low as converging evidence for a reversal; it is a hindsight case study, not a tested signal (pp. 38-39).

## Actionable rules

1. Prepare a daily plan before trading, including market type, entry, exit, stop, and mental preparation (p. 2).
2. Decide whether conditions are trending or range-bound before selecting a breakout, fade, or other tactic (pp. 1-2).
3. Treat size and stop placement as linked risk decisions; the NQ discussion explicitly says wider volatility-based stops should be paired with smaller position size to keep total risk in balance (p. 35).
4. Require a setup to include key price behavior, volume or volatility context, and a predefined exit; the course warns that a level by itself is not confirmation (pp. 18-29).

## Caveats

The PDF is a course compilation with historical ES/NQ conventions and anecdotal examples. The 1:2 preference, 200-day observations, NQ volatility estimate, and event-time behavior are author guidance from 2000-2001, not independent validation. The course supplies no reproducible expectancy study, and futures leverage, contract specifications, and event risk require current verification (pp. 11-12, 17-22, 34-39).

## Who it is for

Experienced intraday futures traders who want a structured discretionary course outline.
