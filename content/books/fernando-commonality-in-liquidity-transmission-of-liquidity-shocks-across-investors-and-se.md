## Summary

Fernando develops a two-period model of investors who experience liquidity shocks and trade a risky asset subject to transaction costs. The paper asks whether common liquidity shocks explain observed commonality in volume, spreads, and volatility. Systematic shocks are shared across investors; idiosyncratic shocks differ across investors and create heterogeneous liquidity demand. The model predicts that common shocks raise price volatility but do not create common trading volume, while idiosyncratic shocks generate volume that can be diversified through trading (pp. 3–9). With multiple securities, liquidity transmission requires meaningful substitutes or correlated fundamentals (pp. 30–36).

## Key points

- Systematic liquidity risk is always priced because investors cannot diversify a shock shared by everyone (pp. 4–8).
- Trading volume rises with the variance of idiosyncratic liquidity shocks, not with the common component alone (pp. 4–7, 37–38).
- Price volatility rises with both systematic and idiosyncratic shock variance, so volatility and volume are not interchangeable liquidity measures (pp. 4–8, 37–38).
- A liquid market lets investors transfer idiosyncratic liquidity risk across the investor base; illiquidity makes that risk more fully reflected in prices (pp. 4, 30–36).
- When asset returns are uncorrelated, common investor shocks do not transmit liquidity across securities in the model (pp. 4, 8–9).
- A liquid, otherwise identical substitute can hedge an illiquid asset and eliminate its theoretical liquidity discount when short selling is available (pp. 8–9, 33–36).
- The paper uses a simple batch market and quadratic transaction-cost specification, which make the comparative statics tractable but stylized (pp. 10–12).

## Actionable rules

1. None given. The paper is a theoretical asset-pricing model, not a trading strategy.
2. As a measurement implication, do not interpret high volume as proof of a common liquidity shock; pair volume with volatility and spread measures (pp. 4–8).

## Caveats

The model excludes information-driven trading and assumes normally distributed shocks, exponential utility, and an exogenous transaction-cost structure (pp. 9–12, 37–38). Its substitute-security result depends on identical payoffs and no short-sale constraints (pp. 33–36). No market dataset or out-of-sample trading test is provided.

## Who it is for

Researchers and execution specialists who need a conceptual map of liquidity risk. It helps diagnose market data but does not generate entries or exits.
