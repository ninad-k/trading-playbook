## What it is

Fisher's method for finding "Super Stocks": companies that will return 3–10x in three to five years. Because sales rarely disappear the way earnings can, he values companies on the Price/Sales Ratio (PSR = total market value ÷ trailing 12-month sales) instead of the P/E ratio, then, for technology companies specifically, cross-checks with the Price/Research Ratio (PRR = total market value ÷ trailing 12-month R&D spend). The screen is meant to surface Super Companies — businesses with strong underlying fundamentals — while they are priced like mediocre ones, typically just after an earnings "glitch" has driven away conventional, P/E-focused buyers.

## Rules

**Step 1 — qualitative filter (must pass before valuation matters):** confirm the company shows growth orientation, marketing excellence, a durable "unfair advantage" (usually lowest-cost producer or a proprietary position), a healthy personnel culture, and strong financial controls. Only apply the ratio rules below to companies that pass this filter — a low PSR on a mediocre company is a value trap, not a Super Stock.

**Step 2 — PSR entry:**
- Buy Super Companies with a PSR of 0.75 or less.
- Avoid any stock with a PSR above 1.5; never buy above 3.0.
- Separate bands apply outside the "Super Company/small-growth" universe: basic-industry ("smokestack") stocks — buy under 0.4; huge (multi-billion-dollar sales) companies — buy near 0.4. Larger, more mature businesses structurally carry lower PSRs than small ones, so scale the threshold to company size.

**Step 3 — PRR cross-check (technology companies only):**
- Favor a PRR between 5 and 10. Below 5 is rare (the company is usually already bid up by someone else).
- Reject anything with a PRR above 15.
- Use PRR to catch mismatches PSR alone misses: a marginally high PSR (~1.0) paired with a low PRR (~5) can mean cheap, underappreciated R&D — investigate the marketing behind it before buying. A low, seemingly attractive PSR (~0.75) paired with a high PRR (25+) usually means the company is starving its product pipeline — treat it as a warning, not a buy signal.
- Do not over-engineer PRR comparisons — Fisher explicitly warns against treating a PRR of 10.4 as meaningfully different from 11.8; treat it as a rough (±20%) gauge, not a precision instrument.

**Step 4 — screening workflow:** scan an industry or the broader market for low-PSR names (Fisher used a paid financial-data service; today any screener with PSR/EV-to-sales works), separately scan for money-losing companies with unfamiliar names (financial-community neglect is itself a signal), and separately collect qualitative "outstanding business" tips from other sources. Cross-reference all three lists against Step 1's qualitative filter.

**Step 5 — sizing and holding period:** this is a concentrated, long-horizon (3–5 year) fundamental position, not a trade — Fisher gives no per-position sizing formula, but implicitly expects a handful of high-conviction names rather than a diversified basket. Hold as long as the PSR stays low and the Super Company traits persist.

## Risk

Risk control happens before and during the position, not through a stop-loss:
- **Before buying**: require free working capital covering at least five years of the company's worst plausible losses and at least three years of negative cash flow; require debt under 40% of total assets; require management stock ownership of at least 10x its combined salary (an alignment check); prefer top-tier-audited financials; treat concentrated "controlling interest" ownership as a factor requiring extra scrutiny of the individuals involved, since no board can remove an entrenched controlling owner who underperforms.
- **While holding**: monitor whether the company still displays the Step 1 qualitative traits; a loss of any of them (e.g., a competitor establishing a genuine unfair advantage) is a reason to exit regardless of PSR.

## Caveats

There is no stop-loss, no position-sizing percentage, and no diversification rule anywhere in the system — Fisher relies entirely on buying quality cheaply and holding. The numeric thresholds (0.75, 1.5, 3.0, 5–10, 15) are calibrated to early-1980s valuation levels and inflation; Fisher states explicitly that they must be scaled to company size and the inflation regime, so applying them unadjusted decades later requires judgment. The PRR leg only applies to technology/R&D-intensive companies. The whole system depends on getting the qualitative Super Company assessment right first — the ratios alone, on a mediocre business, will not produce a Super Stock.
