## What it is

Gary Smith's core method for trading (mostly no-load) mutual funds, and by extension stocks or index shares, by reacting to a handful of recurring multi-day and intraday momentum patterns rather than forecasting or using lagging technical indicators. It is discretionary rather than mechanical — patterns are confirmed against sentiment/breadth indicators before being acted on — but the entry triggers, position-building increments, and exit thresholds are stated as specific numbers throughout the book (see [[gary-smith-how-i-trade-living]]). It produced real, audited returns of 40.55% (1996), 46.88% (1997), and 84.90% (1998) in Smith's tracked INVESCO fund account, against 23.25%/33.55%/28.56% for the S&P 500 over the same years.

## Rules

**Entry pattern 1 — V-bottom reversal.** The Dow trades down at least 0.75% intraday, then reverses to close near unchanged or higher. Only actionable after a prior down day or existing downtrend; ignore V-bottoms occurring after a strong up day. The later in the session the reversal occurs, the stronger the signal.

**Entry pattern 2 — late-day upside surge.** A choppy, range-bound session rallies in its final 2-2.5 hours to close at least 0.50% above the prior day's close. Same context filter as the V-bottom: only significant after weakness, not after a strong prior advance.

**Entry pattern 3 — extreme momentum day.** A day of price action (breadth, point change) more extreme than anything seen in the prior several months; treated as likely to be followed by continuation in the same direction over the following days.

**Entry pattern 4 — Friday-to-Monday continuation / momentum break.** Strong Friday action is expected to carry into Monday (or Tuesday after a holiday), and vice versa for weak Fridays. When the expected carryover fails to appear (a "momentum break"), treat it as a signal of a short-term trend change and trade in the new direction.

**Entry pattern 5 — index divergence.** Compare the Dow, cash S&P, and Nasdaq 100/Composite (or Russell 2000 for small-cap rotation) using a rolling point-ratio rule of thumb (recompute periodically, e.g., Dow close ÷ Nasdaq 100 close, rounded). When one index moves proportionally far more than the ratio implies, trade in the direction of the stronger index; wait until at least mid-morning before trusting the divergence, and treat it as informative for the next 1-2 hours, not the full day.

**Sector/vehicle selection.** After a V-bottom or late-day surge, Smith's own experience is that the technology sector shows the most next-day follow-through; select the fund or index vehicle accordingly rather than defaulting to a broad index fund.

**Seasonal overlays (used to pre-position or add conviction, not as standalone signals):**
- January Barometer: the sector/fund with the strongest percentage gain over the first 5-7 trading days of January tends to lead for the next 2-6 months.
- Thanksgiving-to-January seasonality: buy a fund at/near 52-week highs with strong momentum over the prior 4 weeks on the Friday before Thanksgiving; this period has historically accounted for ~40% of the market's total gain since 1928.
- New-fund effect: buy newly launched funds (little-publicized, from families with a history of strong new-fund performance) as soon as available, or within the first 1-2 weeks if they show early momentum; exit within 3 trading days if a new fund shows no follow-through.
- Small-cap divergence: after an initial large-cap rally (typically following a 7%+ Dow/S&P decline), rotate into small-cap funds on the first clear day the Russell 2000 outperforms the Dow/S&P.

**Position sizing on entry.** Risk no more than 1-2% of the position's value on the initial purchase (i.e., use a mental/actual stop 1-2% below entry). Build the full position in increments — typically 25-33% of the intended size per addition — adding only as the position continues moving favorably (a scale-up), never averaging down.

**Exit rule 1 — tight-rising-channel stop.** Once a position is established and trending, hold as long as it does not react more than 3-4% from its most recent high. A 3-4% reaction is treated as a sign the advance has run its course.

**Exit rule 2 — 1% true selling day.** After at least two weeks of rising prices, if the Dow, S&P, Nasdaq 100, and Russell 2000 all close down 1% or more on the same day (tolerance: one index down only 0.75-1% if the other three are down 1.5-2%+), sell 25-50% of the position immediately; sell the remainder if selling continues the next session or a second true selling day occurs within the following week.

**Exit rule 3 — pre-emptive tightening.** If the overall market registers a "1% true selling day" before a specific fund position has itself reacted 3-4%, that market-wide signal overrides the individual fund's channel and triggers a partial (33-50%) exit regardless.

**Re-entry.** If a true-selling-day exit turns out to be a false signal (no carryover selling the next session, especially following a Friday-to-Monday continuation setup), re-enter promptly — Smith stresses that the ability to reverse course and re-establish a position quickly is a trained discipline, not a natural instinct.

## Risk

Per-position initial risk is capped at 1-2% of the position, but because positions are scaled up aggressively as they move favorably, realized dollar risk on a large, mature position is materially larger than the entry-stage percentage suggests — the 3-4% trailing-channel exit is the operative risk control on winners, not the original 1-2%. The system's least reliable signal (the 1% true selling day) is also its primary aggregate-market exit, so false signals are expected and must be met with fast re-entry rather than a switch to a defensive stance; failure to re-enter after a false signal is presented as the method's biggest source of opportunity cost. The strategy assumes access to switch between funds without redemption fees or meaningful settlement delay — fund families that restrict frequent trading materially degrade the exit-rule timing this system depends on.

## Caveats

The rules are pattern-recognition heuristics confirmed by discretionary judgment (index ratios are described as "rules of thumb" recalculated periodically, not fixed constants), not a mechanical, backtestable system — Smith explicitly says his own attempts to codify this style into an automated system failed. The fund-switching mechanics (no redemption fees, orders accepted to the 4:00 p.m. close, next-day execution) reflect late-1990s mutual fund industry practice; many fund families have since imposed redemption fees or short-term trading restrictions that would materially impair this system's exit speed. Seasonal statistics (January Barometer, Thanksgiving effect, new-fund effect) are based on the author's personal observation and a handful of third-party studies through the late 1990s, not an independent, updated backtest.
