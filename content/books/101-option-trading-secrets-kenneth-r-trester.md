## Overview

Kenneth Trester, an option newsletter writer since 1973, distills three decades of option trading into 101 short numbered "secrets" grouped into ten sections (prediction, buying, writing, analysis, spreading, tactics, tips, resources, power plays) plus a closing tutorial on option basics. Each secret is a one-to-four page vignette combining a rule of thumb with a real trade example (often from his own newsletters, The Complete Option Report and Ultimate Option Strategies). The book is a checklist-style companion rather than a systematic textbook — it assumes the reader already knows what a call and put are (the tutorial at the back covers that) and instead focuses on the practical judgment calls that separate consistent option traders from gamblers.

## Core thesis

Markets are effectively random and unpredictable, so an option trader's edge does not come from forecasting direction but from mathematical and statistical advantages: buying underpriced options, writing overpriced ones, controlling position size, minimizing slippage and commissions, and always using a hard stop-loss. Trester's recurring refrain is "find your EDGE" — a demonstrable statistical, mathematical or informational advantage — before entering any trade, and to design every strategy so it can win even if the trader is right about market direction only 60% of the time or less.

## Key concepts

- **The 60% Rule** — assume you will be right about market direction at most 60% of the time; design strategies that profit even under that constraint rather than trusting conviction.
- **Regression back to the mean** — extreme results (hot streaks, high implied volatility, big gains) tend to revert toward long-run averages; used to fade extremes in both markets and one's own trading.
- **Inertia** — the tendency of option buyers to freeze and not act on a stop-loss or profit target; described as the option buyer's greatest enemy because, unlike stock, an expiring option gives no second chance.
- **The Hot Seat** — the danger zone an option writer occupies while a position is open; the goal is to exit as fast as possible once most of the premium is captured or the underlying scenario changes.
- **Profit Box** — a pre-defined price range (roughly a 3-week window) for a long option position; exit immediately if the underlying leaves the box.
- **Delta** — the rate of change of option price per 1-point move in the underlying; buyers want high delta, writers want low delta.
- **Implied vs. historical volatility** — implied volatility (back-solved from the option's market price) compared to historical (statistical) volatility signals whether an option is over- or underpriced.
- **Alligator spreads** — multi-leg spreads (e.g., butterflies) with three or more entry/exit legs where commissions and slippage erode the edge; Trester advises avoiding any spread needing more than two trades to enter.
- **Sunk cost** — the amount already paid for a position should be ignored; the only question is whether you'd buy/hold at today's price.
- **Surprise volatility / chaos theory** — markets move via unpredictable jumps (crashes, takeovers, terror events) rather than smooth trends; this favors cheap option buyers and threatens naked writers.
- **Probability of profit / simulation** — software-based Monte Carlo-style simulation of the underlying's path used to estimate the odds of hitting a stop-loss or profit target before expiration, as distinct from a plain Black-Scholes probability-at-expiration calculator.

## Rules and setups

1. **Cheap option buying** — cap cost at 2 ($200) per stock option and $400 per futures/commodity option; buy close-to-the-money strikes with as much time to expiration as possible; target at least a 20% probability of profit; scale in over multiple small buys (max 5% of the option portfolio per position) and scale out in tranches as the position doubles, triples, etc.
2. **Never plunge** — never commit a large single bet on a "sure thing"; spread purchases over time and across positions.
3. **Debit spreads (reduce cost of buying)** — buy a close-to-the-money option, sell a further out-of-the-money option; target maximum profit of at least 200% (stocks/indexes) or 300% (commodities); don't sell the short leg for less than 0.4 (stocks) or $100 (commodities); target at least 35% probability of profit; use a spread limit order, not more than three legs.
4. **Option writing defense (10 steps)** — use stop-losses; enter only high-probability trades; write only short-dated options (under 1 month, never more than 2 months); write only overvalued options; cover when most premium is captured; never fear taking a loss; keep positions small; exit when uncomfortable; exit when the underlying trend changes; diversify. Stop-loss discipline and small position size are called the two most important.
5. **Covered call writing** — write out-of-the-money calls against owned stock at a target sale price; set a stop near the strike and roll further out-of-the-money if triggered; avoid covered writing on stocks in strong uptrends unless believed to be topping.
6. **Naked put writing to buy stock** — write puts only on stock you want to own at the strike; use a mental (not hard) stop so a gap below strike results in taking stock rather than panic-covering; keep enough capital on hand to accept assignment. Framed as a "win-win": either keep the premium or acquire the stock below today's price.
7. **Index credit spreads** — sell a far out-of-the-money index option, buy another 5–15 points further out-of-the-money; require a minimum credit of 0.45; enter with less than 3 weeks to expiration; set a stop-loss out-of-the-money from the written strike with roughly an 85% probability (via simulation) of not being touched; exit early if the spread narrows for a good profit or if the market trend turns against the position.
8. **Diagonal spread ("credit time spread")** — sell a near-term option 3–5 points out-of-the-money with under 2 months to expiration; buy a longer-dated option (expiring 1+ month later) 2.5–5 points further out-of-the-money; enter only for a net credit (or minimal debit); set a stop slightly in-the-money of the short strike; close at expiration of the short leg or when it loses most of its value, but consider holding the remaining long option as a "free" kicker. Trester reports 15 of 18 such spreads (83%) profitable in a 2002 sample.
9. **Long-term debit spread ("the secret surrogate")** — using LEAPS, buy an in- or near-the-money long-dated call and sell a far out-of-the-money longer-dated call at least 10 strike points away; target a maximum profit of 400%+ and, via simulation, an 80%+ chance of the spread doubling in value at some point before expiration; take partial profits at a double, trail a stop on the remainder.
10. **Ratio hedge** — buy the underlying (or a long-dated option) and sell two or more overpriced short options/warrants against it to collect enough premium to offset most or all of the underlying's cost, leaving one unit naked; requires simulation of the naked upside stop-loss point; explicitly a high-risk strategy for experienced naked writers only.
11. **Trading cost control** — keep commissions under $3–5 per option; avoid legging into fast-moving markets (prefer spread orders); test the market with a limit price between bid/ask before paying the spread; minimize total trade count since slippage compounds with each leg.

## Risk and money management

Trester's money-management rules are threaded throughout rather than centralized: cap any single option-buying position at under 5% of the option-buying portfolio; never invest more than 5–10% of a total portfolio in speculative "fun money" option buying; diversify option-buying purchases over roughly a 2-year horizon and across multiple underlyings; treat option-buying capital as money you can genuinely afford to lose entirely, since a large share of individual positions (his own 1980s newsletter record shows only ~20% of buy recommendations profitable despite 1500%+ portfolio returns in some years). For writers, a stop-loss and small position sizing are non-negotiable; naked and ratio strategies carry theoretically unlimited risk and are reserved for capital the trader can afford to lose entirely. He also recommends a standing "put insurance" allocation of cheap out-of-the-money puts as portfolio insurance, sized as a small percentage of the total portfolio, and a broader asset-allocation pyramid (cash/bonds base, ~5–10% hard assets, then stock/options, with aggressive spreads and naked writing only at the top).

## Psychology and discipline

Central psychological themes: distrust of gurus and predictive "crystal balls" (the book repeatedly argues markets are close to random and even skilled analysts have long cold streaks); acceptance of long losing streaks as statistically normal even with a real edge; the danger of sunk-cost thinking and inertia in both cutting losses and taking profits; discipline to always honor stop-losses without second-guessing after the fact; annual review of one's own trading track record to identify and correct recurring mistakes; and treating option-buying capital explicitly as "fun money" to remove the emotional weight that causes hesitation.

## Strengths and caveats

Strong on concrete, numbered, back-tested-sounding rules with real trade tickets (strikes, premiums, dates), which makes several strategies (index credit spread, diagonal spread, surrogate spread) directly codeable. Written in 2004, it is dated in places: pre-decimalization fraction pricing appears alongside decimal examples, recommended tools (Option Master®, Palm handhelds, dial-up-era "internet trading" framing) and named services (ivolatility.com, options-inc.com) are obsolete, and commission/slippage figures ($3–5/contract, 0.10–0.15 price improvement) reflect early-2000s retail costs, far higher than modern electronic markets. Several "rules" (e.g., 96% or 80% probability of profit) are cited from the author's own simulations without independent verification, and anecdote-driven examples (single named trades) are used to support general rules — a pattern for which quantitative traders should apply skepticism. The book does not cover the option Greeks beyond delta in any depth, nor does it address tax treatment, margin mechanics in detail, or modern algorithmic/HFT market structure that has changed spread dynamics.

## Who should read it

Retail traders who already understand basic option mechanics and want a checklist of practical, rule-based tactics for buying, writing and spreading options, especially those interested in defined-risk income strategies (credit spreads, diagonals) or disciplined speculative option buying. Not a primer for absolute beginners (though the tutorial section helps) and not a quantitative or academic treatment of option pricing.
