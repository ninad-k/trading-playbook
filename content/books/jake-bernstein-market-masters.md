## Overview

Market Masters is a collection of first-person interviews Jake Bernstein conducted with eight recognized futures traders and market analysts: George Angell (day trader/technical analyst), Gerald Appel (creator of MACD), Bruce Babcock (systems developer and publisher of the Commodity Traders Consumer Report), George Lane (creator of Stochastics), Conrad Leslie (grain-market forecaster), Robert Prechter (Elliott Wave analyst), Welles Wilder (creator of RSI and Parabolic), and Larry Williams (short-term trader). Each chapter follows the same structure: a biographical sketch, a transcribed question-and-answer interview, and Bernstein's own analysis of what the trader's answers reveal. The book closes with two synthesis chapters — "The Keys to Success as a Trader" and "The Psychology of Trading" — that distill the recurring themes across all eight interviews into a single set of practical habits.

## Core thesis

Bernstein argues that successful futures trading is not primarily a function of having a superior system or superior market insight; it is a function of psychology and discipline applied consistently over time. Across all eight interviews, the same handful of traits recur regardless of the trader's specialty (technical, fundamental, or discretionary): the ability to take small losses quickly and without emotional damage, a willingness to follow a defined plan rather than react to news or emotion, and the capacity to keep trading the next day regardless of yesterday's result. Bernstein's explicit claim is that these keys to success "are not only attainable; they can be learned and taught," and that reading how eight different, very successful traders independently arrived at the same conclusions is itself the teaching method.

## Key concepts

- **Discipline (component behaviors)** — Bernstein decomposes discipline into several distinct skills: sticking with a plan, knowing when to abandon a plan, giving a position enough time to work, re-entering the market after a loss, sizing positions reasonably to avoid overtrading, and returning to trade again the next day regardless of the prior day's outcome.
- **Keys to success (recurring habits)** — a checklist repeated in different words by nearly every interviewee: get a method and prove it works before risking real money, do your homework daily, keep a trading diary, monitor your performance continuously, and stay out when signals are unclear ("when in doubt, stay out").
- **Coping with losses** — several traders (Angell, Leslie, Wilder) frame losses as a normal cost of doing business rather than a personal failure; Wilder explicitly compares losses to a business's operating expenses.
- **Good system vs. trading skill** — a recurring debate in the interviews over what fraction of results comes from the system versus the trader's own discipline; Wilder estimates that a fully mechanical system requires less trader skill, while a discretionary/subjective system can require up to 75% skill from the trader.
- **Contrarian/news-fade psychology** — Bernstein's own synthesis chapter advises buying on rumor and selling on news, treating brief intraday price surges or drops (often driven by rumor or a single large order) as exit opportunities rather than reasons to hold for more.
- **75/25 technical-fundamental mix** — Conrad Leslie's own blend: roughly three-quarters technical analysis to one-quarter fundamental input, layered with a preference for simple systems over complex ones.
- **"Second mouse" mentality (implicit across interviews)** — several traders describe waiting for confirmation rather than being first into a move, valuing a validated signal over being early.

## Rules and setups

The book is psychology- and habit-focused rather than a mechanical trading system, so there is no single entry/exit/stop specification. The clearest concrete, numbered guidance recoverable from the sampled pages is Conrad Leslie's personal rule list and Bernstein's own closing "keys to success" checklist:

1. **Leslie's trading rules (partial list, chapter 7):** keep up-to-date price charts; never establish a position until you see potential for a large profit versus a small loss; be prepared to make several probing attempts to board a major price move and to take small losses along the way; do not trade more than four or five commodities at once; avoid commodities with vague or hard-to-obtain statistical information (prefer U.S. commodities); never take an individual or total position larger than the risk or the opportunity justifies; restrict trading to commodities that have consistently returned profits for you; go with the market as it makes new highs or new lows.
2. **Bruce Babcock's discipline-vs-system trade-off:** if forced to choose only one, choose discipline to keep losses small over a good system with no discipline — "having the system will be meaningless" without the discipline to follow it. Trading without fixed rules is stated to reduce the odds of multi-year profitability.
3. **Welles Wilder's stop rule:** decide before entering a trade exactly how much you are willing to lose on it; place the stop as soon as the entry order is filled; move the stop only in favor of the trade, never against it; his stated commitment is to "never, ever take a large loss."
4. **Bernstein's "keys to success" checklist (chapter 11):** keep a trading diary reviewed at the start and end of each day; do market homework daily even with a mechanical system; if you must watch the market and cannot, either close the position or leave a stop-loss order with your broker rather than trying to stay in touch by phone; when in doubt, stay out; monitor performance on a trade-by-trade and day-by-day basis using an accounting program, tracking average winner size against average loser size.
5. **Bernstein's news/price-surge rule:** use a trailing stop-loss (raised or lowered as price moves) once a position is in profit, taking advantage of brief intraday price surges to exit rather than assuming they will extend further.
6. **George Lane's profit-taking rule:** take money out of the market whenever trading is profitable, both to reward good performance and to build a cash reserve against a future drawdown that might require adding funds to the account.

No specific entry signal, timeframe, or position-sizing percentage common to all eight traders is given; each interviewee's numeric preferences (where stated) apply only to that individual's own practice.

## Risk and money management

Risk management in this book is presented as a psychological discipline rather than a formula. The strongest common thread across interviews is "keep losses small, take them quickly" (Angell, Appel, Babcock, Wilder, Leslie all state versions of this). Specific numeric position-sizing rules (e.g., percent-of-equity risk per trade) are not given in the sampled pages; Leslie's rule against "an individual or total position larger than the risk...justifies" and his four-or-five-commodity concentration limit are the closest the sample comes to quantified sizing guidance. Bernstein's own advice layers a trailing stop-loss on profitable positions and a pre-defined maximum loss decided before entry (echoing Wilder). No margin, percent-of-account, or dollar-risk figures are given as universal defaults.

## Psychology and discipline

This is the book's core subject. Bernstein devotes a full chapter ("The Psychology of Trading") to the idea that discipline, not analytical skill, separates winners from losers, and states plainly that futures trading is "more likely a function of not doing than doing" — avoiding the classic mistakes (riding losses, overtrading, abandoning a working system out of impatience) matters more than any edge-seeking behavior. Fear is identified by multiple interviewees (notably Larry Williams) as the primary inhibiting force, arising from past losing experiences; the antidote offered is research-driven confidence rather than willpower alone. Bruce Babcock frames isolation and an unemotional, business-like posture toward losses as essential, while Wilder and Prechter both stress that controlling losses — not achieving spectacular wins — is the most significant determinant of long-run success. The book also flags "fear of profit" indirectly through several traders' comments about cutting winners short out of a need to feel like a winner (locking in small gains) while letting losers run.

- Ch 10 — Larry Williams: trading purely for profit, the role of fear as an inhibitor, and the value of research/education before risking capital.
- Ch 11 — The Keys to Success as a Trader: Bernstein's synthesized checklist (diary, homework, performance monitoring, "when in doubt, stay out").

## Strengths and caveats

The interview format is a genuine strength: readers get first-person, sometimes blunt answers (Babcock in particular) rather than a single author's abstracted theory, and Bernstein's after-each-interview analysis links the individual's answers back to a common framework. The book is explicitly a 1994 snapshot of the pit-trading era — all interviewees are futures/commodities specialists, several tools discussed (Wilder's Delta Society/Delta Phenomenon, floor-based order flow) are dated or discontinued, and there is no discussion of electronic execution, algorithmic trading, or modern market structure. No aggregate statistics, backtests, or verifiable track records are presented for any interviewee's claims; the "keys to success" are qualitative and drawn from a small, self-selected sample of successful traders (survivorship bias is not addressed). These notes are based on a sampled set of OCR'd pages (the source is a scanned PDF where only front matter plus evenly spaced pages were digitized); the George Lane chapter content and the full text of chapters between sampled pages were not recoverable, and no numbers should be assumed for gaps not shown above.

## Who should read it

Traders early in their development who want to see recurring psychological and discipline patterns validated across several independently successful, differently-styled traders (technical, fundamental, mechanical, discretionary) rather than a single author's prescription. Less useful for traders seeking a specific, ready-to-code trading system, current market-structure context, or quantitative backtesting.
