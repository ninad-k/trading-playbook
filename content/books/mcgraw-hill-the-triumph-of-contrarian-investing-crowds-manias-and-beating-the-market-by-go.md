## Overview

Ned Davis, founder of Ned Davis Research (NDR), makes the case that markets are driven as much by crowd psychology as by fundamentals, and that measuring crowd sentiment quantitatively — rather than trying to time markets from economics alone — gives a repeatable contrarian edge. The book moves from social-psychology experiments on conformity, through a historical tour of manias and panics (South Sea Bubble, Mississippi Scheme, Russia's MMM, the 1929 crash, the NASDAQ bubble), to the operational core: a chapter of NDR's actual sentiment indicators with the specific numeric levels the firm treats as "excessive optimism" or "excessive pessimism," plus a long addendum applying the same valuation-based sentiment logic stock-by-stock.

## Core thesis

Financial markets are not driven purely by rational appraisal of fundamentals; they are the aggregate output of human psychology operating in a crowd, and crowds reliably overshoot at extremes because of well-documented biases (conformity, anchoring, loss aversion). Because these overshoots are measurable — via surveys of investor and advisor sentiment, actual positioning data (fund flows, futures positioning), and valuation extremes — a disciplined investor can systematically buy when the crowd is provably too pessimistic and sell (or reduce exposure) when it is provably too optimistic, and this has historically outperformed simply following the crowd's own consensus.

## Key concepts

- **Reflecting barriers theory (Cootner)** — professionals have good information and are rarely all wrong together; nonprofessionals dominate at extremes and, lacking reliable valuation judgment, push price to levels that reverse — the theoretical basis for treating sentiment extremes as reversal signals.
- **Anchoring to the crowd (Sherif's autokinetic experiment, 1935)** — subjects' private judgments about an ambiguous stimulus converged toward the first answer given once judged in a group, despite no rational reason to change; used as evidence that market opinions similarly anchor to whatever view is most repeated in the media.
- **Group pressure / conformity (Asch's line experiment)** — roughly two-thirds of subjects gave an answer they knew or suspected was wrong when a group of confederates unanimously gave that wrong answer first; framed as the mechanism by which "everyone getting rich" during a bubble suppresses individual skepticism.
- **Loss aversion / regret theory (Kahneman & Tversky)** — subjects preferred a 75% chance of losing $10,000 (expected value -$7,500) over a certain $7,500 loss with the same expected value, and preferred a certain $2,400 gain over a 25% chance of $10,000 (higher expected value) — evidence that people weight avoiding regret differently than maximizing expected wealth, and that losses are felt roughly 2.5x as strongly as equivalent gains.
- **NDR Crowd Sentiment Poll** — a composite of seven sentiment indicators (surveys of individual investors, newsletter writers, option traders, etc.) into one bullish-percentage reading used to flag sentiment extremes.
- **Dynamic sentiment brackets** — adjusting the bullish/bearish threshold that counts as "extreme" based on the concurrent monetary trend (interest rate direction), since a given sentiment reading means something different in a rate-falling versus rate-rising backdrop.
- **Commitments of Traders (COT) positioning** — CFTC-reported net long/short positioning of noncommercial (speculative) futures traders, used as a "what people are actually doing" sentiment proxy rather than a survey of stated opinion.
- **Valuation as a sentiment indicator** — P/E, price/sales, price/cashflow, and similar ratios (both for the index and for individual stocks) reframed not as pure valuation tools but as direct readings of how much optimism or pessimism is priced in, each judged against that security's own historical range rather than an absolute level.
- **Manias and panics as recurring, structurally similar events** — the South Sea Company, John Law's Mississippi Scheme, Russia's 1994 MMM scheme, the 1929 crash, and the 1990s NASDAQ bubble are presented as the same psychological pattern (a compelling narrative, self-reinforcing price gains, public participation reaching saturation, and a triggering event that reverses the crowd) recurring across three centuries.

## Rules and setups

The book supplies specific, back-tested numeric thresholds for a set of named sentiment indicators (methodology detailed in [[mcgraw-hill-the-triumph-of-contrarian-investing-crowds-manias-and-beating-the-market-by-go--ndr-crowd-sentiment-indicators]]):

1. **AAII individual investor sentiment**: extreme pessimism below 44% bulls (of bulls+bears); potentially excessive optimism above 61% bulls; historically, S&P 500 forward returns are well above average below 44% and roughly flat above 61%.
2. **Investors Intelligence advisor sentiment, dynamic brackets**: when short-term rates are falling, extreme optimism = bulls/(bulls+bears) above 81%, extreme pessimism = below 51%; when rates are rising, the extreme-optimism bar drops to 58% and extreme-pessimism rises to 31%. A hypothetical buy-at-pessimism/sell-at-optimism strategy using these dynamic levels showed no losing trades and outperformed buy-and-hold over 1968-2003 (hypothetical/backtested).
3. **Rydex bull/bear fund asset ratio**: excessive optimism above 82.5% of combined bull+bear fund assets in bull funds; excessive pessimism below 51%.
4. **COT Speculator Index (S&P 500 futures)**: net noncommercial position as a percent of its trailing 78-week range; readings below 40% (speculators net short relative to their own recent range) preceded above-average S&P gains; readings above 60% preceded S&P losses on average.
5. **NDR Crowd Sentiment Poll (composite of seven indicators)**: extreme optimism when the reading rises above 61.5%; extreme pessimism when it declines below 55.5%, with a required 10-percentage-point reversal off the extreme before a turning point is confirmed (this is a hindsight-confirmed marker, not a real-time trading signal, per the book's own caveat).
6. **Valuation extremes (S&P 500 P/E)**: median forward S&P returns are markedly worse following P/E readings above 20.2 and markedly better following readings below 9.3, across horizons from 3 to 24 months.
7. **Bond yield/earnings yield ratio**: ratio of the 10-year Treasury yield to the S&P 500 earnings yield above 1.45 favors bonds over stocks going forward; below 1.2 favors stocks over bonds.
8. **Individual-stock valuation bands**: for a given stock, test price against each of 7 fundamental variables (dividends, sales, earnings, cashflow, 20-quarter average earnings, 20-quarter average cashflow, book value per share) to find which ratio and threshold have historically marked "overconfident" versus "overly fearful" sentiment for that specific name; combine with a 39-week moving average as a trend filter — e.g., buy only once price crosses back above its 39-week average after showing excessive-pessimism valuation, and look to sell once price falls below its 39-week average after showing excessive-optimism valuation.

## Risk and money management

The book does not present a position-sizing or stop-loss framework; it is an indicator/timing book, not a trade-management book. Its implicit risk control is regime-based: it treats "time spent out of the market" (e.g., switched to cash/commercial paper when sentiment signals extreme optimism) as a way to reduce overall portfolio risk exposure, and repeatedly cautions that sentiment extremes can persist "for frustratingly long periods" before reversing, so acting the moment a threshold is crossed — with no trend confirmation — risks entering or exiting well before the actual turn. The recommended discipline is to pair a sentiment-extreme reading with a price/trend confirmation (such as the 39-week moving average cross used in the individual-stock addendum) rather than acting on sentiment alone.

## Psychology and discipline

The book's real subject is crowd psychology, treated as the mechanism that makes contrarian investing work rather than as an obstacle to be merely managed. It draws a direct line from laboratory conformity experiments (Sherif, Asch) through documented investor sentiment surveys to real market tops and bottoms, arguing that professional analysts are not immune — Wall Street strategist consensus forecasts (reproduced for 2000 and 2001) missed the actual S&P/DJIA close by wide margins both years, and the book uses this to argue that "smart money" herds too. The discipline required of the contrarian investor is explicitly framed as uncomfortable and countercultural: acting against a Bill of Rights-style celebration of individualism versus conformity threads through the book's postscript, and the operational message is that being early and lonely is a structural feature of the strategy, not a bug to be engineered away.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Introduction — the reflecting-barriers theory of price formation; Wall Street strategist forecast failures (2000, 2001); framing of investor expectations as the dominant short-term price driver.
- Ch 2 — Scientific Studies on Crowd Psychology — Sherif's autokinetic-effect anchoring experiment, Asch's conformity experiment, Kahneman & Tversky's loss-aversion and regret-theory experiments.
- Ch 3 — Brief History of Manias and Panics — South Sea Company, Mississippi Scheme, Russia's MMM (1994), the 1929 crash, and the NASDAQ bubble, presented as structurally similar episodes.
- Ch 4 — Headlines and Cover Stories — using mass-media cover stories and headlines as a contrarian sentiment marker (magazine covers as a lagging, crowd-consensus indicator).
- Ch 5 — Indicators of Crowd Psychology — the book's operational core: AAII, Investors Intelligence, Rydex fund flows, COT positioning, the NDR Crowd Sentiment Poll, P/E and bond/stock relative-valuation indicators, household equity allocation, and investment-club counts, each with stated threshold levels and historical forward-return statistics.
- Ch 6 — Postscript — a personal essay on individualism versus conformity, not investing-specific.
- Addendum — Sentiment on Individual Stocks — applies the same valuation-based sentiment methodology to specific companies (3M, Abbott Labs, AMD, and others), stock by stock, with a 39-week moving-average trend overlay.

## Strengths and caveats

The indicator thresholds are genuinely quantified and mostly given with the historical sample's forward-return statistics attached, which is unusually rigorous for a sentiment/contrarian book of this era. The book is explicit about several limitations worth taking seriously: the NDR Crowd Sentiment Poll's extremes are confirmed only in hindsight (arrows placed after a 10-point reversal), so it is not directly a real-time trading signal as presented; the individual-stock valuation bands are described by the authors themselves as hypothetical and not intended as a standalone trading system; and threshold levels for advisor and investor sentiment surveys are explicitly stated to drift over time (hence the "dynamic brackets" adjustment for interest-rate regime), meaning fixed thresholds from 2003 data should not be applied unadjusted today. As with any indicator book built on a specific historical sample (here mostly 1968-2003 U.S. equity data), regime change, changed survey methodologies, and the rise of new instruments (ETFs, options flow) since publication limit how directly the exact numeric levels transfer to current markets — the framework (measure sentiment, compare to that series' own historical extremes, require trend confirmation) is more durable than any single number in the book.

## Who should read it

Investors and analysts interested in a systematic, indicator-driven approach to contrarian investing — as opposed to a purely narrative "buy when there's blood in the streets" philosophy — will get genuine, checkable thresholds here. It complements value-investing contrarian books (which focus on individual security selection) by focusing instead on market-wide and stock-specific sentiment timing. Less useful for a trader wanting execution mechanics (entries, stops, sizing), which the book does not cover.
