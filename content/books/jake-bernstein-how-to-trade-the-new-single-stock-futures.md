## Summary

Bernstein introduces single-stock futures (SSFs) as a leveraged bridge between equity and futures trading. He covers contract mechanics, fundamentals, technical indicators, spreads, order placement, seasonal tendencies, psychology, and day trading. The reviewed chapters stress matching order type to strategy, checking liquidity, and controlling leverage. Concrete methods include gap reversals and a 28-period momentum/moving-average crossover (PDF pp. 143-159).

## Key points

- An SSF transfers the price exposure of a fixed share quantity without immediate stock ownership.
- Leverage amplifies gains and losses; the book's historical comparisons use margin rules and exchanges from the market's launch era.
- Breakout systems use stop orders beyond support or resistance; pullback systems use limit orders at support or resistance.
- Bracketed breakout orders require immediate cancellation or repurposing of the unfilled side (PDF p. 143).
- A stock position can be hedged with an offsetting SSF in the same share amount (PDF p. 147).
- Thin contracts make market orders and stop fills hazardous.
- Gap reversals seek the retracement of emotionally driven opens.

## Actionable rules

1. For a gap-up reversal, require an open at least 5% of the prior day's range above its high; sell when price crosses back below that high. Reverse the logic for a gap-down buy. Exit at the close, use a dollar stop, and trail favorable moves (PDF p. 155). The printed gap-down bullet says “sell short” despite describing an upside penetration; context indicates this is likely an editing error.
2. For momentum timing, calculate 28-period momentum and its 28-period moving average. Buy when momentum crosses above its average and sell when it crosses below (PDF p. 159).
3. Avoid market orders in thin SSFs, record all live orders, and maintain a backup execution channel (PDF pp. 141-148).

## Chapter structure recovered from the scan

The book moves from context to mechanics to method: the end of the 1990s bull market (Ch. 1), histories of futures and of stock trading (Ch. 2-3), the basics of each (Ch. 4), the "synthesis" that single stock futures represent (Ch. 5), fundamental aspects (Ch. 6), technical aspects (Ch. 7), the major categories of technical indicators and trading methods (Ch. 8), spread trading in single stock futures (Ch. 9) and effective order placement (Ch. 10), before the closing chapters on brokers, risk and outlook (PDF pp. 7-9).

## Caveats

The exchange list, regulation, margin examples, and liquidity conditions date from the 2002–2003 launch of U.S. SSFs. The book supplies illustrative charts rather than robust out-of-sample tests.

## Who it is for

Futures-literate traders studying the mechanics and historical strategies of single-stock futures.
