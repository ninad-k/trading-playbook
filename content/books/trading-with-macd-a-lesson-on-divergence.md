## Summary

A trading-lesson newsletter piece teaching MACD (histogram) divergence as a way to catch a trend reversal near its beginning rather than after a lagging indicator confirms it. The author walks through eight real futures-market chart examples (Feeder Cattle, Lumber, Lean Hogs, Cotton, Orange Juice, Canadian Dollar, Copper, Japanese Yen weekly, CRB Index weekly) to teach the pattern and a specific trigger rule for turning a divergence "condition" into an actual entry signal.

## Key points

- Divergence occurs when price and an indicator that normally move together start moving in opposite directions; it signals a possible trend change, not a definite signal on its own.
- Bullish divergence: price makes a new low while the MACD histogram's low is higher than its prior low (bears losing momentum).
- Bearish divergence: price makes a new high while the MACD histogram's high is lower than its prior high (bulls losing momentum).
- MACD is preferred over simple moving averages, momentum, or Rate of Change because its exponential smoothing prevents a single day's price swing from distorting the divergence read; the histogram form makes divergence easier to see visually.
- Entry trigger rule: after marking the divergence's two reference points, draw a horizontal line at the MACD's extreme value between them; the signal fires only when MACD later breaks through that line (not simply on the price/indicator divergence itself).
- A bearish divergence pattern is invalidated if MACD rises above the level it held at the second reference point before the sell signal triggers (and the mirror rule applies to bullish setups).
- The author's subjective observation: the longer the time span between the two divergence reference points, the larger the resulting price move tends to be (not statistically quantified).
- The same method is applied on weekly charts to catch multi-year trend changes (examples: Japanese Yen and CRB Index bottoms).

## Actionable rules

1. Identify a divergence by comparing the MACD histogram's extreme (high or low) at one point to price's extreme at another point in the same direction of travel.
2. Draw a horizontal line at the MACD level of the confirming extreme point (the low between A and B for a bullish setup, the high between A and B for a bearish setup).
3. Enter only when MACD breaks through that horizontal line — do not enter on the divergence condition alone.
4. Place the stop beyond the opposite extreme point (e.g., above point B's high for a bearish/sell setup).
5. Cancel/invalidate the setup if MACD moves back beyond the reference extreme before the entry trigger fires.
6. Apply the identical process on weekly charts to trade longer-term trend changes.

## Caveats

This is an informal newsletter lesson built entirely from historical chart examples with no formal backtest, win rate, or sample size — the "longer time span implies bigger move" observation is explicitly labeled subjective and unquantified by the author. All examples are futures markets from around 2001; no equity or forex examples are given.

## Who it is for

Traders who already know how MACD is calculated and want a concrete, chart-based method for turning MACD divergence into an actual entry/exit rule rather than treating divergence as a vague warning sign.
