## Summary

Jake Bernstein combines personal-finance preparation with a market-timing framework. The General Investment Model organizes decisions into setup, trigger, and follow-through (STF): a favorable background is not enough; price must confirm before capital is committed, and subsequent behavior must validate the position. The book ranges widely across goals, household finances, psychology, small-account strategies, stocks, funds, futures, real estate, and collectibles. Its most concrete market tool is price momentum and divergence (PDF pp. 108-123).

## Key points

- Financial goals, available capital, household agreement, and risk tolerance precede product selection.
- A setup identifies potential; a trigger authorizes action; follow-through determines whether to stay.
- Forecast accuracy alone is misleading. A 30%-accurate method can outperform a 90%-accurate one when winners are much larger than losers (PDF pp. 117-118).
- Momentum is treated as a fuel gauge for price trends.
- Rising price with falling momentum warns of a possible top; falling price with rising momentum warns of a possible bottom (PDF pp. 119-123).
- Divergence is a setup, not an entry by itself.
- Diversification and predefined exits matter more than tips or persuasive narratives.

## Actionable rules

1. Define the setup, objective trigger, and required follow-through before committing money.
2. Use a consistent momentum convention: current close minus the close X periods earlier. The source's prose, subtraction order, and arithmetic on PDF pp. 121-123 conflict; this is an editorial correction matching its intended positive-for-rising-price interpretation. Its long-term example uses 28 weeks.
3. Treat bearish or bullish divergence as an alert and wait for price or momentum to break its trigger level.
4. Judge a method by net dollars, drawdown, and payoff distribution rather than percentage winners.
5. Use dollar or technical stops so one failed idea cannot dominate the portfolio.

## Chapter structure recovered from the scan

Part Two is organised by starting capital rather than by instrument: portfolios for less than $2,500, then $5,000 to $20,000, $10,000 to $25,000, $25,000 to $50,000 and above $50,000, followed by sections on precious metals and on numismatic coins (including guidelines for coin investing and a warning-flavoured treatment of numismatic investment programmes) and a closing section on the "hottest games in town." Part Three looks ahead at emerging opportunities and technological breakthroughs, and closes with a perspective on investor psychology (PDF pp. 7-9, 43-44, 110-112, 151-183).

## Caveats

This partial review covers the central model and momentum chapter, not every asset recommendation. The book's anecdotes and performance expectations are not substitutes for audited evidence, and some products and resources are dated.

## Who it is for

Beginning investors who need a bridge from household planning to rule-based market participation.
