## Overview

Contrarian Investment Strategies: The Next Generation is David Dreman's update to his earlier contrarian-investing books, combining a critique of forecasting-based investment methods (technical analysis, analyst earnings estimates, expert stock-picking) with statistical evidence, drawn from Compustat data spanning 1970–1996, that portfolios of statistically "cheap" stocks — low price-to-earnings, low price-to-cash-flow, low price-to-book-value, and high dividend yield — consistently and significantly outperform both the market and portfolios of the market's most "favored," expensive stocks. The second half of the book grounds this pattern in behavioral psychology (overconfidence, the law of small numbers, investor overreaction) and closes with a history of speculative manias to illustrate the crowd psychology his strategies are designed to exploit.

## Core thesis

Stock prices systematically overreact to both good and bad news because analysts and investors extrapolate a company's recent past too far into the future: "best" stocks (high expected growth, richly priced) are consistently overrated and underperform once reality reverts toward the mean, while "worst" stocks (low expected growth, statistically cheap) are consistently underrated and outperform as their prospects turn out to be less dismal than priced in. Because this reappraisal process is slow (often taking years) and remarkably consistent across more than six decades of market history, an investor can systematically profit from it by buying baskets of statistically cheap, out-of-favor stocks and holding through the market's slow correction of its own overreaction — without needing to forecast which specific company will turn around.

## Key concepts

- **Low P/E strategy** — buying stocks in the cheapest quintile (bottom 20%) of the market by price-to-earnings ratio; Dreman's "core" contrarian strategy because P/E data is the most current and widely available of the four.
- **Low price-to-cash-flow (P/CF) strategy** — buying the cheapest quintile by price relative to cash flow per share; in Dreman's tests, statistically about as strong as low P/E.
- **Low price-to-book-value (P/BV) strategy** — buying the cheapest quintile by price relative to (industry- or market-relative) book value; Dreman notes modern investors should compare book value to industry/market norms rather than to Graham-era absolute book value, since inflation has pushed replacement cost of assets well above balance-sheet values.
- **High dividend yield / low price-to-dividend (P/D) strategy** — buying the highest-yielding quintile; Dreman treats this as a secondary strategy to the other three because its historical outperformance, while still market-beating, is smaller.
- **Investor overreaction** — the behavioral mechanism (rooted in cognitive biases such as overconfidence and the "law of small numbers") that causes favored stocks to be priced for a future that rarely arrives and unfavored stocks to be priced for a future worse than what usually arrives.
- **Earnings-surprise asymmetry** — positive surprises produce only modest price gains for "best" (high-expectation) stocks, because they merely confirm what was already priced in, but produce outsized gains for "worst" (low-expectation) stocks, because the market had priced in continued disappointment.
- **Analyst overconfidence** — cited research shows analysts giving 80% confidence to price forecasts that were only about 60% accurate, and 90%-confidence price ranges that were missed roughly 35% of the time (about 3.5x the expected miss rate).
- **The "five indicators" filter** — beyond the raw valuation quintile, Dreman layers additional company-quality checks before buying (Indicator 5, the only one fully recoverable from the sampled pages: an above-average dividend yield that the company can sustain and grow).
- **Diversification via 16+ stocks** — citing financial research that well-diversified portfolios of as few as 16 stocks can capture roughly 85–90% of the return achievable from the full group they are drawn from.
- **Large-capitalization universe** — the core backtests are run on the Compustat 1500 (large, well-established companies); Dreman notes separately that small-cap contrarian stocks behave somewhat differently and can produce higher (but different-risk) returns.

## Rules and setups

1. **Core low-P/E screen**: rank the investable universe (Compustat 1500-style large caps) by trailing P/E; buy the bottom (cheapest) quintile, avoid the top (most expensive) quintile.
2. **Low-P/CF and low-P/BV screens**: same quintile-ranking approach applied to price-to-cash-flow and price-to-book-value (industry- or market-relative, not raw book value) instead of P/E.
3. **High-yield / low-P/D screen**: rank by dividend yield; buy the highest-yielding quintile, favoring sustainable, growing payouts (Indicator 5) over yield produced by a collapsing share price.
4. **Combined/eclectic approach**: Dreman's own firm uses low P/E as the "core" strategy while actively running the other three opportunistically, since cheap opportunities surface differently across the four measures at different times.
5. **Holding period**: long-horizon — odds of the low-P/E strategy beating the market rise with holding period (roughly 87% of 5-year, 94–95% of 10-year periods in Dreman's sample); avoid quarterly churn, rebalance annually or longer.
6. **Diversification rule**: hold a broadly diversified basket (~16 stocks, per cited research, captures most of the achievable return) rather than concentrating in a handful of "best ideas" within the cheap quintile.
7. **Universe filter**: results apply to large, established companies (Compustat 1500); small-cap/Nasdaq stocks are a separate, differently-behaving regime, not a simple extension of the same screens.

## Risk and money management

Dreman's risk framework is statistical rather than trade-by-trade: the "risk" being managed is the probability of underperforming the market over a given holding period, not a per-position stop-loss. He reports significance testing on the core strategies — low P/E and low P/CF each show only about a 1-in-200 probability of their historical outperformance being due to chance, low P/BV about 1-in-100, while the corresponding "best"/most-expensive quintiles show weaker but still often-significant underperformance (around the 1-in-20, i.e., 95% confidence, threshold). Position-level risk control is handled through diversification (the ~16-stock rule) rather than individual stop-losses, and through explicit warnings against chasing recently "hot" money managers, mutual funds, or narrow specialty indexes with self-serving construction methodologies (Rule 40: avoid small, fast-track mutual funds, whose returns are described as rarely sustainable). No explicit position-sizing percentage (e.g., a "2% rule") or stop-loss mechanism is given; risk management is at the portfolio-construction and behavioral-discipline level, not the individual-trade level.

## Psychology and discipline

Dreman frames investor error as a predictable byproduct of how the mind processes uncertainty, not random noise: professionals are shown, across cited studies (clinical-psychologist prediction research by Paul Meehl, market-forecast overconfidence research by Amos Tversky), to be systematically overconfident and to perform worse than simple statistical baselines despite (or because of) access to more information — a pattern Dreman calls "the floor becomes the ceiling." He warns against being swayed by a manager's or analyst's recent short-term track record without substantiation (Rule 23), against treating an extreme current trend as the new normal rather than reverting toward an average (Rule 27), and argues it is safer to bet on a continuation of predictable crowd psychology than on the specific business prospects of any one company (Rule 28). The discipline required of the contrarian investor is explicitly likened to that of a casino owner: individual periods of underperformance ("cold streaks") must be tolerated without abandoning the strategy, because the edge is statistical and plays out over many holding periods, not every single one.

## Chapter map

- Introduction — the book's aim: a systematic, statistically documented approach to contrarian investing, building on Dreman's earlier books.
- "From Technical Analysis to Astrology" / Why Current Methods Don't Work — critiques technical analysis and the efficient-market hypothesis using market-timer track records.
- "Dangerous Forecasts" / "The Expert Way to Lose Your Savings" — behavioral-psychology research (Herbert Simon, Paul Meehl, Amos Tversky) on the limits of expert and analyst judgment.
- "Nasty Surprises" (Ch. 6) — the Compustat 1500 (1973–1996) study of how "best" vs. "worst" stocks react to earnings surprises.
- "Contrarian Investment Strategies" (Ch. 7) — the core low-P/E, low-P/CF, low-P/BV, high-yield quintile backtests, including bull- and bear-market breakdowns.
- "Boosting Portfolio Profits" (Ch. 8) — the five-indicator quality overlay, significance notes, and diversification/holding-period guidance.
- "A New, Powerful Contrarian Approach" (Ch. 9) — extending contrarian principles internationally, with cautionary case studies of foreign-market bubbles.
- "Profiting from Investor Overreaction" — the behavioral mechanism underlying the strategies' returns (Rules 27–28).
- "Small Stocks, Nasdaq, and Other Market Pitfalls" — cautions on small-cap/Nasdaq investing and unsustainable performance records (Rule 40).
- "Psychology and Markets" — a historical tour of speculative manias (Dutch tulip mania 1637, South Sea Bubble 1720, Mississippi Bubble 1720).
- Appendices and Notes — supporting statistical/methodology detail for the chapter 6–9 studies.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's central claims rest on unusually well-documented statistical backtests (explicit universe, date range, quintile methodology, and significance tests), which is rare among popular investing books and makes the core screens genuinely reproducible. Some content is dated: the underlying study window ends in 1996, pre-dates the late-1990s tech bubble and its aftermath, and several market-structure references (specialist systems, 1990s fund-industry practices) reflect that era. The book is explicitly a psychology-and-statistics case for value investing broadly aligned with, but independently derived from, Benjamin Graham's earlier value-investing framework; readers of both should expect overlapping conclusions (buy statistically cheap stocks) reached via different argument paths (behavioral finance vs. Graham's margin-of-safety accounting). Because this document was OCR'd from only a sample of scanned pages, several supporting details were not recoverable: Indicators 1–4 of the book's five-indicator quality checklist (only Indicator 5, dividend sustainability, appeared in the sampled text), and any explicit sell-discipline or rebalancing-trigger rules beyond the general holding-period guidance above.

## Who should read it

Value-oriented investors who want statistical, behavioral-finance evidence for buying out-of-favor stocks rather than a purely accounting-based (Graham-style) case; useful for anyone building a systematic low-multiple/high-yield screen and wanting the historical base rates and significance levels behind it, though not a source of trade-level risk or position-sizing rules.
