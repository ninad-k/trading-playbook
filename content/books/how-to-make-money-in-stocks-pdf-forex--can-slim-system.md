## What it is

A seven-factor screening and timing system for buying growth stocks near the start of a major price advance, distilled from a statistical study of the biggest stock market winners from 1953 to 1993. The acronym CAN SLIM names each factor: Current quarterly earnings, Annual earnings growth, New product/management/high, Supply and demand, Leader or laggard, Institutional sponsorship, and Market direction. A candidate must satisfy the fundamental factors (C, A) and structural factors (N, S, L, I) simultaneously, be bought at a precise technical entry (a base breakout, or "pivot," on a confirmed general-market uptrend), and then be managed with fixed, numeric sell rules that are followed regardless of the trader's opinion about where the stock will go next.

## Rules

**Screening (all seven factors checked together, not in isolation)**
1. **C — Current earnings.** Require the most recent quarterly EPS up at least 20-50% (ideally more) versus the same quarter one year prior. Reject increases of 8-10% as inadequate. Always compare like quarters year-over-year (e.g., this December quarter vs. last December quarter), never sequential quarters.
2. **A — Annual earnings.** Require compounded annual EPS growth of roughly 25-50% (or higher) over the trailing 3-5 years, with at most one down year that recovers to new highs the following year. Favor an earnings-stability rating below about 20-25 (on a 1-99 scale where lower is more stable).
3. **N — New catalyst plus new price high.** Require a new product, service, management change, or industry condition change, and require the stock to be emerging from a proper price base (built over roughly 7-8 weeks to 15 months) and approaching or making a new price high — not a stock still well below its highs "because it looks cheap."
4. **S — Supply and demand.** Prefer companies with a relatively small share count outstanding (historically, median winners had roughly 4.6 million shares, under 25 million for over 95% of the biggest winners in the study) and a shrinking floating supply (management ownership, buybacks). Require breakout-day volume at least 50% above the stock's normal average volume, often 100%+.
5. **L — Leader, not laggard.** Restrict buys to the #1 or #2 stock (by price action and relative strength) within a leading industry group; explicitly avoid "sympathy" stocks that merely track a true leader. Require a relative-strength rating of at least 70, preferably 80 or higher; the strongest historical winners were near 90 just before their breakout.
6. **I — Institutional sponsorship.** Require at least a handful of institutional owners with the count trending up over recent quarters, ideally including funds with strong recent performance; avoid stocks with zero institutional interest (poor liquidity) and avoid mega-cap stocks so widely owned that a saturation point has been reached.
7. **M — Market direction.** Do not act on any individual stock signal until the general market's own trend is determined. Track a major index (Dow, S&P, or a broad market index) daily; confirm a new uptrend only on a follow-through day.

**Entry timing**
8. Identify the exact pivot (breakout) price where the stock is emerging from its base on the day volume surges at least 50% above normal.
9. Buy at, or within about 5-10%, of the pivot price. Do not chase a stock already extended more than 5-10% past the correct buy point.
10. Confirm the general market is in a confirmed uptrend (see follow-through day rule below) before initiating new buys; avoid initiating new positions when the market is topping or in a confirmed downtrend.
11. **Follow-through day (market-timing trigger).** After a market decline, watch an attempted rally's daily price/volume action starting from day 1. Ignore days 1-2. A valid follow-through occurs when, typically on day 4 through day 7 (rarely as early as day 3, and considered weak/unreliable after day 10), a major index closes up 1% or more with volume higher than the prior session — this is the buy signal confirming a new market uptrend has likely begun.

**Exit — cutting losses**
12. Set a stop-loss at 7-8% below the purchase price on every position, with no exceptions, and execute it immediately at the market when hit — do not wait for a bounce or a "better price."
13. Treat a decline of 8% or more from a stock's peak (not just from cost) as a signal to reexamine the position for a possible top; a normal correction runs roughly 8-12% and a break below 12-15% is a stronger signal to sell.
14. Never average down (add to a losing position to lower the cost basis); only add to a position that is already working (pyramiding).

**Exit — taking profits**
15. Set a default profit target of 20-25% gain from the exact buy point and sell into strength at that level.
16. Exception: if a stock reaches +20% in fewer than 8 weeks from purchase, treat this as exceptional strength — hold at minimum through the 8-week mark before deciding whether to sell or continue holding for a longer-term (e.g., 6-month) gain, rather than selling immediately at +20%.
17. Once a position reaches roughly +15% or more, tighten the stop to no more than about 5% below the pivot purchase price rather than letting a full round-trip back to breakeven occur; never let a stock that has been up 20%+ fall back into an outright loss.
18. Apply supplementary sell signals when present: a rapid "climax" price run over 2-3 consecutive weeks (roughly 8-12 trading days); a stock split rally of 25-30% (rarely 50%) in one to two weeks after an already-extended advance; relative strength dropping below 70; heavy volume with no further price progress (distribution); a weekly close below a major uptrend line or a key support level on heavy volume.
19. Always sell the worst-performing position in a portfolio first; let the best-performing positions run longer under a trailing (not fixed) exit.

**Exit — market-level defense**
20. On recognizing a confirmed general market top (poor rallies, rally failures, heavy-volume non-progress days in the index), raise at least 25% of the portfolio to cash promptly; act faster and more aggressively if the account is margined, since a 50%-margined portfolio loses roughly double the percentage of equity for a given price decline.

## Risk

The system's primary risk control is the fixed 7-8% stop-loss, applied uniformly regardless of conviction, which caps single-position risk and is explicitly designed to let a trader be wrong on the majority of trades and still be net profitable if winners are allowed to reach the 20-25% target (an asymmetry of roughly 2.5-3:1 reward-to-risk before accounting for win rate). Concentration risk is accepted deliberately: the system favors a small number of thoroughly vetted leaders over broad diversification, which raises single-stock and sector-concentration risk relative to a diversified portfolio. Leverage compounds both directions of this risk — a margined account amplifies losses on any stop-out and accelerates the case for the 25%+ cash-raise rule at a market top. Because the S and L criteria favor small-float, high-relative-strength stocks, positions can be more volatile and less liquid than large-cap alternatives, which is an accepted tradeoff for the higher-growth exposure the system targets.

## Caveats

CAN SLIM's criteria were derived by studying stocks already known to have become major winners, and the source does not report a false-positive rate (how many stocks meeting all seven criteria at the time subsequently failed) — the system's numeric thresholds should be treated as historically observed traits of past winners rather than a backtested, forward-validated trading edge. Several of the numeric rules leave room for judgment despite their apparent precision: what counts as a "proper base" versus a faulty one, and the exact vertical-count-style price target for a base, rely on chart-reading skill the source treats as a separate, dedicated skill (covered elsewhere in the parent book) rather than a fully mechanical rule. The follow-through day concept and market-top historical examples are drawn from indices and market structure (fixed-commission era, pre-decimalization, capital-gains holding-period rules referenced in the sell-rule discussion) that have since changed, so the specific historical dates and index levels cited should be read as illustrations of the pattern rather than current benchmarks.
