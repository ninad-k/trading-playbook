## Overview

Written in 2001–2002 and published in 2003, this is Larry Williams' long-term investing book, distinct from his short-term commodity-trading work. It builds a forecasting case for a major stock market buying opportunity around 2002–2003 and 2005–2006 by stacking three independent long-term cycle arguments (a 10-year "decennial" pattern, a four-year phenomenon, and an October seasonal effect), then pivots to fundamentals-based stock selection (consistent earnings growth, low debt, insider buying, price-versus-earnings "discount" divergence), sentiment-based timing indicators for individual stocks, and a full chapter on money-management formulas (Kelly, Optimal F, Fixed Ratio Trading). The book is part market-history argument, part practical toolkit, and openly promotional of the author's own prior forecasting track record.

## Core thesis

Stock market highs and lows recur with enough regularity across multiple independent long-term cycles (decade position, four-year spacing, calendar month) that a long-term investor can time entries far better than buy-and-hold, without needing to predict company-specific news. Layered on top of cycle timing, stock selection should rely only on verifiable past facts (trailing earnings trend, balance-sheet debt, insider transactions) rather than forecasts of the future, because "it's hard to predict the future" but easy to observe what a business and its own executives have already done. Money management — how many shares/contracts to hold per trade — is, in Williams' own words, more important to long-term wealth than the selection or timing system itself.

## Key concepts

- **Decennial (10-year) pattern** — stock market highs cluster in years ending in 9 and 0; the best buying opportunities cluster in years ending in 2 and 3, with the "phenomenal fifth" year of each decade historically the strongest (11 for 11 up years in Yale Hirsch's count) and years ending in 7 a secondary buy zone.
- **Four-year phenomenon** — a repeating ~4-year spacing between major market lows (1962, 1966, 1970, 1974, 1978, 1982, 1986, 1990, 1994, 1998, 2002…) that Williams calculates held in 86% of years back to 1858.
- **"Straight eight" factor** — years ending in 8 have a strong tendency to start major rallies in their first three months, following an oversold year-7 low.
- **October effect** — the majority of postwar bear markets bottomed in October; Hirsch's 1949–1975 study found October produced an average 7.2% gain six months later versus a 3.7% all-month average, and a mechanical "buy last day of October, sell last day of April" strategy averaged 9.05%/year from 1976–2000 versus 1.1%/year for the reverse (April-to-October) half.
- **Dow yield valuation rule** — Dow Jones Industrial Average dividend yield below 3% has historically marked market tops; yield above 6% has marked major bottoms (Williams notes this ceiling/floor loosened in the 1980s–1990s).
- **Fed Stock Valuation Model (Yardeni)** — ratio of S&P 500 to fair value (12-month forward earnings ÷ 10-year Treasury yield) minus 100; readings above 20 signal overvaluation, below −10 signal a long-term buy zone.
- **Earnings/price "discount" divergence** — a buy signal when a stock's price falls quarter-over-quarter while its trailing-12-month earnings rise quarter-over-quarter (illustrated with Harley-Davidson, Federal Home Loan, Carlisle Companies).
- **Investor sentiment index** — Williams' proprietary weekly bullishness measure (extending Garfield Drew's odd-lot-short-sales work); readings above 75% bullish mark tops, below 25% mark bottoms, filtered further by requiring price above/below its 18-week moving average for higher-confidence signals.
- **Odd lot short sales** — percentage of small (<100 share) orders that are short sales; readings above ~10% have historically flagged market lows.
- **Kelly formula (position sizing)** — F = [(R+1) × P − 1] / R, where P = system win rate and R = average win/loss ratio, giving the fraction of account equity to risk per trade.
- **Optimal F / Fixed Ratio Trading** — Ralph Vince's and Ryan Jones's refinements of Kelly-style sizing that avoid the "blow-up" risk of the raw Kelly formula in markets (versus its original blackjack context) by capping how fast position size scales up with account growth.

## Rules and setups

1. **Decennial timing** — favor new long-term positions in years ending in 2 or 3; expect the strongest single year to be the 5th of the decade; treat years ending in 9 or 0 as high-risk for new long exposure.
2. **Four-year phenomenon** — expect a market low roughly every 4 years from the last confirmed low (Williams' own forward projections from the book: 2002, 2006, 2010, 2014…); combine with the decennial pattern for higher-confidence years (e.g., 2002 = four-year low + decennial low, "double whammy").
3. **October seasonal entry** — buy the broad market (Dow) on the last trading day of October; exit on the last trading day of April; historical average gain 9.05%/year (1976–2000) for this half-year window versus 1.1%/year for the November-skip (April–October) half.
4. **Dow Jones Industrials → Dow Jones Utilities rotation ("Williams High-Yield Investment")** — see companion system page; rotate from the 5 lowest-priced/highest-yield Dow Industrial stocks into the 5 lowest-priced/highest-yield Dow Utility stocks each October/April cycle.
5. **Earnings-discount stock selection** — buy a stock when trailing-12-month EPS is higher than the prior quarter's trailing-12-month EPS while the share price has fallen or is flat quarter-over-quarter; avoid stocks with erratic (non-consecutive) earnings growth.
6. **Debt filter** — avoid high-debt companies; prefer companies whose earnings are not primarily consumed by debt service, since debt-heavy companies decline hardest when interest rates rise.
7. **Insider filter** — favor stocks with net insider buying, especially clustered buying by multiple insiders; treat heavy or clustered insider selling as a caution flag (insiders sell roughly 2.5 shares for every 1 share bought on average, so isolated selling is not itself a signal).
8. **Value screen (Table 11.6, 1975–2001 backtest)** — of the ratios tested, lowest price-to-one-year-forward-earnings (19.3%/yr in Freeburg's refinement) and lowest price-to-book (17.1%/yr) outperformed lowest price-to-sales (13.9%/yr), highest cash flow, and highest return-on-equity (10.0%/yr) as annual-rebalance screens on the Dow universe.
9. **Sentiment-index entries** — buy when the sentiment index is below 25% and price is above its 18-week moving average; sell/write calls when sentiment is above 75%; for options: write calls against a downtrend of ≤7 months once sentiment exceeds 80% (target 50% of premium as profit, entered on Fridays), and mirror this by writing puts in an uptrend when sentiment turns excessively bearish.
10. **Position sizing (Kelly)** — for a system with P = 65% win rate and R = 1.3 average win/loss ratio, F = 38% of account equity risked per trade (worked example in the text); Williams notes the raw Kelly formula caused severe equity swings in his own live trading and was superseded by Optimal F and Fixed Ratio Trading.

## Risk and money management

Williams devotes a full chapter (12) to position sizing, treating it as more important than stock selection or timing. He walks through four approaches in order of his own use: (1) the Kelly formula, which he used to grow small accounts dramatically but which also produced a drawdown from $2.1 million back to $700,000 in his own trading because Kelly's underlying blackjack-derived assumptions (fixed win/loss size) don't hold for markets with variable-sized wins and losses; (2) Ralph Vince's "Optimal F," a fixed percentage of account balance recalculated from the system's actual trade distribution; (3) a margin-plus-drawdown formula (account ÷ [margin + 1.5× worst historical drawdown] = contracts to trade); (4) Ryan Jones's Fixed Ratio Trading, which requires a fixed increment of new profit (e.g., $5,000) before adding each additional contract/unit, deliberately slowing position growth compared to fixed-fractional methods to avoid over-leveraging after a hot streak. No single formula is endorsed as best; Williams presents comparative backtest results on the same bond-trading system to let the reader see the risk/return trade-off of each.

## Psychology and discipline

The recurring psychological argument is contrarianism: Williams repeatedly shows that the investing public, financial advisers, and even professional money managers are most bullish at tops and most bearish at bottoms (illustrated with the 75%/25% sentiment thresholds and odd-lot short-sale data), and that "the trend is your friend until the end" — trend-following crowds become the excess that signals reversal. He also frames belief and patience as necessary trading edges in their own right: any of the cycle or fundamental methods described "will not work all the time," including multi-year stretches of underperformance, and Williams argues most investors and even professional managers abandon sound long-term systems during these stretches, which is precisely why the edge persists for those who stay disciplined.

## Strengths and caveats

The named indicators (Dow yield, Fed valuation model, margin debt, odd-lot shorts, sentiment index) are concrete, numerically bounded, and cross-checked against multiple decades of data, and the money-management chapter is unusually candid about a formula (Kelly) that hurt Williams' own live results — a rare admission in trading literature. Caveats: the book is explicitly a market-timing forecast anchored to 2002–2007 written from a 2001–2002 vantage point, so many of its forward-looking calls (e.g., the specific 2005/2006 buy-point predictions) are dated and should be read as a case study in cycle reasoning rather than a live forecast. The proprietary sentiment index's exact construction is not disclosed (Williams calls it "proprietary"), so it cannot be independently reproduced from the book alone. Several of the historical correlations (the four-year phenomenon, the "straight eight" factor) are presented with strong causal-sounding language but no statistical significance testing, and Williams himself concedes he does not know why several of the patterns exist. The insider-tracking website and several named mutual funds/services (insiderreview.com, Richard Fabian's hotline) reflect early-2000s resources that may no longer exist or operate as described.

## Who should read it

Long-term investors and swing traders interested in a cycle- and seasonality-based approach to market timing combined with simple, verifiable (non-forecast-based) fundamental stock screens. Also useful for the money-management chapter alone, which gives a clear worked comparison of Kelly, Optimal F, and Fixed Ratio position-sizing on the same system. Less suited to short-term/intraday traders, or to readers wanting rigorous statistical validation of the cyclical patterns rather than narrative historical argument.
