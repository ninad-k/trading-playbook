## Overview

This document is not a book. It is a page-by-page capture of TurtleTrader.com, the trend-following education and content site run by Michael Covel from the mid-1990s through 2003, printed to PDF. The 807-page count is misleading: the same navigation sidebar (30-plus links) repeats on almost every page, and the homepage introduction is duplicated in full roughly a dozen times in German, Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch, Portuguese, and Russian for the site's international landing pages. Stripped of repetition, the unique content is closer to a few hundred short articles, Q&As, and interview transcripts.

The material covers the origin story of Richard Dennis's Turtle experiment, profiles of trend-following "Market Wizards" (Dennis, John W. Henry, Ed Seykota, Bill Dunn, Richard Donchian), a running debunking of "Holy Grail" claims (accuracy-chasing, buy-and-hold, stock tips, CNBC pundits), and a cluster of money-management essays (the Kelly formula, Ph.D. gambling-game failures, drawdown-recovery math, expectancy versus win rate). It is explicitly a lead-generation site for a paid Turtle-system course: the specific entry/exit rules and position-sizing formula the original Turtles used are referenced constantly but never disclosed here. Readers wanting the actual rules should look to [[curtis-faith-way-of-the-turtle]] or [[the-complete-turtletrader-the-legend-the-lessons-the-results]], both of which were published later once the rules became public.

## Core thesis

Trend following is a reactive, non-predictive discipline: it does not forecast price, it responds to price and time with a mechanical, rule-based system, and it survives long enough to catch the few large trends that generate most of the profit. The site repeatedly argues that money management (position sizing, risk per trade, drawdown control) accounts for the majority of trading results — one page attributes "90% of the Turtle success story" to money management rather than to the entry/exit signal. A second recurring argument is that percent-accuracy and win rate are irrelevant; what matters is expectancy (average win x win rate minus average loss x loss rate), illustrated with a paired example where an 85%-accurate system is less profitable than a 45%-accurate one.

## Key concepts

- **Trend following** — a systematic approach that trades price and time only, ignoring fundamentals, news, and prediction; positions are entered on breakouts and held until an exit signal, in either direction.
- **Money management / position sizing** — the site's term for the formulas that decide how many contracts or shares to trade at any time; presented as more important than the entry signal itself.
- **Kelly formula** — Bell Labs' 1956 optimal-bet-size formula (J.L. Kelly), cited as inspiration for Richard Dennis's focus on position sizing, but described as too complex for direct real-world trading use.
- **Expectancy** — average profit per trade after accounting for win rate and average win/loss size; used to argue that a lower win-rate system can outperform a higher win-rate one.
- **Frequency versus magnitude (the "Babe Ruth effect")** — being right often matters less than the size of wins when you are right; referenced via a Michael Mauboussin white paper.
- **Drawdown-recovery math** — a loss of X% requires a larger percentage gain to recover (e.g., a 70% loss requires a 233% gain), used to argue for capping losses per trade.
- **Zero-sum framing** — every futures trade has a winner and a loser; the site uses this to argue that skill, not tips or forecasts, separates traders.
- **No prediction** — trend followers do not forecast tops, bottoms, or economic outcomes; they follow whatever the market does.
- **Holy Grail hunting** — the site's label for traders chasing high-accuracy systems, gurus, or stock tips instead of a disciplined, tested process.

## Rules and setups

The document does not disclose the original Turtle entry/exit rules (the 20-day/55-day Donchian breakout system, the N/ATR-based unit sizing, or the specific pyramiding increments). These are referenced as proprietary content of the paid course and are the subject of the companion books in this library. What the site does state in general terms:

1. The Turtle system decides, in this order: how and when to enter, how many units to trade, how much to risk per trade, and how to exit both losing and winning trades — this checklist is given, but the numeric parameters are not.
2. Position size should shrink in more volatile markets and shrink further during losing periods, expanding again only as favorable trends reappear (volatility- and equity-scaled sizing, described qualitatively).
3. No day trading — trades are held for the duration of a trend, sometimes months.
4. No forecasting or fundamental analysis is used to select entries; price and time are the only inputs.
5. The system trades both long and short and applies uniformly across markets (currencies, stock indices, single stocks, and commodities), per the site's claims.

If a reader needs codeable Turtle rules, this document is not a sufficient source — see Caveats.

## Risk and money management

Risk and money management is the site's best-developed topic, though it stays conceptual rather than prescriptive. Recurring arguments:

- Money management (not signal accuracy) is described as the dominant driver of the Turtles' results.
- A Ph.D. gambling experiment is cited: 40 people with a 60%-win-rate, even-money game were given $10,000 and 100 trials; only 2 of 40 finished with a profit, because most increased bet size after losing streaks on the mistaken belief they were "due" for a win.
- Drawdown-recovery is nonlinear: a 20% loss needs roughly a 30% gain to recover; a 70% loss needs about 233%.
- Two hypothetical funds are compared: one with lower volatility and lower average return outperforms a higher-return, higher-drawdown fund once leverage is applied to the lower-risk fund to match its risk profile — used to argue that consistency compounds better than spectacular but volatile returns.
- Expectancy example: an 85%-win system with $500 average win / $1,500 average loss has a $200 per-trade expectation; a 45%-win system with $1,500 average win / $500 average loss has a $400 per-trade expectation — the lower accuracy system is superior.

No specific percent-of-equity risk figure, stop-loss distance, or unit-sizing formula is given in this document.

## Psychology and discipline

Psychology essays are numerous but short. Recurring themes: successful trading requires accepting volatility and drawdowns as the cost of large gains (illustrated by Bill Dunn's Japanese yen trade, which sat through an adverse multi-month period before a large reversal paid off); traders should not personalize entries — two traders using the same system get the same result regardless of who "felt" more confident about timing; and starting with limited capital, so that losses are actually felt, is framed as an advantage over starting with inherited wealth. Several pages needle other market commentators (CNBC pundits, stock tip services, buy-and-hold advocates) as purveyors of "Holy Grail" thinking, positioning trend-following discipline as the alternative.

## Chapter map

The site has no chapters; it is organized as a flat set of linked articles under recurring sidebar categories. Representative groupings, in the order they cluster in the file:

- Trend Following — origin of the Turtles, FAQs, "why it works," money management, zero-sum trading, Holy Grail debunking.
- Market Wizards — profile pages for Richard Dennis, John W. Henry, Ed Seykota, Bill Dunn, Richard Donchian, plus a Ludwig von Mises quote page (free-market philosophy, only loosely connected to trading).
- Market Proof — performance claims, correlation charts, case studies (Enron, Barings Bank, Metallgesellschaft) framed as failures of poor risk control.
- Clients / Pricing — course marketing pages (client counts by country, pricing rationale) with no trading content.
- Money Management or Bet Size — the Kelly formula, Ph.D. experiment, drawdown math, "Tale of Two Traders."
- Holy Grails — CNBC, analyst hype, stock tips, Warren Buffett commentary, Larry Williams and Motley Fool critiques.
- Psychology — life values and risk, critical thinking, Myers-Briggs, quotations.
- International landing pages — the homepage introduction repeated in roughly ten languages.

## Strengths and caveats

The money-management essays (Kelly formula framing, drawdown math, expectancy-over-accuracy) are clear and hold up regardless of era. The Market Wizard profiles give useful color on Dennis, Seykota, Henry, and Donchian for readers unfamiliar with trend-following history.

The central caveat is that this is not an instructional document: it deliberately withholds the actual Turtle trading rules (breakout lookback periods, N/ATR-based unit sizing, pyramiding increments, stop placement) that a reader would need to replicate or code the system — those rules are the product being sold. A large fraction of the page count is navigation-menu boilerplate and repeated multilingual homepage text rather than unique content. Some performance claims (e.g., specific trader returns) are self-reported and unaudited. The site also blends legitimate trading concepts with tangential political/philosophical content (Ludwig von Mises quotations) that has no direct bearing on trading method. No system sub-page is included for this reason: there is no complete, codeable ruleset in the source text.

## Who should read it

Useful as background reading on the marketing and mythology around Turtle trading and trend-following in general, and for the money-management math (Kelly, expectancy, drawdown recovery) presented in an accessible way. Not useful as a standalone source for implementing a Turtle-style system — pair it with [[curtis-faith-way-of-the-turtle]] or [[the-complete-turtletrader-the-legend-the-lessons-the-results]] for the actual rules, or [[turtlerules]] for a direct rules summary.
