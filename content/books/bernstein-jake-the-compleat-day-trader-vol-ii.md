## Overview

The 1998 sequel to [[bernstein-jake-the-compleat-day-trader-vol-i]], continuing Bernstein's futures-focused day-trading toolkit for the same Library-of-Congress-classified futures/futures-market audience. Volume II opens with a pointed "Observations, Claims, and Caveats" chapter (commissions eating marginal edges, broker fill practices, misinformation in commercially sold systems, futures-options myths), reviews the same family of timing indicators more briefly than Volume I, then introduces newer proprietary systems — most prominently the 30-Minute Breakout (30MBO) — alongside day-of-week seasonal patterns (including a Dual Breakout, or DBO, variant), the inside-day pattern, refined gap methods (including a multi-day "multigap" variant), an Accumulation/Distribution oscillator derivative, order-handling mechanics, and an expanded psychology and "Day Trader's Rules for Success" close.

## Core thesis

The same discipline-over-discretion thesis as Volume I, sharpened with more emphasis on realistic transaction-cost and slippage accounting, on choosing markets suited to day trading by volume and volatility rather than forcing a method onto an illiquid instrument, and on treating a chosen system as a closed contract with oneself — Bernstein explicitly warns against combining entry rules from one system with exit rules from another, and against building a pyramided position the wrong way round (adding smaller, not larger, size as a trade moves favorably).

## Key concepts

- **30-Minute Breakout (30MBO)** — Bernstein's newer breakout system, trading a market's break of its first half-hour range by a market-specific number of ticks at the end of any subsequent half-hour period (full detail in the sub-page).
- **Dual Breakout (DBO)** — a day-of-week seasonal breakout: buy on a stop a set number of ticks above the previous day's high (or sell below the previous day's low) only on days where a prior open/close relationship condition is met, exiting on the next profitable opening.
- **Inside day** — a session whose high is below the prior day's high and whose low is above the prior day's low; used as a potential trend-change signal, traded by buying above the inside day's high or selling below its low by a market-calibrated tick offset.
- **Multigap method** — a gap-trading variant comparing gaps against a multi-day (not just prior-day) high/low window, exiting on the nth subsequent profitable opening rather than same-day.
- **Accumulation/Distribution Oscillator Derivative and Advance/Decline Derivative (ADD)** — volume- and breadth-based derivative oscillators intended to time entries around institutional accumulation or distribution.
- **Range breakout system** — a Keltner-type volatility-band breakout described as a day-trading variation on classic channel breakout methods.
- **MOC (market-on-close)** — a standard session-end exit convention used across several of the book's systems.
- **Pyramiding discipline** — adding progressively smaller (not larger) size as a position moves favorably, to avoid a "top-heavy" position that a small adverse move can wipe out.

## Rules and setups

1. **30-Minute Breakout (30MBO)** — full rule set in the [[bernstein-jake-the-compleat-day-trader-vol-ii--30-minute-breakout|30MBO system sub-page]]: mark the first 30 minutes' high/low, then buy or sell any subsequent half-hour close that exceeds that range by a market-specific number of ticks, with a stop-loss or reversing-signal exit and an end-of-day/MOC close.
2. **Dual Breakout (DBO), worked S&P example** — trade only on the indicated day of the week; buy on a stop a set number of ticks above the previous day's high if the previous close was above the previous open (mirror for sell signals); in the book's Monday S&P case study this was long 3 ticks above Friday's high or short 26 ticks below Friday's low, with a $3,400 stop, exiting on the first profitable opening. The general tick offset ("x ticks") is stated to be market- and day-specific, not a single universal number — only this one worked example survives in the sampled pages.
3. **Inside-day breakout** — after an inside day, buy at the inside day's high plus a tick offset, or sell at its low minus a tick offset; the offset must be "large enough to avoid whipsaw-type trades and small enough to give a sufficient number of trades," but no specific value is given in the sampled pages, and a risk-management stop is called for separately.
4. **Gap methods** — basic gap buy/sell signals mirror Volume I's approach (enter on a penetration back through the prior day's extreme by a given number of ticks, exit at a fixed-dollar stop, a stop beyond the day's extreme, or MOC); the multigap variant in the book's S&P testing used a 9-day lookback window for buy signals and a 2-day window for sell signals, producing an average trade of about $482 same-day or about $780 when held to the next profitable opening (both after slippage and commission, per the book's stated testing).
5. **S&P stop-sizing caution** — even a 500-point ($1,250) stop can be insufficient in S&P futures given an average daily range of roughly 600 points; undersized stops are cited as a primary reason many S&P day traders lose.
6. **Pyramiding rule** — when adding to a working position, use a front-loaded, shrinking size sequence (e.g., 5 contracts, then 3, then 2, then 1), never an escalating one.
7. **Order-handling discipline** — insist on prompt fill confirmations (especially in currencies, T-bonds, S&P, and petroleum, where "flash fills" are common), know which order types a given exchange will and won't accept, and treat the broker as an employee who executes decisions rather than a source of trading opinion.

## Risk and money management

As in Volume I, risk rules are system-specific rather than governed by one universal formula: the 30MBO uses either a predetermined (fail-safe) stop or the opposite signal as a reversing exit, plus an optional trailing stop once a profit objective is reached; the DBO worked example carries an explicit $3,400 dollar stop; the inside-day breakout calls for "a risk management stop loss" without a specified value. The book's clearest general risk lesson is the S&P stop-sizing caution above: a stop must be sized to the instrument's actual average range, not to an arbitrarily small, comfortable-looking dollar figure. No percent-of-equity position-sizing formula is given in the sampled pages; sizing decisions (including the pyramiding sequence) are framed qualitatively rather than numerically.

## Psychology and discipline

The psychology chapters expand on Volume I with more material on broker relationships (be assertive; the broker executes your decisions and should not talk you into or out of a trade), self-control specifically around overtrading, and the same trading-diary and daily-goal discipline from Volume I. A closing "Frequently Asked Questions"-style section addresses two common discipline failures directly: combining entry rules from one system with exit rules from another (discouraged — trade one tested system's rules "to the exclusion of all else"), and pyramiding incorrectly (discouraged unless sized in a shrinking, not growing, sequence as described above).

## Strengths and caveats

Volume II is notably more self-critical than a typical trading manual, devoting an entire opening chapter to why most commercially sold systems fail and to the specific mechanics (fills, commissions, undersized stops) that erode S&P day-trading edges — a useful corrective before the systems chapters that follow. Its flagship 30MBO system is presented with a complete entry/exit/stop framework, but the actual tick threshold that triggers an entry is explicitly described as market-specific and "discussed later in the chapter," and that specific figure did not survive in the sampled OCR pages — it must be treated as a parameter to calibrate per instrument, not a fixed constant. Several other systems (DBO, inside-day breakout) similarly rely on an unspecified "x ticks" parameter, with only the DBO's single S&P worked example (77 percent win rate per its Omega Research-generated performance table) giving a concrete number — a single historical backtest record, not an out-of-sample validated result. As with Volume I, examples and tick/dollar figures are calibrated to 1990s futures contracts and would need translation to a stock or forex trader's own instrument.

## Who should read it

Futures and other short-term traders who have read or are familiar with Volume I and want its newer, generally better-specified breakout systems (especially the 30MBO), plus a sharper skeptical framing of why most day-trading systems and claims don't hold up. Less useful as a standalone starting point — several chapters assume familiarity with concepts (MAC, CTOD, basic order types) introduced at greater length in Volume I — or for a reader who wants every system's parameters fully pinned down rather than partially left to per-instrument calibration.
