## Summary

A popular explainer of Warren Buffett's investing approach, built from his letters, interviews, and the influences of Benjamin Graham and Philip Fisher. The core chapters lay out Buffett's 12 investing principles (buy understandable, financially strong businesses run by honest management, at sensible prices), then contrast value investing (low P/E, low price-to-book, unloved companies) with growth investing (high P/E, popular, momentum-driven), and catalog behavioral biases that trip up investors. The back half profiles a dozen professional value managers — Ruane (Sequoia), Hagstrom, Simpson (GEICO), Browne (Tweedy Browne), Whitman, Schloss, and others — who each apply Buffett-style discipline with their own variations.

## Key points

- Buffett's 12 principles: understandable business, consistent operating history, favorable long-term prospects, honest/competent management, focus on return on equity (not EPS), high profit margins, retained-earnings value creation, buy at a sensible price relative to intrinsic value.
- Value stocks trade at low P/E and low price-to-book; growth stocks trade at high multiples on strong momentum — Buffett shifted from pure "cigar butt" value (Graham) toward "growth at a reasonable price" (GARP) as Berkshire's capital base grew.
- Buffett's preferred holding period is "forever" — he avoids frequent trading and treats permanent holdings (Coca-Cola, Gillette, Washington Post) as businesses to own, not stocks to trade.
- Philip Fisher's small-investor guidance: concentrate in ~5 blue-chip names (20% each) or ~10 mid-caps (10% each); avoid overdiversification, which makes a portfolio impossible to monitor closely.
- Common investor mistakes catalogued: cognitive dissonance, anchoring on purchase price or 52-week highs, herd instinct, overconfidence, sunk-cost fallacy, status-quo bias, and the "halo effect" of a favored industry.
- Value investors' typical errors: buying too soon on the way down, and selling too soon after a modest bounce, leaving further gains on the table.
- Buffett is described as businesslike to the point of severity — prioritizing shareholders over sentiment (e.g., closing the Berkshire textile mill after years of losses despite personal attachment).
- The profiled managers (Ruane, Hagstrom, Simpson, Browne, Whitman, Schloss, Torray, Walczak, Gipson, Price) share concentrated, low-turnover, business-focused portfolios but differ on international exposure, cash levels, and sector concentration.

## Actionable rules

1. Favor businesses you understand, with a consistent multi-year operating history and honest, competent management.
2. Judge management by return on equity rather than earnings-per-share growth alone.
3. Buy at a price that leaves a margin between price and estimated intrinsic value; do not chase popularity.
4. Limit portfolio size to a number of names you can genuinely monitor — Fisher suggested as few as 5 blue chips (20% each) for smaller accounts, or up to 25 for larger ones split by risk tier.
5. Once you own a quality business, default to holding rather than trading; sell mainly when the thesis breaks or a better opportunity requires the capital.
6. Watch for the specific cognitive traps listed (anchoring, herd instinct, sunk-cost, halo effect) as a pre-trade checklist before buying or selling.

## Caveats

Written as a popular synthesis (2001) rather than original research; it aggregates Buffett's public statements and journalist interviews rather than primary financial-statement analysis, so treat the "12 principles" as a curated summary, not a verbatim list from Buffett himself. Several fund-manager profiles reflect performance and holdings current only through 2000–2001 and are dated. Aimed at long-horizon equity investors rather than active traders — it contains no entries, stops, or position-sizing rules of the kind used in the rest of this library.

## Who it is for

Beginner and intermediate investors who want an accessible entry point into Buffett/Graham/Fisher-style value investing, or traders curious how professional value managers translate the philosophy into concentrated, low-turnover portfolios.
