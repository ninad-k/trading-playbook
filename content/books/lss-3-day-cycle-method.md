## Summary

A short introductory guide, written by futures trader George Angell, to the "LSS" system, which is his software implementation of George Douglas Taylor's 1950s "Book Method" (from Taylor's "The Taylor Trading Technique"). The core idea is that markets are recurrently "engineered" by informed participants: a lower opening creates a buying opportunity (an "L" or Buy day), the market then rallies to near the prior high (an "S" or Sell day), and finally opens at an extreme to offer a short-sale opportunity (an "SS" or Short Sell day) — a repeating 3-day cycle. These gap moves are explained as stop-loss orders (placed by convention above/below the prior day's high/low) being run by "smart money," who then fade the resulting short-lived move. The book gives a practical framework — buy/sell "envelopes," an overbought/oversold oscillator, and volume clues — for identifying which day of the cycle is in play and trading it with defined risk.

## Key points

- **3-day cycle** — Buy Day (low made first, then rally) -> Sell Day (trades at/near prior high) -> Short Sell Day (high made first, then decline), repeating (sometimes stretching to 4-5 days).
- **Taylor's "Book Method"** — a running log ("taking the count") of recent rallies and declines used to derive support/resistance "envelopes" rather than a single price level.
- **Buy/sell envelope** — a price zone (not a single price) within which the trader can average into a position, giving room for the trade to be "difficult" without abandoning the plan.
- **Non-trending day filter** — fade rallies if the prior day's one-day overbought/oversold reading was above 70%; buy dips if below 30%.
- **Trending day filter** — follow the trend using a 10-day overbought/oversold oscillator: buy above 50%, sell below 50%.
- **Volume signal** — tops and bottoms (even intraday) tend to occur on markedly higher volume; a major reversal is unlikely to be confirmed until volume spikes.
- Suggested day-trading markets: S&P 500, US Treasury bonds, Swiss franc, Deutschemark, British pound, silver, NYSE Dow, coffee, copper, soybeans.
- Day-trading margin cited as roughly half of overnight margin (example: $7,500 vs. $15,000 for S&P 500 futures at the time of writing), doubling effective leverage.

## Actionable rules

1. Identify the prior day's cycle position (Buy/Sell/Short-Sell) by whether the high or low printed first, to anticipate the next day's likely pattern.
2. Trade within the buy/sell envelope (support/resistance zone), not a single exact price; average in if the zone holds.
3. Exit immediately if a support/resistance envelope is violated with a genuine breakout (price moves significantly away rather than snapping back) — this signals the anticipated pattern failed.
4. On non-trending days, fade the extreme using a one-day oscillator: sell above 70%, buy below 30%.
5. On trending days, follow the 10-day oscillator: buy above 50%, sell below 50%.
6. Take small losses quickly when wrong; do not hold and hope.
7. Use "Market on Close" (MOC) orders when exiting day-trade positions, submitted before each market's specific close cutoff.

## Caveats

Reflects 1950s-era pit-trading concepts (Taylor's original work) applied to a 1990s/2000s retrofit with computer software; several specific numeric envelope-calculation rules from the underlying "Book Method" are referenced but not fully reproduced (the author repeatedly defers to Taylor's original book for full detail). Futures margin and market-selection specifics (margin dollar amounts, tick values) are dated and would need updating for current markets.

## Who it is for

Short-term futures day traders interested in contrarian, stop-run-fade setups and classical support/resistance cycle analysis, who are willing to supplement this short introduction with Taylor's original "The Taylor Trading Technique."
