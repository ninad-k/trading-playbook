## Summary

A short trading-lesson handout on John Bollinger's Bollinger Bands: how they are built, what they're meant to show, and the author's preferred way of combining them with RSI to flag potential reversals. It is written as a beginner-friendly primer rather than a research paper.

## Key points

- **Bollinger Bands** — three lines: a simple moving average (default 20-period) in the middle, with upper and lower bands plotted at a multiple of standard deviation (default 2) above and below it.
- Standard deviation measures volatility, so the bands widen and narrow as volatility rises and falls.
- The closing price is the input most commonly used to build the moving average and bands.
- Primary uses are (a) identifying high/low volatility regimes, (b) flagging price extremes that may be ready to reverse, and (c) confirming a trending market — not generating standalone buy/sell signals.
- **The Squeeze** — a period of unusually low volatility (bands pinch together), often preceding a large price move and useful for spotting breakout zones.
- Bands alone don't signal trend start, but combined with other indicators they can confirm an established trend and are easy to read visually.
- The author's preferred combination is Bollinger Bands with a 14-period RSI to time overbought/oversold reversals.
- The author reports the bands working across timeframes from 5-minute to monthly charts, though settings should be tuned per market.

## Actionable rules

1. Build the bands with a 20-period simple moving average of closing price, plus/minus 2 standard deviations.
2. Set RSI to a 14-period lookback.
3. Treat the market as overbought when RSI is above 70 and price is at or pushing through the upper band; consider shorting or covering longs.
4. Treat the market as oversold when RSI is below 30 and price is at or pushing through the lower band; consider buying or covering shorts.
5. Watch for a "squeeze" (bands narrowing sharply) as an alert that a breakout may be approaching.
6. Experiment with moving-average length and standard-deviation multiplier per instrument and timeframe rather than assuming the 20/2 default is optimal everywhere.
