## Summary

The book opens by attacking common stock-market myths (that investing is gambling, that you must watch the market constantly) and cataloguing investor mistakes such as returning emotionally to a stock that "owes you," or averaging down out of ego. It sets out eight criteria for judging any strategy: let gains run while cutting losses short, size losses as a small percentage of account value rather than an arbitrary dollar stop, enter gradually rather than all at once, cap possible loss per position, use unambiguous predefined rules, exit promptly on underperformance, and keep the biggest dollar allocation in the biggest winners. It then illustrates the worst possible strategy — "Scale Trading" (adding to a position as it falls) — to show why it snowballs losses, before inverting it into the book's core system, the "Reverse Scale Strategy," and closing with a chapter on using margin to amplify it.

## Key points

- **Variability Concept**: in any portfolio, some stocks will perform far better than others; the goal is having most capital ride the eventual winners, not spread evenly.
- **Trend Concept**: large stock moves are gradual, multi-month-to-multi-year trends, not one-off jumps (barring buyouts or bankruptcies).
- **Scale Trading (to avoid)**: buying more as a stock declines starves winners of capital and funnels cash into losers, with theoretically unlimited required capital and no defined loss-cutting point.
- **Reverse Scale Strategy**: buy an initial position, then add an equal *dollar* amount (fewer shares each time) whenever price rises to the next fixed percentage increment above the last purchase ("decision point").
- **Exit rule**: sell the entire position if price retraces all the way back to the previous decision point, on the logic that giving back a full increment signals lost leadership momentum.
- **Loss sizing**: size positions so a full stop-out loses only a small, predetermined percentage of total account equity, rather than picking an arbitrary per-share stop distance.
- **Margin Power**: a later chapter layers controlled margin borrowing onto the strategy to increase returns, with an explicit warning that margin increases risk of loss correspondingly.

## Actionable rules

1. Cut losses at a small, fixed percentage of total account equity per trade, sizing the position so a full stop-out costs only that percentage.
2. Take an initial position, then add an equal dollar amount (fewer shares each time) whenever price advances to the next fixed percentage increment above the prior purchase.
3. Move the stop-loss up to the previous decision point after each add; sell the entire position on a retrace through that level.
4. Never add to a declining position (the behavior rejected as Scale Trading).
5. Diversify across enough positions that capital can concentrate into the eventual winners over time.

The PDF's text extraction was corrupted for large stretches (a font-encoding shift dropped most numeric characters), so the exact "decision point" percentage and loss-cutting percentage could not be recovered — the mechanics above are accurate but the author's specific numbers are missing. ## Who it is for

Retail stock investors who want a disciplined, rules-based alternative to averaging down, and who are comfortable building positions gradually into strength rather than diversifying and holding statically.
