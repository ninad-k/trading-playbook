## Overview

Gary Smith spent nineteen unprofitable years trading commodities before finding his edge in the spring of 1985 trading stock index futures, and later, far more profitably, trading mutual funds. How I Trade for a Living is his account of that method: a discretionary, momentum-driven style built on recurring intraday and multi-day price patterns, filtered through sentiment indicators (put/call ratios, the McClellan Oscillator, short-selling data) and calendar seasonality. The book documents his real, audited returns — 40.55%, 46.88%, and 84.90% in his three tracked INVESCO fund years (1996-98) against the S&P 500's 23.25%, 33.55%, and 28.56% — and walks through dozens of dated, real trades showing exactly how those numbers were made.

## Core thesis

Consistent trading profit comes from recognizing the market's short-term "rhythm" — a handful of repeatable momentum patterns (V-bottom reversals, late-day surges, index divergences, true selling days) — and reacting to them as they happen rather than forecasting or relying on lagging chart indicators. Smith is emphatic that he is a trader, not a market timer: timers relying on purely mechanical signals underperform buy-and-hold over most multi-year periods (he cites MoniResearch and Hulbert Financial Digest data), while his discretionary, momentum-reactive approach beat the S&P by a wide margin during his tracked years. His other central claim is that vehicle choice matters as much as method: leveraged instruments suit small accounts needing to grow quickly, but unleveraged, low-volatility mutual funds are the better wealth-compounding vehicle once capital is adequate, since they tolerate the large position sizes his money management calls for.

## Key concepts

- **V-bottom reversal** — an intraday pattern where the Dow is down at least 0.75% for most of the session, then reverses to close near unchanged or higher; more significant the later in the day it occurs and only meaningful after a prior down day or downtrend.
- **Late-day upside surge** — a choppy, trendless session that rallies in its final 2-2.5 hours to close at least 0.50% above the prior day's close; interpreted the same way as a V-bottom (only significant coming off weakness).
- **1% true selling day** — an exit trigger: the Dow, S&P, Nasdaq 100, and Russell 2000 all close down 1%+ (tolerance to 0.75% on one index) after at least two weeks of rising prices; Smith's least reliable but most consequential pattern.
- **Index divergence** — comparing the Dow, cash S&P, and Nasdaq Composite/Russell 2000 via a rule-of-thumb point ratio (e.g., 8 Dow points ≈ 1 Nasdaq 100 point); a stronger-than-implied move in the broader/tech indexes signals continuation.
- **Tight rising channel** — a mutual fund or stock advancing steadily with reactions no larger than 2-3%; the trading behavior Smith actively seeks out because it tolerates large, patient position building.
- **New-fund effect** — the empirically documented tendency of newly launched mutual funds to outperform in their first one to six months, driven by small asset bases and favorable IPO allocations.
- **Small-cap divergence** — a signal that the Russell 2000 begins outperforming the Dow/S&P after an initial large-cap rally (typically following a 7%+ prior decline), used to rotate into small-cap funds.
- **Scale-up money management** — building a position in increments as it moves in your favor (Darvas/Livermore style); Smith's rule "you buy on a scale-up and sell on a scale-down," never the reverse.
- **Death Zone** — the 12:30-1:30 p.m. CST window (originally 1:00-1:40 p.m.) prone to sharp countertrend reversals in stock index futures.
- **Trin (Arms Index)** — an intraday breadth/volume ratio Smith uses to confirm shorting setups; readings ≥ 0.95 support a short, readings below 0.90 negate it.
- **McClellan Oscillator / put-call ratio** — breadth and sentiment indicators used as confirming filters rather than standalone signals; extreme readings (e.g., CBOE put/call ≥ 1.00 on consecutive days, or the OEX put/call ≥ 1.60) flag contrarian buy conditions.

## Rules and setups

Smith's two fully mechanical rule sets are documented as companion system pages: [[gary-smith-how-i-trade-living--momentum-fund-timing]] covers the momentum-pattern entries and exits he uses to trade mutual funds and stocks (V-bottom reversals, late-day surges, true selling days, seasonality strategies, and the 1-2% initial stop / 3-4% trailing exit), and [[gary-smith-how-i-trade-living--sp-futures-day-trading]] covers his original and current intraday stock-index-futures methodology (breakout-to-new-highs, bounce-off-the-low, the Dow/S&P/Nasdaq divergence method, and his shorting setup). Both rely on the same underlying momentum philosophy applied at different timeframes and in different vehicles.

## Risk and money management

Smith's money management is unusually explicit and risk-averse for someone posting triple-digit annual returns: on any initial fund or stock purchase he risks no more than 1-2% of the position's value, exits if a "tight rising channel" position reacts 3-4% from its recent high, and always builds positions gradually (25-33% increments) while exiting all at once or in large chunks (33-50%) — "it can take me weeks to build up a position... but only a couple of days to be completely back in cash." He avoids leverage once an account no longer needs it, arguing leveraged vehicles suit only small accounts needing rapid initial growth; his own futures trading stayed capped at one contract because his risk tolerance couldn't handle a meaningful drawdown. He also cites the finding (Barber & Odean, 1991-96) that frequent traders (48+ round trips/year) earned roughly a third less than average accounts, framing his own trading as selective rather than habitually active.

## Psychology and discipline

Smith frames his own multi-decade path (nineteen years as a break-even commodities trader before finding an edge) as proof that persistence and self-knowledge matter more than raw talent. His central psychological warning is against the inability to re-enter a position after being stopped out of a good trend — he describes repeatedly exiting winning trades early during his unprofitable years and then being unable to "make the U-turn" back in when the trend resumed, a defect he says traders must consciously train themselves out of. He is explicit about matching method to temperament: because he is "psychologically unable" to handle the volatility of individual stocks or leveraged futures in size, he built his strategy around mutual funds specifically for their low intraday volatility and trend persistence, arguing that finding a trading "niche" suited to one's own risk tolerance is as important as any specific pattern or indicator.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's chief strength is its specificity: real dates, real percentages, and audited multi-year fund returns rather than vague principles, which makes the patterns testable. Its central weakness is that same specificity's fragility — several strategies depend on market microstructure that no longer exists as described (fee-free fund switching, the overseas-lag trade exploiting stale fund pricing, the MidCap futures contract, and the original Nasdaq 100 futures contract all changed within the book's own text). The patterns are discretionary rather than mechanically defined, so results hinge on the trader's judgment about when a setup is "confirmed" — Smith notes his own attempts to mechanize the method into a black-box system failed. The book also reflects the extraordinary 1990s bull market; the author acknowledges his near-exclusive long bias, which may not generalize to bear or range-bound markets.

## Who should read it

Discretionary swing and day traders interested in momentum/divergence pattern recognition across equities, mutual funds, and stock index futures, and readers who want a real-money, warts-and-included account of position sizing and psychological discipline rather than a purely mechanical system.
