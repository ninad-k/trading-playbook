## Summary

A short introductory primer positioning technical analysis as the study of price action to identify trend direction, built on two ideas: "markets discount" (price reflects all fundamentals and psychology combined) and "history repeats" (recurring psychology creates recurring chart patterns). It covers bar-chart construction, argues for anchoring analysis on monthly and weekly charts before timing entries on the daily chart, explains support/resistance and why old resistance becomes new support, briefly introduces flags, pennants, and triangles, the four gap types, and closes with a note on oscillators.

## Key points

- Core formula: "Market perception (psychology) + Fundamentals = Price action" — technicians don't need to know why a market moves, only that the move is real.
- "Price + Time = Value": fast price spikes are usually short-lived and prices tend to revert toward value.
- Establish the long-term trend from monthly and weekly charts first, then time entries off the daily chart; never read the daily chart in isolation.
- Four gap types: common (fill quickly, little meaning), breakaway (signals a new major trend, stays open for weeks/months), runaway (confirms an established trend), and exhaustion (marks a climax, gets filled on a close).
- Old resistance becomes new support because shorts caught in a breakout cover near their old entry while new longs add on the pullback to the breakout level.
- Continuation patterns: flags/pennants (brief pauses after a fast move) and triangles (ascending/descending/symmetrical), breaking in the direction of the steeper trendline.
- Calls day trading "the worst possible way of trading" because shorter timeframes are more random and commissions/slippage erode returns relative to reward.

## Actionable rules

1. Establish trend direction from the monthly chart, then the weekly, and use the daily chart only to time entries.
2. Treat a support/resistance level as more valid the more times it has been tested.
3. Expect broken resistance to flip to support (and vice versa) and plan entries around that flip.
4. Read a breakaway gap as the start of a major trend; read an exhaustion gap that fills on a close as a sign the move is over.
5. Read ascending triangles as bullish, descending as bearish, symmetrical as either direction.
6. In a strong trend, weight oscillator divergence only in the overbought zone (uptrend) or oversold zone (downtrend); a turn up from oversold can flag trend resumption, not reversal.
