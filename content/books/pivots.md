## Summary

Gopalakrishnan describes pivot points as next-day support and resistance levels calculated from the previous day’s high, low, and close. The traditional pivot is `(H+L+C)/3`; first support and resistance are `2P−H` and `2P−L`, while second support and resistance are `P−(H−L)` and `P+(H−L)` (p. 1). Two variations include today’s open in the average or replace yesterday’s close with today’s open to account for gaps (p. 1). Prices above the pivot are treated as bullish and prices below as bearish; the article suggests using first and second levels as objectives and a stop near the broken level (pp. 1–2). For trend context, it compares the daily pivot with a three-day pivot average and a five-day moving average; agreement is bullish or bearish, while conflict is a reason to stand aside (p. 3). The proposed routine calculates levels, determines daily and weekly trend, sets objectives and stops, waits 20–30 minutes after the open, and enters when price turns from the pivot in the trend direction (p. 4). A Nasdaq 100 example lists P=2,852.33, S1=2,834.67, R1=2,883.67, S2=2,803.30, and R2=2,901.33, with a long exit at R2 after a move from S2 through the pivot (pp. 4–5).

## Key points

- The author prefers the traditional formula but allows the open-based variation on gap days (p. 1).
- Market range and liquidity affect whether support/resistance levels are reached (p. 2).
- The example is one historical trade, not a performance test.

## Actionable rules

1. Calculate the next session’s pivot and two support/resistance levels from completed prior-session data, recording which of the three formulas is used (p. 1).
2. Compare the pivot with its three-day average and with a five-day moving average; consider a trade only when the daily and weekly directions agree (p. 3).
3. Set stops and objectives, wait 20–30 minutes after the open, then enter only when price turns from the pivot in the trend direction (p. 4).
4. Use the first level as the initial objective; if price breaks it, trail behind that level toward the second objective, as illustrated rather than statistically validated (pp. 2, 5).

## Caveats

The article offers selected futures and equity examples and says the technique does not work every time. Its levels may be less meaningful in different sessions, instruments, and volatility regimes.

## Who it is for

Intraday traders testing a transparent support/resistance framework.
