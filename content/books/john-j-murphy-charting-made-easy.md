## Overview

A short "Trade Secrets" series booklet (17 brief chapters over ~60 pages of content) distilling core technical-analysis concepts from Murphy's longer reference works into a beginner-friendly primer. Each chapter covers one tool — trendlines, reversal/continuation patterns, gaps, volume, moving averages, oscillators, ratios, options sentiment — in a page or two, illustrated with real MetaStock chart examples from 1999-2000 (Intel, GE, AOL, JDS Uniphase, and other dot-com-era names). It closes with a "top-down" framework for narrowing from the broad market down to an individual stock, and a resource guide pointing to Murphy's fuller books.

## Core thesis

Markets move in trends, and the job of chart analysis is to identify a trend, gauge its probable extent, and catch its reversal as early as possible — using price action itself as a shortcut form of fundamental analysis, since all fundamental, political, and psychological factors are assumed to already be reflected in price. No single tool is treated as sufficient on its own: Murphy's closing "principle of confirmation" holds that a signal is only as strong as the number of independent technical factors (price pattern, volume, moving averages, oscillators, related stocks in the same group, multiple timeframes) that agree with it.

## Key concepts

- **Trend classification** — major (over a year), secondary/intermediate (1-3 months), minor (a couple of weeks or less); trends are a series of rising or falling peaks and troughs.
- **Support and resistance** — a prior trough (support) or peak (resistance); once decisively broken, the level reverses role (broken support becomes new resistance, and vice versa).
- **Trendline** — connects two rising lows (up trendline) or falling highs (down trendline); a third touch that holds is what confirms the line as valid, not just the first two points.
- **Channel line** — a line parallel to the basic trendline on the opposite side of price, marking the expected far boundary while the market trends.
- **Reversal patterns** — head and shoulders, double/triple tops and bottoms, spike (V-reversal), saucer (rounding) — signal a trend change is underway.
- **Continuation patterns** — symmetrical/ascending/descending triangles, flags, pennants — signal a pause before the prior trend resumes; take less time to form than reversal patterns.
- **Price gaps** — breakaway (at a pattern breakout), runaway/measuring (mid-move), and exhaustion (at the move's end); two gaps isolating a few sessions form an "island reversal."
- **On-balance volume (OBV)** — a running cumulative total that adds a day's volume on an up close and subtracts it on a down close; divergence between rising price and a flat/falling OBV line warns the move lacks support.
- **Disparity/relative-strength oscillators (RSI, stochastics)** — bounded 0-100 tools that flag overbought/oversold extremes and generate crossover signals.
- **Top-down approach** — market averages, then sectors/industry groups, then individual stocks, on the premise that as much as 50% of a stock's move is attributable to its industry group.
- **Principle of confirmation** — a signal's reliability scales with how many independent technical factors (pattern, volume, moving averages, oscillators, peer stocks, multiple timeframes) agree.

## Rules and setups

The book is a conceptual primer rather than a single tradable system, but it states these concrete, numbered rules throughout:

1. **Trendline validity**: two touch points draw a candidate trendline; a third touch that bounces off it (rather than breaking through) is required to treat the line as confirmed/valid.
2. **Head and shoulders target**: measure the vertical distance from the head to the neckline, then project that same distance from the point where the neckline breaks.
3. **Double top/bottom target**: measure the height of the pattern and project it from the point where the intervening trough/peak is broken.
4. **Triangle target**: measure the triangle's height at its widest (left) point and project that distance from wherever either trendline is broken; use for symmetrical, ascending, and descending triangles alike.
5. **Retracement bands**: a minimum retracement against a trend is about one-third of the prior move; a 50% retracement is the most common; two-thirds is treated as the maximum retracement consistent with the prior trend resuming — beyond that, expect a full reversal. Fibonacci 38% and 62% retracements are also flagged as watched levels.
6. **Moving averages (stocks)**: the 50-day MA is the standard intermediate support/resistance reference (a decisive close below it often precedes a slide toward the 200-day MA); the 200-day MA is the standard long-term reference; a 20-day MA is used for short-term trend changes. Weekly equivalents: 10-week and 40-week.
7. **Bollinger Bands**: 2 standard deviations above/below a 20-day moving average; a touch or break of either band signals an over-extended, mean-reverting-prone market.
8. **RSI**: scaled 0-100 with the 70/30 lines marking overbought/oversold; most common lookback is 9 or 14 days.
9. **Stochastics**: scaled 0-100 with 80/20 marking overbought/oversold; buy when the fast line crosses above the slow line from below 20, sell when it crosses below the slow line from above 80; the slow line is typically a 3-day moving average of the fast line; 14 periods is the standard lookback, kept consistent across whatever timeframe (14 days, 14 weeks, 14 hours) is charted.
10. **CBOE VIX**: typically ranges 20-30; readings below 20 are associated with market tops, readings above 30 with market bottoms (a contrary/sentiment indicator, not a price-pattern rule).
11. **Chart-reading order**: for any full analysis, start with the monthly chart, then the weekly, then the daily (and intraday only if needed) — establish the long-term context before narrowing the timeframe.

## Risk and money management

The book does not address position sizing, stop placement, or portfolio risk in any formal way — it is purely a chart-pattern and indicator primer. The closest it comes to risk guidance is structural: using price/volume/pattern confirmation to avoid acting on a single, unconfirmed signal, and using the top-down approach to concentrate analysis (and by extension capital) in the strongest sectors and industry groups rather than spreading attention evenly across the whole market.

## Psychology and discipline

Psychology is not a dedicated topic; the implicit discipline taught throughout is procedural rather than emotional — always seek confirmation across multiple independent technical factors before acting on a signal, and always work top-down (market, then sector, then stock) rather than jumping straight to an individual chart without establishing the broader trend context first.

## Strengths and caveats

As a concise primer the book is clear and well-illustrated, but it is explicitly promotional in parts (MetaStock software, Murphy's CPR pattern-recognition plug-in, and other Traders' Library titles are advertised at length in the back matter) and every worked example is drawn from the 1999-2000 dot-com-era bull market, which skews the illustrative charts toward strong uptrends and may not represent pattern reliability in choppier or bearish regimes. No pattern success rates, backtests, or statistical validation are given anywhere in the book — every rule (retracement bands, moving-average lengths, oscillator thresholds) is presented as conventional practice, not as tested or quantified. Given its brevity, it functions best as an index of concepts to look up elsewhere in more depth rather than a complete reference.

## Who should read it

Newer traders who want a fast, non-technical map of standard chart-reading vocabulary and conventions before tackling denser reference texts (including Murphy's own *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*). Not useful for traders seeking backtested rules, money management guidance, or novel technique — it is a vocabulary and concept primer, not a systems or strategy book.
