## Summary

Larry Levin, founder of Futures Education, frames the book around one idea: since you cannot control the market, the only lever available is controlling yourself, and success comes from consistently "acting in your own best interest." It opens with a parable contrasting two aspiring S&P 500 day-traders — one who fails from unmanaged emotion despite a strong business background, one who succeeds using goal-setting and visualization with no prior trading experience. Section I covers needed skills (goal-setting, loving a loss, personal trading rules, flawless execution, stops, objectivity). Section II covers habits to eliminate (revenge trading, wishing/hoping/praying, doubling up on losers, greed, fear). Section III introduces Psycho-Cybernetics — visualization and self-image work (citing Maxwell Maltz and Mark Douglas's "The Disciplined Trader") to rehearse trading behavior before it's needed live.

## Key points

- **Acting in your own best interest**: the core theme — since the market can't be controlled, discipline over your own actions is the only edge.
- **Loving to take a loss**: treat a predefined loss as a cost of doing business, not a reflection of self-worth; hesitating to exit reinforces disaster (quoting Mark Douglas).
- **Structured freedom**: because trading has no external rules, the trader must write and follow a personal rule set or be overwhelmed by discretion.
- **Revenge trading**: trying to "get back" a prior loss in the next trade is revenge on yourself, since each trade is statistically unrelated to the last.
- **Wishing, hoping, praying**: a form of passivity to be distinguished from genuine trading intuition.
- **Psycho-Cybernetics**: self-image, not willpower, determines behavior; visualizing successful, rule-following trades reprograms it the way athletes rehearse performance.

## Actionable rules

The author's personal 12-point rule sheet (offered as an example to adapt, not a universal system):
1. Always use a stop order on every trade.
2. Stop trading for the day after three losing trades.
3. Move stop to breakeven once a trade shows 100+ points of profit.
4. Only enter on a technical signal, never on a "feeling" or hunch.
5. Trade long above the Market Profile value area and short below it.
6. Avoid trading on thin holiday-volume days.
7. Always act with best interest in mind on every trade.
8. Use single-tick/Market Profile clues to fine-tune entries.
9. Don't trade every day — skip days with no edge.
10. Wait for the market to show "one-time framing" (a trend-following confirmation) before acting.
11. Keep trading enjoyable; if it stops being so, stop trading.
12. Review the rule sheet after every session and note which rules were broken.

## Caveats

Anecdotal and motivational in style, built on a single author's floor-trading and S&P day-trading experience rather than tested data; the psycho-cybernetics material leans on self-help concepts (subconscious self-image, "deserving" money) that are not empirically verified for trading outcomes. The specific dollar/point figures cited (e.g., $250/day goals, 100-point stop triggers) reflect late-1990s S&P futures point values and are not directly transferable to other markets or eras.

## Who it is for

Newer discretionary or day-traders struggling with discipline and loss-aversion who want a psychology-first framework and a template for writing their own rules, rather than a technical trading system.
