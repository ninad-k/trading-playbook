## Overview

Written by W.D. Gann in the mid-1930s as a follow-up to his earlier books (Truth of the Stock Tape, Wall Street Stock Selector), this short manual responds to the post-1929 regulatory environment (SEC oversight, tighter margin rules, curtailed short selling) by restating and updating his stock-trend rules. It opens with a chapter on how the "New Deal" changed trading conditions, a chapter on the personal qualifications for trading success, and a study of historical bull/bear campaign durations back to the 1870s. The core of the book (Chapters IV-VII) gives concrete, numbered rules for reading Monthly/Weekly/Daily charts, identifying tops and bottoms, confirming trend with volume, and pyramiding a winning position, illustrated with named stocks (Chrysler, United Fruit, Corn Products, Westinghouse, Auburn Motors, Timken Roller Bearing) and the Dow-Jones Industrial and Railroad Averages from 1907-1935.

## Core thesis

Stock trends can be detected — without forecasting exact tops or bottoms in advance — by combining three things: a top-down chart hierarchy (Monthly chart for the main trend, Weekly as secondary confirmation, Daily for timing), price-structure rules built on single/double/triple tops and bottoms with a fixed "3-point" buffer for entries and stops, and volume as a confirming filter (heavy volume near tops warns of distribution, shrinking volume near lows suggests accumulation). Gann is explicit that individual stocks must be traded on their own trend, not assumed to move with the general market averages, and that success depends as much on trader discipline (patience, knowledge, capital, and never averaging a loss) as on the rules themselves.

## Key concepts

- **Chart hierarchy** — Monthly chart for the main/primary trend, Weekly chart as the next-best trend guide, Daily chart for entry and exit timing; used together rather than any single timeframe alone.
- **Single, double, and triple tops/bottoms** — repeated tests of the same price level; a level tested a third time at essentially the same price is treated as especially significant for entries or stops.
- **The 3-point rule** — a fixed buffer (in points, not percent) used both to confirm a breakout/breakdown and to set the corresponding stop-loss (e.g., a buy is confirmed when price crosses a previous high by 1-3 points; the stop sits 3 points below the old top/bottom).
- **"Old tops become bottoms, old bottoms become tops"** — Gann's rule that a broken support or resistance level tends to reverse its role on a later retest.
- **Early leaders** — stocks that top out (or bottom out) ahead of the general market at the start of a bear (or bull) campaign; identified by comparing an individual stock's chart against the Averages.
- **Volume confirmation** — sales volume rising into a top and falling into a bottom is treated as a warning/confirming signal layered on top of the price rules; Chapter VI walks through the NYSE's monthly total volume from 1929-1935 as a worked example.
- **Pyramiding** — adding to a winning position in stages, only after a profit cushion has developed, with position size reduced on later additions.
- **Qualifications for success** — Gann's five stated prerequisites: knowledge (systematic study, not tips or guesswork), patience, nerve, good health, and adequate capital.
- **"Never average a loss"** — a recurring warning against adding to a losing position in hope of a rally, illustrated by the 1929-1932 panic where late buyers who averaged down were wiped out.

## Rules and setups

1. **Trend identification.** Determine the main trend from the Monthly high/low chart; use the Weekly chart as secondary confirmation; use the Daily chart only for timing entries/exits in stocks that are actively traded on meaningful volume — Gann explicitly says to leave alone stocks that trade in a narrow range on low volume until they break out.
2. **Buy near support.** Buy near a single, double, or triple bottom; protect with a stop-loss order no more than 3 points below the bottom.
3. **Sell against resistance.** Sell against a single, double, or triple top; protect with a stop-loss order no more than 3 points above the top.
4. **Breakout entry.** Buy when a stock crosses a previous high (a prior peak, or the high of a previous year) by 1-3 points, confirming the uptrend is resumed; stop placed 3 points below the breakout/old top.
5. **Breakdown entry.** Sell short when a stock breaks an important previous low (or a prior year's low) by 3 points; stop placed 3 points above the broken level.
6. **Old-level role reversal.** After a stock breaks to new low levels and later rallies back above the old bottom by more than 3 points, treat that as an indication the decline is over and the stock is turning up (and the mirror case for a broken top).
7. **Time-to-hold rule.** If a new position closes against you for three consecutive days, exit — you are likely wrong or trading against the trend; a profit showing through the third day is treated as confirmation you are positioned with the trend.
8. **Volume confirmation (Rule 4).** After the first sharp advance (or decline) off a low (or high), expect a secondary reaction; if that reaction happens on decreased volume and the subsequent move resumes on increased volume, treat the trend as confirmed rather than exhausted.
9. **Pyramiding (Rule 5 — "How to Pyramid").** For stocks trading roughly between 20 and 50: after the first trade shows about 5 points of profit, add a second lot and place a stop that locks in no loss on the combined position; wait for a further 10 points of favorable movement before adding another lot; after the fourth or fifth lot, reduce the size of each additional unit (e.g., step down from 50-share to 10-share increments).
10. **Re-entry.** If stopped out, wait for the same entry condition (a fresh breakout, or a new bottom/top formation) to be satisfied again before re-entering.
11. **Never average a loss.** Add only to winning positions (per the pyramiding rule); never add to a losing position hoping for a reversal.

## Risk and money management

Every entry rule pairs with an explicit stop-loss of 1-3 points beyond the triggering level (an old top/bottom or a breakout point) — a fixed point distance, not a percentage of capital or of the stock's price, so the implied percentage risk is smaller on higher-priced stocks and larger on cheap ones. Position size is scaled up only through the pyramiding rule (adding lots after a profit cushion of about 5 points, then further 10-point intervals), and scaled back down on later additions, which limits the size of the most speculative (latest) lots in a pyramid. Gann repeatedly warns against buying stocks outright without a stop ("the only time it is safe to buy stocks outright is when they cannot lose on it... that is just where they are wrong") and against averaging into a losing position.

## Psychology and discipline

Chapter II ("Foundation for Successful Trading") lays out five explicit qualifications: knowledge (systematic study — Gann suggests 30-60 minutes a day over years — rather than trading on tips, broker opinions, or guesswork), patience (waiting for a trend change and never "bucking the trend"), nerve (knowledge converted into the confidence to act at the right time, free of hope or fear), good health, and sufficient capital. The book's most vivid discipline lesson is historical: a section on "who bought stocks in the panic" of 1929-1932 describes traders who bought after declines on the mistaken belief a stock was "cheap" simply because it was down from its highs, held through further losses out of hope, and were ultimately forced out — used to argue for mechanical stop-losses and against averaging down.

## Chapter map

- Ch. I — A New Deal in Wall Street — how post-1929 regulation (margin requirements, curtailed short selling, SEC oversight) changed trading conditions.
- Ch. II — Foundation for Successful Trading — the five qualifications for success: knowledge, patience, nerve, health, capital.
- Ch. III — History Repeats — study of bull/bear campaign durations from the 1870s through 1935, used to flag future time windows to watch for trend changes.
- Ch. IV — Individual Stocks vs. Averages — trading each stock's own trend rather than assuming it tracks the index, with paired stock comparisons (e.g., Chrysler vs. United Fruit).
- Ch. V — New Rules to Detect Trend of Stocks — the core trend-detection rules: chart hierarchy, single/double/triple tops and bottoms, the 3-point rule, identifying early bull/bear-market leaders.
- Ch. VI — Volume of Sales — using the NYSE's monthly volume record (1929-1935) as a confirming/warning indicator near tops and bottoms.
- Ch. VII — A Practical Trading Method — concrete breakout entries, stop placement, and the Rule 5 pyramiding method.
- Ch. VIII — Future Trend of Stocks — a forward-looking market call for the period following the book's writing (not covered in the sampled OCR pages).

## Strengths and caveats

Strength: the sampled material is unusually concrete for a Gann text — explicit point-based stops, a numbered pyramiding rule, and dated, named case studies — with no astrological or numerological content appearing in the sampled pages (Gann's reputation for planetary/esoteric methods comes from other works). Caveats: these notes are based on OCR of a sampled subset of pages, and several sampled pages extracted in scrambled reading order (a common artifact of column-based scans), so some rule text was reconstructed from surrounding context rather than quoted directly — treat the exact wording of any single rule as approximate. One page fragment references "Twenty [-some] Rules" on pages 18-19 of Gann's separate book, Wall Street Stock Selector; that numbered rule list is not contained in this book, so no "24 rules" list could be recovered here despite the topic being commonly associated with Gann. All stop distances and price levels are quoted in absolute points calibrated to 1930s stock prices (mostly $20-150/share, pre-decimalization) and would need conversion to percentage terms for modern instruments; no backtested win rate or drawdown statistics are given anywhere — support is entirely narrated historical chart examples.

## Who should read it

Traders interested in classical, rule-based price/volume trend-following as practiced before modern indicators existed, and who want Gann's more concrete, testable side (stop-loss discipline, pyramiding) rather than his esoteric reputation. Not for beginners: the terminology, share-price scale, and regulatory references are all specific to 1930s U.S. equities and require translation to be applied directly today.
