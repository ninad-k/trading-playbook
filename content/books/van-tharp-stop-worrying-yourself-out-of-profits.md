## Summary

An excerpt from Van Tharp's course volume "How to Control Stress to Become a More Successful Investor." Tharp distinguishes two components of trading stress: a biological fight-flight response (useful for physical survival, not for markets) and a psychological component — worry — which he argues does far more damage to performance. Using an illustrative memory-recall exercise (fifteen two-digit numbers, harder to recall under simulated pressure of fines for mistakes), he argues worry consumes limited conscious processing capacity that would otherwise go toward reading the market accurately, and that worry also distorts perception itself, causing traders to selectively notice information matching their fears. Five hypothetical commodity traders reacting differently to the same 20-cent soybean price drop illustrate that profit/loss outcome and emotional experience are independent — what matters is each trader's interpretation, not the event itself. The article closes with two practical exercises for identifying a trader's personal "worry loop" (self-talk and imagery that reinforces itself) so it can be deliberately interrupted and replaced.

## Key points

- Two components of trading stress: the biological fight-flight response (physiological arousal, narrows focus) and the psychological component, worry (fear of failure and negative self-evaluation).
- Worry consumes conscious processing capacity: research cited shows students who worry about test performance devote roughly 25% of conscious thought to the worry itself rather than the task.
- Worry is self-fulfilling in Tharp's framing: "if you worry about what will happen if you make a mistake, you probably will make that mistake."
- Perception is an active filter shaped by expectation, not a passive recording of reality — a trader expecting a bull market will selectively notice bullish chart patterns and discount contrary evidence; worry is a negative-expectation version of this same filtering.
- The five-trader soybean vignette: three of five traders who actually lost money had a neutral or positive emotional reaction (because they followed their plan or predicted the move), while some traders in profit were anxious — showing P&L outcome and emotional distress are not the same thing.
- Worriers get caught in a self-reinforcing negative feedback loop: negative self-talk produces bad feelings, which produce more negative self-talk.
- Two techniques to identify a personal worry pattern: (1) replaying a past painful trading loss as if watching a movie of yourself, to trace what triggered the worry; (2) keeping a real-time "worry diary" logging the trigger, the self-talk/imagery, and the resulting loop each time worry occurs.
- Once the pattern is identified, Tharp recommends deliberately disrupting it (e.g., following a negative thought with a different voice, image, or action) and rehearsing the replacement until it becomes automatic.

## Actionable rules

1. When you notice worry arising while trading, do not act on the position decision until you consciously name the worry (this preserves processing capacity for market information).
2. Keep a worry diary: log the trigger, the self-talk/imagery, and the resulting emotional loop each time it happens, then review it after several weeks for patterns.
3. Use the "movie replay" technique on a specific past loss to identify exactly what triggered your worry pattern before the loss occurred.
4. Deliberately interrupt an identified worry loop with a different behavior (a changed inner voice, a different image, a physical action) and rehearse the replacement until automatic.
5. "None given" for any market/position-sizing numeric rules — this is purely a psychology and self-management technique.

## Caveats

Short (5 pages) and purely psychological — no market rules, position sizing, or system content; the memory-capacity research it cites is described generally ("research suggests," "research shows") without full citation detail in the excerpt. It is designed to be applied alongside an existing trading system and risk plan, not as a standalone strategy.

## Who it is for

Traders who recognize a pattern of worry impairing their decision-making or sleep, looking for a structured self-diagnosis and behavior-change exercise rather than a market technique.
