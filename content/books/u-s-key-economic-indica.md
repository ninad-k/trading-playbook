## Summary

A reference document profiling roughly 30 major US economic data releases (Auto and Truck Sales, Business Inventories, Chicago PMI, Consumer Confidence, Construction Spending, Consumer Credit, CPI, Durable Goods Orders, Employment Cost Index, and more through to GDP, ISM/NAPM, employment, and housing releases). Each entry gives a letter grade (A-F) for market-moving importance, the releasing agency, release time/schedule, and a short explanation of what the number measures and how it feeds forecasts of related releases (e.g., unit auto sales feeding retail sales and PCE estimates).

## Key points

- **Format per indicator** — Importance grade (A-F), Source agency, Release Time (time of day and day-of-month convention), and an "In Brief"/"In Depth" explanation of methodology and market relevance.
- **CPI** graded B+; released ~8:30 ET around the 13th of the month; the "core rate" (ex food and energy) is emphasized as the more closely watched figure, with year/year core CPI called the best measure of underlying inflation.
- **Auto and Truck Sales** graded C-; a leading indicator (~25% of retail sales) but reported piecemeal by individual manufacturers over the first three business days, making it hard for the market to react to quickly.
- **Employment Cost Index** graded B+; gained importance after Fed Chairman Greenspan referenced it in 1996; adds an industry-mix adjustment and benefit-cost data the monthly hourly-earnings figure lacks.
- **Regional manufacturing surveys** — the document ranks the Chicago PMI (91% correlation to the national NAPM/ISM index) and Philadelphia Fed Index (76-78% correlation) as the two most useful regional surveys for forecasting the national manufacturing index, ahead of Milwaukee, Detroit, New York, and APICS surveys.
- **Consumer Confidence (Conference Board)** graded B-; based on a 5,000-household survey; only moves of 5+ points are considered meaningful; expectations sub-index (60% weight) is a better leading indicator than the current-conditions sub-index (40% weight).
- **Construction Spending** graded D; monthly figures are volatile with large revisions, so only 3+ month trends are considered significant.
- **Consumer Credit** graded D-; released after nearly every other consumer-spending indicator, so the market rarely reacts to it.
- **NAPM/ISM index composition** — a composite of five sub-indices weighted new orders 30%, production 25%, employment 20%, supplier deliveries 15%, inventories 10%; 50% is the expansion/contraction breakeven line.

## Actionable rules

1. Use the letter-grade ranking to prioritize which scheduled releases justify pre-positioning or caution around the calendar (A-/A grades such as CPI-adjacent and top-tier releases warrant more attention than D/F-graded ones).
2. For manufacturing-sector forecasting, weight the Chicago PMI and Philadelphia Fed surveys most heavily among regional surveys when anticipating the national ISM/NAPM print, since both are released before it.
3. Treat single-month moves in low-grade indicators (Construction Spending, Consumer Credit, Business Inventories) as noise; only act on multi-month trends.
4. When reading CPI, focus on the core (ex food/energy) year-over-year change rather than the volatile headline month-over-month number.
5. No position-sizing, entry, or stop-loss rules are given; this is a data-interpretation reference, not a trading system.

## Caveats

The source file has no author, title-page date, or publisher metadata — release-time conventions and agency URLs (e.g., census.gov, bls.gov paths) suggest a compilation likely dating from the early-to-mid 2000s, and some release-day conventions or agency page structures may since have changed. Importance grades reflect one commentator's (unattributed) judgment rather than a rigorously tested market-impact study.

## Who it is for

Traders and analysts who want a fast reference for which US economic releases matter most, when they come out, and how they interact with each other (e.g., auto sales feeding retail sales feeding PCE) when building a macro news calendar.
