## Summary

Question: can a multi-agent "game" model trained purely on a price series' history be run forward to predict future movement? Data: hourly USD/JPY, 1990–1999. Method: the authors treat an observed price series as if produced by an unknown "black-box" multi-agent game (their Grand Canonical Minority Game), fit a "third-party" game to the historical data, then drive it forward to generate next-tick predictions and multi-step probability "corridors." Finding: the trained game predicted next-hour USD/JPY direction correctly ~54% of the time (vs. ~50% for a genuine random walk control), and a naive trading rule using its predictions substantially outperformed acting on the same information independently. Ten-step-ahead corridors bracketed roughly half of the large subsequent price swings within tight, high-confidence bands.

## Key points

- The model (Grand Canonical Minority Game): agents choose buy/sell based on which strategy recently won; the minority side wins each round.
- The simulated game reproduces real-market "stylized facts": fat tails, volatility clustering, volume autocorrelation.
- The fitted game achieved ~54% next-hour direction accuracy on real USD/JPY versus ~50% on a random-walk control.
- A naive hourly trading rule using the collective prediction beat the same capital split across independently-acting agents.
- Multi-step forecasts are probability "corridors," not point forecasts; about half of large observed swings occurred within narrow, high-confidence corridors.
- The authors argue most high-frequency movement is self-generated by agent interaction rather than driven by external news.
- Results are explicitly preliminary; the reported profit excludes transaction costs, which the authors call prohibitive in reality.

## Actionable rules

None as a tradable system — this is exploratory research demonstrating an approach, not entry/exit rules, and the 54% edge excludes costs. Takeaways: agent-based/game-theoretic models are a legitimate alternative to statistical time-series models for short-horizon FX direction forecasting, and probabilistic "corridor" forecasts may be more useful than single-point predictions for gauging when large moves are likelier.

## Caveats

A very short paper (7 pages) with a small, dated dataset and no out-of-sample validation on other pairs or eras. The authors note a patent covers the full methodology and that key implementation details are "presented elsewhere," not in this document. The reported edge is modest and, by the authors' own admission, ignores transaction costs.

## Who it is for

Quant researchers interested in agent-based/econophysics approaches to short-horizon FX prediction, as an entry point into Minority-Game-style modeling rather than a source of deployable rules.
