## Overview

Myles Wilson Walker, a full-time trader since 1986, wrote this book to convert Elliott Wave analysis from a subjective, retrospective interpretation exercise into a real-time, executable trading method. The book is short on prose and heavy on annotated chart diagrams (203 pages carry under 15,000 words of running text), which matches its stated purpose: teach a visual pattern library rather than a theory of markets. After covering standard Elliott Wave mechanics (impulse and corrective wave rules, alternation, degree, zigzags, flats, triangles) in the first four chapters, the book's core contribution is a catalog of eleven specific "C wave" patterns — the shape a corrective wave's final leg takes just before it ends — each with a defined entry-stop and stop-loss rule. The final chapters walk through real charts (N.Y. Silver, Deutschemark, S&P/OEX) trading the method live, bar by bar.

## Core thesis

Standard Elliott Wave analysis fails traders in real time because different analysts count waves differently and only agree in hindsight. Walker's claim is that if you narrow the goal to recognizing the end of the C wave (the final leg of any A-B-C correction) using a fixed set of pattern shapes, wave counting becomes objective enough that independent traders looking at the same chart reach the same entry and stop levels. The C wave patterns are presented as self-contained: they do not require confirmation from other indicators or fundamental inputs.

## Key concepts

- **Impulse wave** — a five-wave move (1-2-3-4-5) in the direction of the larger trend; must satisfy fixed rules (wave 3 is never the shortest of 1/3/5; wave 4 does not overlap wave 1's price territory; waves 2 and 4 show alternation in price, time, or pattern).
- **Corrective wave (A-B-C)** — a three-wave move against the trend; wave C is the leg Walker's method targets for entry.
- **C wave pattern** — one of eleven defined end-of-correction shapes (Patterns 1–11) used to time entry as wave C completes.
- **Alternation** — the rule that wave 2 and wave 4 of an impulse must differ meaningfully in price, time, or internal structure; used to validate or reject a wave count.
- **Degree** — the relative scale of a wave (e.g., a wave on a monthly chart versus one on a 5-minute chart); waves must be grouped by matching degree before counting.
- **Extension** — one impulse leg (1, 3, or 5) running unusually long relative to the other two; complicates profit-taking if misjudged.
- **Zigzag** — a simple A-B-C correction where wave B retraces no more than 61.8% of wave A and wave C fully retraces wave B.
- **Flat** — an A-B-C correction where wave B retraces more than 61.8% (up to 138.2%) of wave A, and wave C is at least 38.2% of wave B.
- **Diagonal triangle** — a five-wave overlapping structure that appears at the end of a wave 5 or wave C and typically precedes a major move.
- **Strong B wave (running correction)** — Patterns 6–9, where wave B extends well beyond the start of wave A, disguising the correction as a continuation of the prior trend.
- **Midpoint method** — an alternative wave-counting technique using only the midpoint of each day's range to smooth noise; used as a supplementary tool, not the primary method.

## Rules and setups

The book's tradable method — the eleven C wave patterns, their entry/stop rules, and the associated money-management approach — is documented in full as a system sub-page: [[walker-myles-wilson-how-to-indentify-high-profit-elliott-wave-trades-in-real-time--c-wave-method]]. In summary, every pattern shares the same entry mechanic (a stop order placed one tick beyond the price bar labeled "d" or "4" in the pattern) and the same stop-loss mechanic (one tick beyond the bar labeled "e" or "5"), with the specific wave-shape criteria varying by pattern number.

## Risk and money management

Walker gives no percent-of-capital risk rule, fixed stop distance, or position-sizing formula. Risk control is entirely structural: the stop-loss is always placed one tick beyond the extreme of the pattern's final sub-wave (wave e or wave 5), so risk is defined by the pattern's own geometry rather than a fixed dollar or percentage amount. Profit-taking is based on a percentage of exchange margin requirement (e.g., roughly 50% of margin on the S&P, 100% on currencies) rather than a fixed price target or Fibonacci projection, on the reasoning that margin scales with volatility and therefore keeps profit targets realistic across changing conditions. The book recommends trading two contracts per signal: one closed at the margin-based profit target for a quick gain, the other held and pyramided using subsequent C wave patterns as a longer-term trend position, reversing only when a new opposing pattern signals.

## Psychology and discipline

The book has little explicit psychology content; its discipline case is implicit in the insistence on objective rules. Walker repeatedly warns against forcing a trade when the pattern is not clean ("if the ideal pattern does not form, do not take the trade — save your capital for a better situation") and against holding a fixed long-term directional view that overrides what the current C wave pattern is signaling ("do not get locked into a view of market direction through your long-term analysis... trade all the C wave patterns as long as all the rules are met"). The chapter on Alternate Counts and Recounts frames being wrong about a wave count as routine and non-threatening, provided the trader stays with whatever pattern the market currently confirms rather than defending a prior interpretation.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Basics of Counting Waves — introduces wave direction, sub-waves, and the midpoint method of wave counting.
- Ch 2 — Elliott Wave Principles — full impulse-wave and corrective-wave rulebook: alternation, degree, extensions, zigzags, flats, elongated flats, triangles, diagonal triangles.
- Ch 3 — Guidelines for Counting Waves — practical wave-counting workflow and terminology.
- Ch 4 — Logic of Counting Waves — worked chart examples applying the counting rules.
- Ch 5 — C Wave Patterns — the core pattern catalog: Patterns 1–5 (standard C wave endings) and Patterns 6–9 (strong/running B wave variants), each with entry and stop rules.
- Ch 6 — How To Find C Wave Patterns — additional chart examples for pattern recognition practice.
- Ch 7 — Profit Takes — the margin-percentage profit-taking method and rationale.
- Ch 8 — Alternate Counts and Recounts — handling wrong counts, trading two contracts, large/extended C waves.
- Ch 9 — C Wave Patterns on Daily and Intraday Charts — extended worked examples on N.Y. Silver and Deutschemark spot.
- Ch 10 — C Wave Method in Action — a full real-time trade walkthrough (entry, stop adjustments, pyramiding) on the S&P/OEX.
- Appendix I — Fibonacci in a Nutshell — Fibonacci price and time ratio targets, explicitly framed as a secondary indicator, not a standalone method.
- Appendix II — Daily Worksheet — a checklist template for applying the method to a live chart.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's strength is specificity: unlike most Elliott Wave literature, every pattern comes with a stated entry-stop and stop-loss rule referencing named bars, which is unusually close to codeable. The "Trade Strategy" chapter is honest about the method's limits — it explicitly says not to trade when the pattern is unclear, rather than forcing a signal. The weaknesses are inherent to Elliott Wave itself: pattern classification still requires subjective judgment about wave degree and which bars count as pivots, alternate counts are acknowledged as common, and no backtested win rate, sample size, or drawdown statistics are given anywhere in the text — the method's edge is asserted from the author's trading experience, not demonstrated with data. The book is undated in its examples relative to modern markets (charts reference 1980s–1990s futures and currency data, pre-decimalization and pre-electronic execution), so tick-based entry/stop placement may need adaptation for today's price granularity and transaction costs.

## Who should read it

Best suited to traders who already have basic Elliott Wave literacy (impulse/corrective structure, Fibonacci ratios) and want a narrower, more mechanical entry framework than general wave theory provides. Not a good starting point for a complete beginner to Elliott Wave, since the pattern catalog assumes familiarity with terms like "alternation" and "degree" introduced only briefly before the pattern chapters begin.
