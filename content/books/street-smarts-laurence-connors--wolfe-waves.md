## What it is

A geometric wave pattern credited to trader Bill Wolfe, based on the idea that price action follows a version of Newton's action-reaction principle, producing a recognizable five-point wave whose first three points can be used to project both a reversal entry (point 5) and a price target (the "Estimated Price at Arrival," or EPA). It works across timeframes — the authors cite finding three to six setups a week on 5-minute S&P charts alone — and is presented as one of the most distinctive patterns in the book, requiring practice to train the eye to spot it.

## Rules

**Wave labeling for a buy setup** (reversed for a sell setup, counting from a bottom instead of a top):
1. Start counting at a top: wave 2 is that top.
2. Wave 3 is the bottom of the first decline following wave 2.
3. Wave 1 is the bottom that preceded wave 2 (the top); point 3 must be lower than point 1.
4. Wave 4 is the top of the bounce following wave 3; on the best-quality setups, point 4 should be higher than the point-1 bottom (this rules out an outright runaway market).

**Entry and target:**
5. Draw a trend line connecting points 1 and 3, and extend it forward — its extension projects the anticipated reversal point, wave 5, which is also the entry area. Enter when price reverses back above (below, for sells) this trend line near the projected point 5, since price commonly overshoots point 5 slightly before turning.
6. Draw a second trend line connecting points 1 and 4 — this is the Estimated Price at Arrival (EPA), the pattern's price target.
7. Place the initial stop just beneath (above, for sells) the newly formed point-5 reversal; move it to breakeven quickly once the trade shows a profit.

**Precondition:** points 1, 2, 3, and 4 must all already be formed before the pattern can be identified and traded — there is no way to anticipate a Wolfe Wave before its first four points exist.

## Risk

The initial stop sits just beyond the point-5 reversal extreme, giving a tight, well-defined risk point relative to the projected move to the EPA target — one worked S&P example shows a 12-point potential gain from a stop placed only a few ticks away. The authors' stated practice of moving the stop to breakeven as soon as the trade shows a profit further limits realized risk on winning trades. No fixed percent-of-equity sizing rule is given; sizing follows the book's general money-management framework.

## Caveats

The pattern depends on correctly identifying which swing points are 1, 2, 3, and 4 before drawing either trend line — a subjective judgment despite the otherwise geometric, rule-based construction, and the authors themselves note the "odd sequence in counting" is unintuitive at first. Points 1 and 4 must satisfy specific relative-height conditions (point 3 lower than point 1 for a buy, point 4 ideally higher than point 1) that filter out extreme trending conditions, meaning the pattern is not meant to fire in a runaway market. No independent backtested win rate, sample size, or drawdown is cited in this chapter; the evidence is a series of annotated S&P (60-minute and 5-minute) and sugar (10-tick) chart examples, and the authors credit the pattern's development entirely to Bill Wolfe and his son Brian rather than to their own original research.
