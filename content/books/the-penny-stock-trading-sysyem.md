## Summary

Donny Lowy's guide surveys the OTC Bulletin Board and Pink Sheets, financial research, corporate developments, and catalyst trading. It defines its penny-stock universe as OTC or Pink Sheet stocks bought below one dollar and acknowledges that large percentage moves can erase capital quickly (PDF pp. 6-11). The closing chapter gives 13 strategies, including reverse mergers, shells, low-float stocks, dips, limit orders, high-volume issues, timed press-release trades, and a fixed loss threshold (PDF pp. 190-214). These are the author's prescriptions and anecdotes, not results from a documented test.

## Key points

- The book's “penny stock” label is market- and price-based, not a pure market-cap definition (PDF pp. 9-11).
- OTC quotation and reporting history matters because disclosure quality determines how much can be verified (PDF pp. 12-13).
- A reverse merger can turn a dormant public shell into an operating company; the author expects early buyers to benefit if the acquired business has value (PDF pp. 190-193).
- The author favors low-float, low-market-cap shells because demand can move them sharply, while acknowledging that the merger may never occur (PDF pp. 193-194, 199).
- Corporate announcements need confirmation in filings and financial statements; press releases alone do not reveal expenses, debt, or cash needs (PDF p. 13).
- Strategy 9 says always use limit orders, and Strategy 11 favors high-volume penny stocks for better liquidity and market-maker competition (PDF pp. 204-209).
- Strategy 12 supplies timed intraday examples; Strategy 13 directs readers to sell at 20% below cost (PDF pp. 209-212).
- **Turnaround situations (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 96-106)** — companies driven toward failure by outside factors (litigation, regulation, boycotts) rather than by the business itself are treated as the most promising recovery candidates, on the argument that the underlying operation may still be sound once the external cause resolves.
- **Float and lock-up arithmetic (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 76-95)** — the book's mechanism for price appreciation is supply withdrawal: shares taken off the market by investors who commit not to sell shrink the float, so steady demand alone lifts the price; early profit-taking is named as the main thing that stalls the move.
- **Investor-relations firms (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 165-179)** — a warning not to credit an IR firm for a price rise that a genuine corporate development (the example given is a 300% revenue increase) would have produced anyway; the causal link has to be established before an IR firm's record means anything.
- **Negative situations (Ch. 12, PDF pp. 180-188)** — reverse splits are flagged as a warning sign: a 100-for-1 reverse split leaves ownership unchanged while multiplying the quoted price by 100 (a $0.03 stock becomes $3.00), so a higher share price after a reverse split carries no information about value.

## Actionable rules

Items 1 and 3 paraphrase Lowy's rules; items 2 and 4 are editorial safeguards added because his examples do not establish predictive performance:

1. For the reverse-merger setup, identify the shell and acquired business, then assess whether the new operations provide value beyond the announcement (PDF pp. 190-194).
2. Verify the post-transaction share structure, float, market capitalization, filings, operating results, and cash requirements.
3. Use limit orders and prefer actively traded issues; the author's separate loss rule exits at 20% below purchase price (PDF pp. 204-212).
4. Treat the precise intraday times, percentage targets, and recovery thresholds as hypotheses requiring new data rather than established edges.

## Caveats

This review covers the market introduction, the corporate-development, turnaround, investor-relations and negative-situation chapters, and the complete strategy chapter. The source supplies precise examples but no sample definition, audited record, position-sizing formula, or evidence for the thresholds. Lowy reports losing 80% in one year and gaining 1,000% the next, without verification (PDF pp. 213-214). Regulations, venues, and execution assumptions are dated.

## Who it is for

Readers studying microcap due diligence and the historical mechanics of OTC catalyst investing.
