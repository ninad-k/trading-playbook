## Summary

A short collection of 15 essay-style chapters (originally a "special report" with a foreword by Bill Poulos) making a single, repeated argument: trading systems are rarely the reason traders lose money — a lack of emotional discipline is. Each chapter uses a narrative scenario (a trader second-guessing a stop, over-trading after a small win, chasing a guru) to illustrate a specific psychological trap — limiting winners while letting losers run, overconfidence from a lucky streak, treating "risk capital" as truly disposable money, poor workspace/routine habits, and skipping self-review after trades. The recurring prescription is to build a daily mental-discipline practice (reading psychology material, reviewing personal rules, journaling) with the same consistency as running the trading system itself.

## Key points

- Losing is not the same as having a bad system: the author claims to know 50+ "A+" systems that most of their users still lost money on, because of emotional decision-making, not the system's edge.
- "Limit your losses and let your winners run" is easy to say and hard to execute — the natural human tendency is the reverse (cutting winners early from fear, holding losers hoping for a reversal).
- A single "huge winning trade" people fixate on is usually a distraction — it breeds impatience and overtrading while waiting for the next one instead of executing the plan consistently.
- Coping with losses well (accepting them as a cost of doing business) is described as the single biggest determinant of long-run survival.
- Overconfidence after a string of wins ("I see the future") is flagged as a direct precursor to the next large loss.
- New traders are described as vulnerable to being "fed on" by more experienced market participants (informational and psychological disadvantage), reinforcing the need for a defensive, rules-based approach.
- Money labeled "risk capital" often isn't treated as truly expendable once it's actually at risk — the psychological reality of losing it differs from the abstract idea of it beforehand.
- Recommended daily practice: read a chapter of a trading-psychology book, review a personal list of trading rules/"commandments," or use mental-training material — every day, before trading begins, not just when things go wrong.
- Workspace and self-talk matter: a cluttered or distracting trading environment and unmanaged internal dialogue are treated as concrete, fixable contributors to poor decisions.
- Keeping a trade journal ("take notes" on yourself versus the market) is presented as necessary to see and correct one's own recurring behavioral patterns over time.

## Actionable rules

1. Perform some form of mental-discipline practice every trading day before the first trade (rule review, journaling, or psychology reading) — not only after a losing streak.
2. Treat "risk capital" designations as a discipline aid, not a psychological free pass; expect and prepare for the real emotional impact of a loss regardless of how the capital was labeled.
3. Pre-commit to stop placement and target rules before entry, and do not override them mid-trade based on a "feeling."
4. Keep a written trade journal that records both market conditions and your own emotional state/decisions at each step, and review it regularly.
5. After a losing trade, resist doubling down or immediately re-entering to "get even" — the text repeatedly frames this reflex as the direct cause of larger subsequent losses.

## Caveats

This is a short promotional report (no page numbers past 36, distributed by "Profits Run") rather than a peer-reviewed or data-backed psychology text; every claim is illustrated by a hypothetical narrative rather than actual trading statistics. The disclaimer explicitly notes the publisher is not a registered investment adviser or broker-dealer. Content is purely psychological/behavioral — it contains no system, entry/exit rules, or money-management formulas of its own.

## Who it is for

New and struggling traders whose losses stem from emotional decision-making rather than a flawed system, and anyone looking for a quick, chapter-a-day psychological discipline primer rather than a technical or quantitative trading text.
