## Overview

Dirk du Toit, a South African forex trader and mentor (founder of DayForex), wrote this book to warn retail traders about the "marketing wizards" of the retail FX industry and to lay out, via a mentor-and-student narrative (the fictionalized student "Joe"), the discretionary approach he calls Real Time Analysis. Four parts: Part 1 frames trading as a business; Part 2 develops the "edge" concept; Part 3 covers FX market mechanics; Part 4 is the practical core - the 4x1 strategy (one currency, one lot/low gearing, one direction, one-percent targets), Median Trading (a self-drawn support/resistance grid used as a "comfort zone"), and Relational Analysis (a Price-Event-Time framework for reading fundamental/sentiment order flow) - followed by risk management. Du Toit is skeptical of most indicators and tight, timing-based stops, favoring small "edges" stacked together: goal-setting, low gearing, patience, staggered entries, discretion.

## Core thesis

No single tool - not technical analysis, fundamentals, or psychology alone - gives a durable edge in spot FX; success comes from accumulating small edges (pre-trading discipline, low gearing, trading with the fundamental trend, a fixed reference grid, patience, discretion) until they compound into a statistical advantage, the way a casino's small edge compounds over many hands. The author argues FX's short-term price action is largely random noise, so traders needing pinpoint entries fight an unwinnable battle against order flow they cannot see. Low gearing (his ceiling is about 3:1 per entry, with total exposure sometimes reaching 10-15:1 across staggered entries) buys the room for multiple entries and cost-averaging, turning volatility into an ally. His most quotable idea: "a stop loss must never be a stop profit" - stops should defend against catastrophic risk, not act as tight mechanical exits that kill profitable trades on noise.

## Key concepts

- **Edge** — any repeatable advantage, from goal-setting and gearing discipline to discretion itself; many small edges stacked together produce a statistical advantage.
- **Real Time Analysis** — du Toit's umbrella term for combining 4x1, median trading, and relational analysis to decide from current information rather than lagging indicators or stale fundamentals.
- **PET (Price-Event-Time)** — the three interacting factors behind every price move, including "internal events" like the trader's own psychological state.
- **Median grid (comfort zone)** — a horizontal support/resistance channel (roughly 300-400 pips on EUR/USD, 400-500 on GBP/USD, ~2.5-3% of price) drawn from ~60-minute charts over 3-4 weeks, with a median band where price spends most of its time.
- **Quadrants (Q1-Q4)** — the grid divided into four equal price bands; Q1/Q2 are primary buying zones in an uptrend, Q3 a cautious continuation zone, Q4 the high-risk "top" zone.
- **One-way play** — a pair whose fundamentals support a clear medium/long-term directional bias, chosen so the trader only ever trades that bias.
- **Gearing (leverage)** — position size ÷ margin; ceiling of roughly 2:1-3:1 per entry, allowing multiple simultaneous entries rather than one large position.
- **Multiple entries / cost averaging** — splitting one "trade" into several "entries" at different prices/times, borrowed from mutual-fund dollar-cost-averaging.
- **One-percent edge** — a profit benchmark (~30-40 pips at 3:1 gearing on a 10K lot) used as a repeatable, bankable target rather than maximizing every trade.
- **Japanese Follow-Through Indicator** — a session-based sentiment read: how Tokyo closed anticipates whether London/New York will "follow through" the same direction.
- **Storyline vs. flavour of the day** — separating the durable fundamental trend (storyline) from short-lived news noise (flavour).

## Rules and setups

1. **Instrument selection**: trade one currency pair at a time (EUR/USD, occasionally GBP/USD) until mastered; avoid cross pairs due to lower liquidity and less transparent drivers.
2. **Directional bias**: establish a medium/long-term fundamental trend view and trade only in that direction (buy dips in an uptrend, never sell the "overbought" top as a counter-trend bet).
3. **Grid construction**: on a 60-minute chart with 3-4 weeks of data, draw horizontal lines at the clearest support/resistance; typical width ~300-400 pips (EUR/USD) or ~400-500 pips (GBP/USD), with a median band in between.
4. **Quadrant entries**: divide the grid into four equal quadrants; buy predominantly in Q1/Q2 (bottom half), treat Q3 as reduced-size continuation, and Q4 (top) as high-risk, breakout-only.
5. **Entry trigger**: enter when price is "high" or "low" relative to recent action, in 20-40 pip zones near support; no attempt to pinpoint an exact tick.
6. **Gearing**: cap at roughly 2:1-3:1 per entry; total exposure across multiple entries can run higher (10:1-15:1 in worked examples) but is built up gradually, never as one large position.
7. **Multiple entries (pyramid)**: scale in as price dips further into the grid (broadening the base, unlike a reverse pyramid that adds size into profit); each entry averages the cost basis.
8. **Profit target**: bank ~30-40 pips (~1% of capital at 3:1 gearing) per entry as a repeatable benchmark; profits can also run through periods out-of-the-money if the setup is unchanged.
9. **Exit rule**: exit for profit whenever an "acceptable" gain exists; exit for loss when the fundamental setup that justified entry no longer holds - not simply because the position is underwater.
10. **Event avoidance**: avoid opening or holding tight positions around major scheduled data releases; step aside or take partial profits into the volatility.

## Risk and money management

Du Toit treats gearing discipline, not the stop-loss order, as the primary risk control: "if you are too highly geared you will fail... understand this and you are nine tenths of the way to trading success." His worked example shows a trader geared 20:1 with 30-pip stops wiped out by a cumulative 175-pip adverse move, versus a 1:1-geared trader who would need a 2,500-pip move for the same fate. Set a maximum account drawdown tolerance before trading (his example: 25% pain threshold) but start actively managing - scaling out, reducing gearing, hedging - at a much earlier trigger (begin the "when" question at 10%, act before 5%). Cost-averaging via multiple entries is the core position-building risk tool. Stops, when used, should sit beyond a real support/resistance level (never a fixed, tight 20-40 pip distance, which he calls too tight for normal volatility) and should never be tightened into a "stop profit." Hedging (an offsetting position in the same pair) is an advanced tool for buying time, not recommended for novices. He reports an 85%+ win rate on managed accounts and rejects the conventional "50% win rate with bigger wins than losses" model in favor of high win-rate, patient position management.

## Psychology and discipline

The book frames trading as a business requiring goal-setting, realistic drawdown expectations, and comfort with randomness before ever placing a trade ("pre-trading edges"). Du Toit argues indicators and mechanical signals fail traders because they outsource judgment that should remain discretionary; his ideal is a "discretionary technical trader" who trusts deliberate judgment over rigid rules. He treats his own emotional state as data within PET - a personal event (stress, a run of losses) is as real as an interest-rate announcement - and recounts deliberately stopping trading for weeks when his own state of mind was unreliable. Patience is a trainable skill tied to the one-directional, multiple-entry approach: because entries aren't timed precisely, the trader must tolerate extended periods out-of-the-money without abandoning a sound setup, while recognizing the specific point at which the original thesis has actually failed.

## Strengths and caveats

The gearing/risk-management chapters are unusually concrete and numerically worked, which is rarer in discretionary-trading books than in mechanical-system books. The core critique of high leverage and tight, timing-based stops in a volatile, decentralized market is sound. However, the method is fundamentally discretionary and admits it: entries and exits rest on the author's subjective read of "high or low relative to recent price action" and on judging whether a "fundamental setup" still holds - not mechanically testable as written. Some content is dated - the EUR/USD grid widths, 2002-2004 examples, and the assumption of easy 100:1+ retail leverage reflect the mid-2000s environment. The author states he has not backtested the system at time of writing, and flags but doesn't resolve curve-fitting risk in his own ongoing automation project. The conversational, anecdote-heavy mentor/student style requires patience to extract the specific rules from.

## Who should read it

Discretionary FX day traders who are over-leveraged or over-reliant on tight mechanical stops and lagging indicators, and who want a structured way to combine trend direction, a self-drawn reference grid, and macro/event awareness. Less suited to systematic/quant traders wanting a backtestable, rules-only system, since key decisions are explicitly left to trader judgment.
