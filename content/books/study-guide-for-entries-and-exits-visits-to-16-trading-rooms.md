## Overview

This is a workbook, not a narrative book: 101 scored multiple-choice and multi-select questions built around the sixteen traders Elder profiled in his companion volume, *Entries & Exits*. Part One groups the questions into six chapters — Organization, Psychology, Markets, Trading Tactics, Money Management and Record-Keeping, and Case Studies — followed by a short "Traders Speak" section of quotes attributed to the interviewees. A back section, "Answers and Rating Scales," explains every answer and gives a points-based grading table per chapter so a reader can score their own trading readiness. The Case Studies chapter is the core of the book: real annotated charts from the sixteen traders' actual setups, asking the reader to identify signals and make an entry, stop, or exit decision at the "foggy right edge" of the chart before seeing what happened next.

## Core thesis

Reading about trading rules is not the same as being able to apply them under uncertainty. The book's premise is that trading competence should be tested, not just studied — by forcing a decision on a real, unresolved chart, under scoring, the same way the sixteen interviewed traders had to decide in real time. It assumes and reuses the technical vocabulary of Elder's other books (Triple Screen, Impulse System, Force Index, EMA-based trend reading) rather than introducing a new method.

## Key concepts

- **The "right edge" problem** — signals are clear in the middle of a chart but foggy at the current (right) edge; professional trading is defined by the ability to act well under that uncertainty rather than waiting for clarity that arrives too late.
- **Scored self-assessment** — each question chapter has a maximum-points table (multiple trial columns) so a reader can retake the quiz and track improvement over time.
- **Businessman's risk vs. rule violation** — the money-management chapter tests the distinction (carried over from Elder's other books) between an accepted, planned loss and an undisciplined breach of a trading rule.
- **Impulse System reading** — several case studies test whether the reader can read the color-coded state (bullish/bearish/neutral) of Elder's 13-day EMA + MACD-Histogram Impulse System across weekly and daily timeframes and resolve conflicts between them.
- **Force Index divergence** — case studies ask readers to spot bullish/bearish divergence between Force Index and price as a reversal signal.
- **Kangaroo tail** — a chart-pattern answer referenced in a case study (a sharp, quickly reversed price spike) used as a reversal/exhaustion signal.
- **False breakout** — case studies repeatedly test whether a break to a new high/low is genuine or a stop-run trap, using indicator confirmation.
- **Track record for a small account** — the Organization chapter tests the idea that a trader with limited capital should prioritize building a verifiable track record over trying to extract meaningful income from a tiny account.
- **Traders Speak** — a set of statements the reader must correctly attribute to real interviewed traders versus plausible-sounding but wrong advice, testing whether the reader has absorbed the interviewees' actual philosophy rather than generic trading folklore.

## Rules and setups

The book teaches by testing, so its "rules" are the trading principles embedded in the correct answers to specific numbered questions (only a sample of question/answer pairs was recovered from the OCR scan; numbering below follows the book's own question numbers):

1. **Safety before profits (Q69)**: always place a protective stop order before a "take profit" order; if a broker doesn't support one-cancels-other (OCO) orders, consider switching brokers.
2. **Order discipline (Q69)**: use limit orders to avoid slippage; switching from a limit to a market order once a stock starts running is amateurish; never place orders from memory in a hurry (traders have accidentally reversed buy/sell orders this way).
3. **Life-saving rules of a serious trader (Q15)**: use money-management rules, never add to a losing position, never let a meaningful profit turn into a loss, and always use a protective stop.
4. **Trade frequency and learning (Q14)**: trading more frequently can speed up learning, but only if trade size is kept small and good records are maintained — frequency without small size and record-keeping is not a shortcut.
5. **Dealing with right-edge uncertainty (Q18)**: wait for indicator signals to be confirmed by price action (a broken trendline, support, or resistance level) rather than acting on the indicator alone; a foggy signal traded with a tight stop is preferable to a clear signal traded with a wide stop.
6. **Reading trend from a rising EMA (Q91)**: a rising long-term EMA is supportive of a long bias but is explicitly flagged as an incomplete/trap answer on its own — case studies expect corroboration (e.g., expanding ranges, position relative to prior extremes) before treating "EMA rising" as a standalone buy signal.
7. **Weekly/daily Impulse System conflict rule (Answer 86 case study)**: it is acceptable to act on a daily-timeframe signal when the weekly Impulse System is neutral (no conflict); a genuine conflict exists only when the weekly and daily signals point in opposite directions, in which case a discretionary trader relying on preponderance of evidence takes more risk than a strict system trader who would already be stopped out.
8. **Position sizing prompt (Q59)**: the money-management chapter poses a worked example (a $74,000 account, a $7 stock, a hard stop at $5.60) to test whether the reader can size a position from account equity and per-share stop distance; the sampled OCR pages preserved the question but not the worked answer.
9. **Patience (Traders Speak, Q95)**: be patient, be deliberate, wait for the perfect setup — and when it appears, don't hesitate; if no trade is present, do not force one by changing the system.
10. **Trading diary (Traders Speak, Q95)**: keep a diary of thoughts, emotions, and incidents at the time of each trade and review it periodically to surface personal behavior patterns.

## Risk and money management

The dedicated chapter (Questions 59–69) tests position sizing from account equity and stop distance, order-type discipline (limit vs. market, OCO orders), and the mechanics of good record-keeping. The book's own closing note to that chapter states plainly that money management is the weakest area for the majority of traders and that the quality of a trader's records has a greater impact on results than any technical tool or indicator. Beyond the position-sizing example in Question 59 (a $74,000 account, a $7 stock, a stop at $5.60), no additional numeric formula or percentage-risk rule was recovered from the sampled pages; readers are expected to already know the 2%/6% framework from Elder's other books.

## Psychology and discipline

The Psychology chapter (Questions 14–26) centers on activity level (how often to trade), the discipline needed to size trades appropriately as experience grows, and — most substantively — how to act under the market's characteristic uncertainty at the current price edge: wait for price-action confirmation of an indicator signal, and prefer a foggy-but-tightly-stopped trade over a clear-but-widely-stopped one. The "Traders Speak" section reinforces this with attributed statements from the sixteen interviewed traders on patience, not forcing trades, and keeping an emotional/behavioral diary. Because the book is a workbook, its treatment of psychology is entirely example- and quiz-driven rather than expository.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Organization (Q1–13) — setting up a trading business, evaluating black-box systems, allocating study time.
- Ch 2 — Psychology (Q14–26) — trade frequency, activity level, handling uncertainty at the right edge of the chart.
- Ch 3 — Markets (Q27–38) — intermarket relationships (interest rates vs. bonds, dollar vs. euro), sources of trade ideas.
- Ch 4 — Trading Tactics (Q39–58) — reactions to news/earnings, volume and volatility, moving-average signals, long-term chart patterns, system trading.
- Ch 5 — Money Management and Record-Keeping (Q59–69) — position sizing, order types, record quality.
- Ch 6 — Case Studies (Q70–94) — annotated real charts from the sixteen traders' rooms; identify signals and make entry/stop decisions.
- Traders Speak (Q95–101) — attribute real statements from the interviewed traders versus plausible distractors.
- Answers and Rating Scales — full answer explanations plus a per-chapter points/grading table.

## Strengths and caveats

These notes are based on a sampled OCR scan of the source PDF (front matter plus evenly spaced pages); the majority of individual question and answer pages were not captured, so the rules above reflect only the sample that was recovered, not the full 101-question set. As a workbook, the book is explicitly a companion to *Entries & Exits* — the case-study charts and indicator vocabulary (Impulse System, Force Index, EMA slope) are not re-explained here and assume the reader already owns that background from Elder's other books. There is no new trading system introduced; the value is entirely in deliberate-practice repetition against real, previously-unseen setups.

## Who should read it

Readers who have already read Elder's *Entries & Exits* (or *Come Into My Trading Room* / *Trading for a Living*) and want a scored self-test of whether they can apply Elder's indicator vocabulary and money-management discipline to live charts, rather than just recognize it in exposition.
