## Summary

Written by strategist Donald G. M. Coxe in 2003, in the aftermath of the Nasdaq crash, this book argues that major market manias and crashes follow a repeatable pattern he calls a "Triple Waterfall": a cascading three-stage collapse (interrupted by two false-hope rallies) that follows a long build-up of collective over-belief. Coxe traces this pattern through seven historical episodes — including the 1929 crash, the 1970s gold/silver/oil manias, Japan's bubble, and the dotcom Nasdaq crash — then pivots to prescriptive portfolio construction, walking the reader through building a diversified "Survival Pak" across ten asset classes as insurance against the next such cycle.

## Key points

- **Triple Waterfall** — a six-stage market cycle: Optimism (~2 years), Faith (~2 years), Fanaticism (1-2 years), Sudden Shock/First Cascade (6-12 months), Last Chance/Second Cascade (2-10 months), and Long-Term Collapse/Third Cascade (12-20 years); the two intervening rallies during the crash phase lure investors back in to "average down" before the final collapse.
- Coxe identifies seven historical Triple Waterfalls: the Nifty Fifty/1929-style crashes, the 1970s-80s gold, silver, and oil-stock manias, Japan's bubble economy, and the 1997-2002 Nasdaq/telecom crash.
- Two defining features separate a Triple Waterfall from an ordinary bear market: active, deliberate promotion of the mania's belief system by trusted institutions, and a "Shared Mistake" — a collectively held, ultimately false conviction (rather than simple fear/greed cycling).
- **"Di-worsification"** (a term Coxe borrows from Peter Lynch) — the common mistake of owning many overlapping equity funds across styles, which in effect just recreates an index fund at a much higher fee.
- Recommends the "Survival Pak": a portfolio diversified across 10 asset-class buckets — U.S. large-cap equities, U.S. REITs, U.S. small-cap equities, foreign equities, emerging-market equities, U.S. short/medium tax-exempt bonds, U.S. short/medium taxable bonds, U.S. long tax-exempt bonds, U.S. long taxable bonds, and gold (mining shares and/or bullion).
- True diversification, per Coxe, means always holding meaningful exposure to at least one asset class you currently dislike — if you expect good returns from everything you own, you are not actually diversified.
- Distinguishes true "growth stocks" (durable earners like Walgreens, Microsoft, Johnson & Johnson) from capital-spending cyclical stocks mistaken for growth stocks during the tech mania (most dotcom/telecom names).
- Momentum investing is characterized as reliant on the "Greater Fool Theory" and self-reinforcing during a mania, but fails once the largest pool of new buyers ("greater fools") is exhausted.
- Recommends a maximum initial risk-of-ruin mindset rather than chasing outsized single positions; argues capital preservation, not high average returns, is the core determinant of long-run outcomes.

## Actionable rules

None given as tradable entry/exit rules with numbers — this is a macro/portfolio-construction book, not a trading system. The closest actionable guidance: (1) build a portfolio spanning all ten Survival Pak asset-class buckets rather than concentrating in equities alone; (2) avoid "di-worsification" — consolidate overlapping equity-style funds rather than accumulating them; (3) recognize the six-stage Triple Waterfall pattern (particularly the two "relief rally" traps during the crash phase) as a warning against averaging down into a structurally broken mania.

## Caveats

Written in 2003 in the immediate aftermath of the dotcom crash; the specific market levels, interest-rate environment, and named securities (Nasdaq levels, Treasury yields ~5%, dollar-bear-market thesis) are dated to that period and should be read as historical case studies rather than current guidance. The author discloses a financial conflict of interest — his firm sells analytics-based investment management, which he favors in the text. The "Triple Waterfall" framework is Coxe's own pattern-recognition thesis, not an academically validated model, and the book cites uncredited or loosely sourced statistics in places (e.g., historical stock/bond return comparisons).

## Who it is for

Intermediate investors and traders interested in market-cycle history and long-horizon, multi-asset-class portfolio construction rather than short-term trading technique.
