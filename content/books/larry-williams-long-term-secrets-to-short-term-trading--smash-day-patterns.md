## What it is

A short-term (1–3 day) reversal pattern built on the idea that a violent, emotionally driven close beyond the recent range ("smash day") pulls the public into a breakout trade that then immediately fails, trapping them and fueling a reversal. Williams presents two variants — an obvious "naked close" version and a subtler "hidden smash day" version — and uses both in strong trends (as a re-entry/pyramiding signal) and in choppy congestion (as a breakout-confirmation signal). Works on daily bars and, per the author, on 5-minute, 30-minute, and hourly bars as well.

## Rules

**Naked smash day buy setup:** a day closes lower than the previous day's low (often taking out the prior 3–8 days' lows). Entry trigger: the very next day, price trades above the smash day's high — buy.

**Naked smash day sell setup:** a day closes higher than the previous day's high, breaking out to the upside. Entry trigger: the very next day, price trades down to the smash day's low — sell.

**Hidden smash day buy setup:** a day has an up close (not a naked down close), but the close falls in the lower 25% of that day's range and below the day's open. Entry trigger: the next day, price trades above the hidden smash day's high — buy.

**Hidden smash day sell setup:** a day has a down close in the upper 25% of that day's range and above the day's open. Entry trigger: the next day, price trades below the hidden smash day's low — sell.

**Two ways to apply the pattern:**
1. In an established trend: a smash day against the trend direction, followed by the reversal-confirmation trigger, is used as a re-entry / pyramid signal in the direction of the existing trend.
2. In a choppy/congested range: note the smash day and act once its high or low is penetrated — Williams treats this as a likely breakout of the congestion, since the failed reversal "elects" stops and pulls in fresh momentum.

Williams stresses using additional confirmation (TDW/TDM bias, intermarket relationships, or trend context) rather than trading the pattern in isolation, especially on intraday charts.

## Risk

- No single stop-loss figure is given specifically for this pattern in isolation; it is generally traded with the book's standard exit rules (Chapter 11): a fixed dollar stop, the bailout exit (close on first profitable opening), and reverse-on-opposite-signal.
- Because the entry trigger is itself a failure of the smash day's extreme, a logical protective stop is placement just beyond the smash day's opposite extreme (i.e., beyond the low for a buy setup, beyond the high for a sell setup).
- No portfolio-level sizing guidance is given specific to this pattern; use the book's general money-management formula (position size = risk% of equity ÷ largest historical loss per contract).

## Caveats

- No dedicated backtest statistics (win rate, trade count, dollar profit) are given for the smash day pattern alone — unlike Oops! and Greatest Swing Value, which include full backtest tables, this pattern is illustrated with chart examples rather than quantified performance.
- The "hidden" variant requires subjective judgment about what counts as "upper/lower 25% of the range," which is more prone to inconsistent identification than the naked version's objective high/low breach.
- Williams explicitly cautions that on shorter time frames the pattern should not be traded on price structure alone — it needs a secondary filter (trend, TDW/TDM, or intermarket data) to avoid low-quality signals.
