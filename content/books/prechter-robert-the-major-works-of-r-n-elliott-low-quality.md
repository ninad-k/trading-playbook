## Summary

This second-edition collection combines Robert Prechter's biography of R. N. Elliott with Elliott's *The Wave Principle*, 1939 *Financial World* articles, and *Nature's Law* (pp. 1-4). It therefore documents both the technical pattern language and the broader natural-law interpretation Elliott later attached to it. The sampled material presents a directional advance as five waves—three with the trend separated by two corrections—while treating corrective movements as varied three-wave or compound structures (pp. 30, 70, 92).

Prechter's editorial footnotes matter because they sometimes tighten or correct Elliott's early wording. For example, a note on the overlap discussion says wave four should not fall below wave one's peak, and another replaces “rarely” with “never” for complete retracement of the prior motive wave if the method is to remain consistent (p. 30). The collection is thus a historical primary source with later editorial interpretation, not a clean modern rulebook.

## Key points

- A normal five-wave advance has waves one, three, and five in the trend direction, with waves two and four corrective (p. 30).
- Elliott describes zigzags, flats, irregular corrections, and triangles; their internal forms differ (pp. 70, 92).
- A larger triangle consists of five legs, with each leg itself potentially subdividing into three waves (p. 92).
- The alternation guideline expects waves two and four to differ in corrective form (p. 98).
- Elliott connected the Wave Principle with Fibonacci ideas during the early 1940s, after the market framework was already developing (p. 18).

## Actionable rules

1. For the book's normal five-wave advance, expect wave three to exceed wave one's endpoint and wave five to exceed wave three's endpoint; treat this as an initial classification test rather than a universal law, because the collection also discusses rare wave failures (p. 30).
2. Do not allow wave four to overlap below wave one's peak in the ordinary impulse interpretation described by Prechter's editorial note (p. 30).
3. If wave two is structurally simple, look for wave four to be complex, and vice versa; use alternation as a guideline rather than a standalone entry trigger (p. 98).

## Caveats

Only the OCR-recovered pages listed above were reviewed, and the scan combines facing book pages on each PDF page. The texts use historical examples and subjective pattern labels, offer no transaction-cost study, and sometimes contain statements that the editor qualifies. Fibonacci and natural-law discussions are interpretive rather than empirical validation.

## Who it is for

Readers studying the original development of Elliott-wave terminology and its later editorial codification.
