## Summary

This is a second-year PhD textbook (from the author's Ohio State graduate lecture notes) that develops and empirically tests the major models used to explain exchange rates and international capital flows. It moves from institutional background and time-series econometrics (VAR, GMM, unit roots, cointegration) through flexible-price models (monetary model, Lucas asset-pricing model, international real-business-cycle models) to two central anomalies — deviations from uncovered interest parity (UIP) and purchasing-power parity (PPP) — before covering sticky-price models (Mundell-Fleming), new open-economy macroeconomics, and target-zone/fixed-rate collapse models. Each chapter derives a canonical model's testable predictions, then confronts them with calibration or econometric evidence on mostly G7 data. Finding: none of the workhorse models fit the data cleanly — two anomalies dominate the field.

## Key points

- **Monetary model** of exchange rates (PPP plus a stable money-demand function) is rejected out of sample; it does not out-forecast a random walk over short horizons.
- **Forward-premium bias / UIP puzzle**: regressing exchange-rate changes on the interest-rate differential yields a slope coefficient well below the predicted 1, often negative, for most currency pairs (dollar-deutschemark is a partial exception) — high-interest currencies tend to appreciate rather than depreciate as UIP predicts.
- **Lucas asset-pricing model** can rationalize a UIP-violating risk premium in principle, but only with implausibly high risk aversion given observed exchange-rate volatility.
- **Peso-problem and noise-trader models** offer two competing explanations — rational regime-shift risk vs. non-fully-rational trading — for these persistent deviations.
- **PPP puzzle**: real exchange rates do eventually mean-revert (panel unit-root tests reject a permanent random walk), but the estimated half-life of a deviation is roughly 1.7 to 5.3 years, far slower than nominal price stickiness alone predicts.
- **Mundell-Fleming and its dynamic/stochastic extensions** reintroduce a role for policy via nominal rigidities, evaluated with VAR-based impulse responses.
- **Target-zone models** treat managed exchange-rate bands, and their eventual collapse, as a distinct fixed-rate framework.

## Actionable rules

None given. This is an academic text deriving and testing theoretical models rather than prescribing trades; the closest thing to a trading takeaway is descriptive: the empirical UIP violations documented here are the academic basis for the currency carry trade (borrow low-interest, invest high-interest currencies), and the PPP half-life findings imply real exchange rate mean-reversion is a multi-year, not multi-month, phenomenon.

## Caveats

This is dense graduate-level econometrics (GMM, cointegration, calibration) aimed at PhD students, not practitioners; most of the value for a trader is qualitative (which anomalies are robust, roughly how persistent they are) rather than a usable signal. Data and tests are pre-2001 and G7-currency-centric. Only a fraction of the book's 11 chapters were read in depth for this note; chapters on the New International Macroeconomics and on collapsing fixed-rate regimes were skimmed rather than covered in full.

## Who it is for

Quant researchers or macro traders who want the academic grounding behind carry-trade and PPP-reversion strategies, not traders looking for rules or systems.
