## Summary

A four-page client-education handout from an Australian financial planning practice, using historical return charts (1978-2004) to compare Australian and international equity performance and to argue for holding a blended (50/50) portfolio for diversification and risk-reduction reasons.

## Key points

- Both Australian and international shares showed similarly volatile nominal annual returns from 1978-2004, with a similar frequency of negative years.
- Australian returns in the 1980s were dominated by the volatile resources sector; since the mid-1990s by less volatile financials; international returns were driven by the late-1990s tech boom, then a 2000s "tech wreck" hangover.
- Real (inflation-adjusted) average annual returns, 1978-2004: Australian shares 9.3%, international shares (in AUD) 7.9%.
- A 50:50 Australian/international "High-Growth" blend achieved a similar real return (9.0%) to 100% Australian shares but with lower volatility (risk-return chart shows the blend inside the efficient frontier of either single-country option).
- Rolling 5-year real returns show international shares matched or beat Australian shares in most periods except 2002-2004 (post tech-wreck hangover).
- Australia represents only about 2% of global stock market capitalization; staying domestic-only misses ~98% of investable opportunities and skews exposure toward financials/materials while underweighting pharma, aerospace, electronics, telecom, IT, and utilities.
- A well-diversified Australian + international portfolio has historically shown lower currency-related risk than an Australian-only portfolio, and a better chance of benefiting from currency moves.

## Actionable rules

1. Consider a blended allocation (illustrated at 50% Australian / 50% international) rather than a 100% domestic equity portfolio to reduce volatility for a similar long-run real return.
2. Do not treat currency exposure as a reason to avoid international diversification — the data cited shows diversified portfolios have lower currency-driven risk than single-country ones.
3. Recognize sector concentration risk in an Australian-only portfolio (heavy financials/materials weighting) and use international exposure to access sectors underrepresented domestically.

## Caveats

This is a marketing/client-education document from a financial planning firm with a licensed adviser's name and license number attached — it is promotional in nature and reflects the return-data window (1978-2004) available at time of writing, not a forward-looking guarantee. Not independent, peer-reviewed research; treat the specific return figures as illustrative rather than current.

## Who it is for

Australian retail investors evaluating whether to diversify beyond domestic equities, and advisers looking for a simple historical-data argument for international allocation.
