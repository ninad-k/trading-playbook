## Overview

A practitioner's manual of short-term (1–5 day) futures trading patterns and money management formulas, written by Larry Williams from roughly 40 years of trading Bonds, the S&P 500, and grains. The book is organized around a single claim: price is not perfectly random, and small, measurable statistical edges (day-of-week bias, volatility expansion off the open, emotional overreaction patterns) compound into large profits once you add a disciplined exit and correct position sizing. Nearly every pattern in the book is backed by a specific backtest (date range, number of trades, win rate, dollar profit) rather than a general chart-reading heuristic.

## Core thesis

Markets are "not a coin flip" — Williams presents day-of-week and day-of-month statistics showing prices do not close up or down with even 50/50 odds, and short-term price patterns that reflect public overreaction (emotional buying/selling into gaps and false breakouts) reliably set up short-term reversals. His trading approach layers several independent, individually weak biases (a chart pattern, a calendar bias, an intermarket relationship such as Gold vs. Bonds) on top of each other — "stacking the deck" — to raise win rate and average profit per trade far above any single filter alone. He argues explicitly that money management, not entry timing, is what turns a mediocre edge into large compounding wealth, and devotes his longest chapter to sizing formulas.

## Key concepts

- **Trading Day of the Week (TDW)** — the finding that certain weekdays have a statistically different open-to-close bias than others (e.g., S&P 500 Mondays close up 57% of the time, average +$109; Bond Tuesdays open-to-close positive 55% of the time, average +$53); used as a trade filter.
- **Trading Day of the Month (TDM)** — the count of trading days elapsed in the current month (not the calendar date) used to time recurring monthly rally/decline windows; avoids the ambiguity of calendar dates falling on weekends/holidays.
- **Volatility breakout entry** — buying/selling a fixed percentage of the prior day's range added to (or subtracted from) the next day's open; Williams calls this "the most consistently profitable mechanical entry technique" he has traded.
- **Smash day** — a day that closes below the prior day's low (buy setup) or above the prior day's high (sell setup), signaling public over-reaction; a "hidden smash day" is a milder version where the close is in the outer 25% of the range against the trend.
- **Specialists' trap** — a Wyckoff-derived pattern: a range-bound market breaks out with a naked close beyond the range, then the breakout day's extreme is violated within 1–3 days, trapping breakout buyers/sellers.
- **Oops!** — Williams' best-known pattern: an opening gap beyond the prior day's range that then reverses back into that range, indicating the gap could not hold and trapping traders who acted on it.
- **Greatest Swing Value (GSV)** — the average "failure swing" (the open-to-high move on a down-close day, or open-to-low move on an up-close day) over the past 1–4 days, used as a volatility-based entry offset from the next open.
- **Will-Spread** — an intermarket indicator built from the ratio of a primary market's price to a related secondary market (e.g., Bonds vs. Gold), smoothed with a fast/slow exponential-average crossover to time short-term swings.
- **Bailout exit** — Williams' default profit-taking rule: exit on the first profitable opening after entry (immediately for fast markets like the S&P; delayed a day or two for slower markets).
- **Optimal f / fixed-fraction sizing** — position size computed as a fixed percentage of current account equity divided by the system's largest historical loss per contract, recalculated every trade as equity changes.

## Rules and setups

See linked system pages for full entry/exit/stop specifications on each named pattern (Smash Day, Specialists' Trap, Oops!, Greatest Swing Value, TDW/TDM). Williams' three universal exit rules (Chapter 11), which apply across every pattern in the book:

1. **Always use a dollar stop** on every trade as final protection (values given per pattern, typically $1,000–$2,500 depending on market).
2. **Bailout exit**: close the trade on the first profitable opening — if profit is even one tick, take it (delay a day or two in slower markets to let profit grow).
3. **Reverse on an opposite signal**: if short and a buy signal fires, exit and reverse immediately rather than waiting for the stop or bailout exit.

Money management formula (Chapter 13, his own solution after testing the Kelly criterion and Ryan Jones's fixed-ratio approach): contracts to trade = (account balance × risk%) / largest loss per contract. He states typical risk% by trader temperament: ~5% (conservative) up to ~20%+ (aggressive), with 10–15% as his general recommendation and his own trading using up to ~15%; he found the point where drawdown grows faster than profit typically falls between 14% and 21% risk.

## Risk and money management

- Every pattern trade carries a hard dollar stop (examples: $1,500–$1,800 in Bonds, $2,000–$2,500 in the S&P 500, $1,600–$1,750 for Greatest Swing Value trades).
- Williams explicitly rejects the "bet a thousand to make a thousand" mindset; in his own results (turning $10,000 into $1,100,000 in one year, and a separate $50,000 into over $1,000,000), he states he never risked more than 20% of his stake on a single trade, and that was "larger than it should have been."
- The Kelly criterion (F = ((R+1)×P − 1) / R, where P = win rate and R = win/loss size ratio) is presented and then explicitly discredited for trading — Kelly assumes a fixed win/loss size (true in blackjack, false in trading where the largest loss is the real killer of an account).
- His own formula recomputes size every trade from current equity, so position size shrinks automatically after losses and grows after wins — deliberately avoiding the "add a contract every $10,000 of profit" trap that let one 90%-accurate hypothetical system still net a loss after one large loss with an oversized position.
- Illustrative backtests scale position size up to 5,000 Bond contracts to show formula behavior — Williams explicitly warns readers this is not a realistic size and to focus on the relative shape of the results, not the raw dollar totals.

## Psychology and discipline

Williams frames his core trading belief as "the current trade will be a loser" — a deliberately pessimistic stance meant to counteract the natural human bias toward overconfidence, which he says causes traders to hold losers too long and oversize positions. Speculation, in his framing, has three components — selection, timing, and trade management — and only the last is where most traders fail. He repeatedly stresses that patience (waiting for a "stacked deck" of confirming filters rather than trading every day) and unemotional rule-following separate career traders from the 80% of short-term traders he cites as losing their capital. A recurring theme is "press your winners, not your losers": traders instinctively do the opposite, increasing size after losses and cutting size after wins, which he identifies as the single most damaging behavioral error alongside oversized bets and skipped stops.

## Strengths and caveats

- Every pattern is backed by specific, falsifiable backtest statistics (date ranges, trade counts, win rates, dollar totals), which is unusually rigorous for a pattern-trading book of this era.
- Several results reflect same-author, in-sample optimization on a handful of liquid futures (Bonds, S&P 500, grains) from the 1977–1998 period; no out-of-sample or walk-forward validation is shown, and Williams himself flags the risk of curve-fitting the Oops!-plus-calendar combination.
- Dollar figures and stop sizes reflect late-1990s futures tick values, margins, and commission levels ($50/round-turn cited); position-sizing examples using up to 5,000 Bond contracts are explicitly unrealistic and shown only to illustrate formula mechanics.
- The book does not detail Williams' best-known indicator, Williams %R — its short-term toolkit here centers on Will-Spread, Greatest Swing Value, and the pattern catalog instead.
- The Kelly-formula money-management episode (a managed account swinging from $10,000 to $2,100,000 to $700,000 in one year) is presented candidly as a cautionary tale of a flawed formula, which strengthens rather than undermines the book's credibility.

## Who should read it

Short-term futures and index traders who want concrete, quantified pattern rules (with exact stop and profit figures) rather than qualitative chart reading, plus traders looking for a plain-English, worked-example introduction to fixed-fraction position sizing.
