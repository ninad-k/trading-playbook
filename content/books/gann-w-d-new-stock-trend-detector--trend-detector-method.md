## What it is

The book's core mechanical trend-following method, drawn mainly from Chapters V and VII ("New Rules to Detect Trend of Stocks" and "A Practical Trading Method"): a chart-hierarchy trend filter, breakout/support-resistance entries built on single/double/triple tops and bottoms with a fixed point-based stop (the "3-point rule"), a volume-confirmation check, and a staged pyramiding rule for adding to winners. Gann presents it as a substitute for trading on tips, broker opinion, or guesswork.

## Rules

1. **Trend filter.** Establish the primary trend from the Monthly high/low chart; use the Weekly chart as secondary confirmation; use the Daily chart for entry/exit timing. Trade only stocks that are actively traded on meaningful volume — leave alone stocks stuck in a narrow, low-volume range until they break out.
2. **Old-level reversal.** Treat "old tops become bottoms, old bottoms become tops" as a working assumption: a broken support or resistance level is expected to reverse its role on a later retest.
3. **Buy setups.** (a) Near a single, double, or triple bottom, with a stop-loss no more than 3 points below the bottom. (b) On a breakout when price crosses a previous high (a prior peak or a previous year's high) by 1-3 points, confirming an uptrend, with a stop 3 points below the breakout/old top.
4. **Sell setups.** (a) Against a single, double, or triple top, with a stop-loss no more than 3 points above the top. (b) On a breakdown when price breaks an important previous low (or a prior year's low) by 3 points, with a stop 3 points above the broken level.
5. **Volume confirmation.** After the first sharp advance (or decline) off a low (or high), expect a secondary reaction. If that reaction occurs on decreased volume and the subsequent move resumes on increased/heavier volume, treat the trend as confirmed rather than reversing.
6. **Three-day hold check.** If a new position closes against you for three consecutive days, exit — the trade is likely wrong or against the prevailing trend. A profit showing through the third day is treated as confirmation that the position is aligned with the trend.
7. **Pyramiding.** Applicable roughly to stocks trading between 20 and 50: after the first trade shows about 5 points of profit, add a second lot and set a stop that locks in no loss on the combined position; wait for a further 10 points of favorable movement before adding another lot; after the fourth or fifth lot, reduce the size of each additional unit (e.g., step down from 50-share to 10-share increments).
8. **Re-entry.** If stopped out, wait for the same entry condition (a fresh breakout, or a new bottom/top formation) to be satisfied again rather than guessing a re-entry price.
9. **Never average a loss.** Only add to positions that are already profitable (per the pyramiding rule); never add size to a position that is underwater.

## Risk

Every entry carries a fixed, absolute stop of 1-3 points beyond the triggering support/resistance level rather than a percentage of capital or share price — so the effective percentage risk is smaller on higher-priced stocks and larger on cheap ones. Size is added only after a realized profit cushion (about 5 points on the first add, 10-point intervals afterward), and reduced again on later pyramid additions, which caps exposure in the most speculative, latest-added lots. Volume acts as a secondary risk filter: rising volume against a stalling price move is treated as a warning to tighten stops rather than to add to the position.

## Caveats

Reconstructed from OCR-sampled pages, some of which extracted in scrambled reading order (a known artifact of column-based scans) — the numbered rules above are reconstructed from surrounding sentence context rather than quoted as a clean enumerated list in every case, so treat exact wording as approximate. All stop distances and price levels are calibrated to 1930s stock prices (mostly $20-150/share, pre-decimalization) and would need conversion to percentage terms to apply to modern instruments. No backtested win rate, sample size, or drawdown statistics are given anywhere in the source — support is entirely narrated historical chart examples (Chrysler, United Fruit, Corn Products, Westinghouse, Auburn Motors, Timken Roller Bearing, and the Dow-Jones Averages, 1907-1935).
