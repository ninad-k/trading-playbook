## Summary

Trader John Hayden, drawing on Andrew Cardwell's extensions to J. Welles Wilder's original RSI, argues the Relative Strength Index (RSI) is the fastest way to read trend because — unlike Momentum, Rate-of-Change, or Stochastics — it is bounded 0–100 and damps the distortion caused by large price moves dropping out of the lookback window. The essay teaches a "range-shift" framework: watching which RSI band (80/40 in a bull market, 60/20 in a bear market) price respects, and reading divergences the opposite of the textbook interpretation.

## Key points

- Standard RSI lookback is 14 periods (recommended by Hayden as "one half the lunar cycle"); 9-period is common intraday, 25-period for gold/silver/crude/financials (roughly half a 50-day cycle). At least 90, ideally 200+, days of data are needed for reliable readings.
- In an uptrend, RSI tends to find support around 40 and resistance around 80 (an "80/40 range"); in a downtrend, resistance around 60 and support around 20 (a "60/20 range"). A shift from a 60/20 to an 80/40 range signals a trend change to bullish, and vice versa.
- Old RSI support/resistance levels behave like price support/resistance: once broken they tend to reverse roles (former support becomes resistance and vice versa).
- Contrarian divergence read: Hayden treats a bearish RSI divergence as a sign the market is still in a bull trend (just due for a pause), and a bullish divergence as a sign of an underlying bear trend — the opposite of the conventional "divergence signals reversal" reading.
- A valid divergence requires confirmation: price must actually turn before the divergence is considered "locked in," not merely possible.
- Divergence strength is inversely related to its duration — a 2–3 period divergence is the most powerful signal; multi-week/month divergences are comparatively weak.
- A moving-average trend filter is proposed: a 9-period SMA vs. a 45-period WMA, computed on both price and RSI. Trend is up when both 9-periods are above their 45-periods, down when both are below, and "sideways" when price and RSI moving averages disagree.
- The RSI's 9/45 moving-average cross typically leads the price 9/45 cross because RSI is more volatile than price.
- Illustrated on a multi-year Japanese Yen chart and a U.S. 30-year Treasury bond chart, tracking the RSI through multiple range shifts.
- Closing five-question trend checklist: What is the current RSI range? Is support/resistance being respected or violated? Has a key trendline broken (on price or RSI)? What divergences are present? What are the moving averages showing?

## Actionable rules

1. Use a 14-period RSI as the default; drop to 9-period for intraday work, 25-period for gold/silver/crude/financial futures.
2. Classify the market as bullish while RSI respects an 80/40 range, bearish while it respects a 60/20 range; the first violation of the "wrong side" of the range (e.g., 60 acting as resistance, then a break below 40) signals a change in trend.
3. Do not act on a bearish divergence in an uptrend as a short signal — Hayden's rule treats it as a pause/partial-profit signal, not a reversal signal (and symmetrically for a bullish divergence in a downtrend).
4. Confirm trend with the 9-period SMA / 45-period WMA cross on both price and RSI; require agreement between the two before trusting the up/down/sideways call.
5. Require at least 90 (preferably 200+) periods of price history before trusting RSI-based trend reads.

## Caveats

Self-published essay (1999) with no statistical backtest of the range-shift or divergence rules — the method is presented via annotated historical chart examples (Yen, T-bonds, Cisco) rather than quantified win rates. The "reverse divergence" interpretation explicitly contradicts the mainstream RSI textbook reading (Wilder's own divergence signal), so it should be treated as one practitioner's contrarian framework, not settled technical-analysis consensus.

## Who it is for

Traders already familiar with basic RSI who want a more structured, range-based way to read trend and want to understand the Cardwell school's non-standard divergence interpretation.
