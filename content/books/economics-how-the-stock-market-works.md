## Summary

A 15-page introductory tutorial (author Mark A. Bradley) covering the plumbing of the equity market rather than any trading strategy. It explains why companies raise capital by issuing stock instead of (or alongside) debt, walks through the IPO process from SEC registration through prospectus and underwriting, defines the different market participants (broker-dealers, registered representatives, market makers, specialists), and traces the life cycle of an order from placement to fill.

## Key points

- Capital is what a business needs to acquire assets; it can be raised via equity (selling ownership/stock) or debt (borrowing, to be repaid with interest).
- Advantages of issuing stock over debt: no obligation to make periodic interest or principal payments, and a company can often raise more capital than it could borrow; disadvantage: existing owners dilute their ownership and control.
- An IPO requires filing a registration statement with the SEC and a mandatory 20-day waiting period before shares can be sold; a preliminary prospectus ("red herring") and later a final prospectus disclose offering price, underwriting terms, and company history.
- Underwriting arrangements vary: best-effort (agent basis), all-or-none, negotiated, competitive bid, standby (existing shareholders' preemptive rights), and firm commitment (underwriter buys the whole issue and resells it).
- Broker-dealers must register with the NASD/SEC; brokers act as intermediaries for a commission and take no inventory risk, while dealers trade from their own inventory and take on risk.
- Prohibited broker-dealer practices include churning (excessive trading to generate commissions), unauthorized transactions, and misrepresenting SEC approval of a security.
- Market makers maintain continuous bid/offer quotes (used heavily on Nasdaq); specialists on exchange floors are obligated to keep an orderly, continuous market by buying when there are only sellers and vice versa.
- A trade's "life": an order is matched with a counter-order either electronically or via floor auction; unfilled or away-from-market orders can be left with a specialist; GTC (good-till-canceled) orders remain open until filled, canceled, or the end of the following calendar month.
