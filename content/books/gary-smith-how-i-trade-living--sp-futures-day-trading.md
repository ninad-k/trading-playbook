## What it is

Gary Smith's original (1985-mid-1990s) and updated (late 1990s) intraday methodology for day trading S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 stock index futures, described in [[gary-smith-how-i-trade-living]]. It combines a small set of price-action breakout patterns with tape-reading confirmation from market internals (the Dow, cash S&P, Nasdaq Composite, trin, and options put/call volume). Traded as a single-contract system over a 122-month real-money track record, it produced an annualized return of about 86% on an account that averaged under $10,000 — used by Smith as a springboard to build capital before shifting most of his trading to mutual funds.

## Rules

**Contract reference.** S&P 500 futures (CME): $250 × index price per point; E-mini S&P: $50 × price. Nasdaq 100 futures: $100 × price; Nasdaq 100 E-mini: $20 × price. A "point" in trader shorthand equals 100 (e.g., "a 180-point bounce" = 1.80 index points). All times in the book are U.S. central time.

**Entry pattern 1 — breakout-to-new-highs (Smith's favorite).** Note the high made by the futures during the first 50 minutes of trading (i.e., by ~9:20 a.m.). Buy if that high is exceeded at any point between 9:20 and 11:00 a.m. Narrow-range prior days (e.g., the narrowest range of the preceding 13 sessions) increase the reliability of the eventual breakout.

**Entry pattern 2 — bounce-off-the-low.** Between 9:20 and 11:00 a.m., buy when the futures trade 180 points (1.80) above their intraday low.

**Entry pattern 3 — high/low tracking.** During the first 2.5 hours, if the futures hang at or within a few ticks of their intraday high for 45+ minutes, with a series of marginal new highs and no significant reaction, treat this as confirmation of underlying strength and a candidate for a long entry.

**Entry pattern 4 — OOPS pattern (Larry Williams).** If the futures open above the previous day's high and then trade back below that high, or open below the previous day's low and then trade back above it, treat this as a reversal signal in the direction back toward the prior range — especially potent after a large gap opening that immediately reverses.

**Confirmation filters (required before acting on any entry pattern).** Check that market internals confirm the price pattern: the Dow, Transports, tick, and (in later years) the Dow/cash-S&P/Nasdaq Composite relationship should point the same direction as the breakout. Do not take a breakout signal that is contradicted by a negative divergence in the cash S&P or Nasdaq relative to the Dow.

**Divergence method (Sliter-derived).** Establish a rolling rule-of-thumb ratio between index point moves (example given: an 8-point Dow move ≈ a 1-point cash S&P move). When the cash S&P and/or Nasdaq Composite are running well ahead of or behind what the Dow implies by that ratio, and a supporting indicator (e.g., trin crossing above/below its recent range) confirms, trade in the direction of the stronger index. Wait until at least 10:00 a.m. before trusting a divergence reading; treat it as predictive for the next 1-2 hours, not the whole session.

**Entry pattern 5 — shorting setup.** Between 9:00 and 9:45 a.m., the futures make new intraday lows with little to no upward bounce, then make further new lows, again with little bounce. Confirm with the trin: it must be rising and at or above 0.95. Do not short if trin is below 0.90 — this negates the pattern.

**Death Zone caution.** Between 12:30 and 1:30 p.m. (originally described as 1:00-1:40 p.m. in Smith's earlier writing), expect a higher-than-normal chance of a sharp countertrend reversal, especially after a strong directional move earlier in the day; downtrend reversals in this window are more reliable than uptrend reversals given the market's historical upward bias.

**Nonrepeater filter.** The exact intraday pattern from one day (e.g., a large down-gap opening that trends lower all session) rarely repeats identically on the next trading day, especially during the Death Zone or the final hour; use a repeat of the setup as a reason to lean toward the opposite outcome, except across a Friday-to-Monday gap, where the rule does not apply.

**Trade management.** Enter with the intent to hold to the close or carry the position overnight into the next session if the trend is intact at the close, rather than scalping; Smith generally does not use tight intraday profit targets, preferring to ride a confirmed trend for the day (and sometimes the next day) once entered.

**Vehicle preference.** Nasdaq 100 futures are Smith's preferred vehicle over the S&P because of their more persistent, less choppy intraday and weekly trends; confirm Nasdaq 100 setups against the Semiconductor Index (SOX) and Morgan Stanley High Tech Index for divergent weakness before entering.

## Risk

Smith traded this system as a strict one-contract trader throughout the documented 122-month track record specifically because his personal risk tolerance could not absorb a meaningful drawdown from multi-contract sizing; the system as described has no explicit percent-of-equity position-sizing rule beyond this self-imposed one-lot discipline. There is no fixed dollar or percentage stop-loss specified for any entry pattern — risk is managed through the confirmation filters (avoiding trades where internals disagree) and by exiting quickly (often intraday) when the anticipated follow-through fails to appear, rather than through a predefined stop distance. Margin requirements for full-size S&P and Nasdaq 100 futures are substantial relative to a small account (tens of thousands of dollars per contract as of the book's writing), and day-trading margin is lower but still concentrates risk in one large, leveraged position per contract.

## Caveats

Several of the specific reference points are dated and were already flagged as such in the book itself: the MidCap futures contract Smith once traded became illiquid; the original 1985-launched Nasdaq 100 futures contract was discontinued and reintroduced in 1996; and Smith states outright that his mechanical breakout-system variants (sold commercially in the early-mid 1990s) had lost much of their effectiveness by the late 1990s due to rising market volatility, working for him only because he continued to filter signals with live tape reading rather than trading them mechanically. The Death Zone window itself shifted over the years covered by the book (1:00-1:40 p.m. to 12:30-1:30 p.m.), underscoring that these are empirically observed, drifting market habits rather than fixed constants. The trin threshold (0.95/0.90) and the Dow-to-other-index point ratios are described as needing periodic recalibration as index composition and price levels change.
