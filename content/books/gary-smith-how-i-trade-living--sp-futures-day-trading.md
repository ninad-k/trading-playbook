## What it is

Gary Smith's original (1985-mid-1990s) and updated (late 1990s) intraday methodology for day trading S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 stock index futures, described in [[gary-smith-how-i-trade-living]]. It combines a small set of price-action breakout patterns with tape-reading confirmation from market internals (the Dow, cash S&P, Nasdaq Composite, trin, and options put/call volume). Traded as a single-contract system over a 122-month real-money track record, it produced an annualized return of about 86% on an account that averaged under $10,000 — used by Smith as a springboard to build capital before shifting most of his trading to mutual funds.

## Rules

**Contract reference.** S&P 500 futures (CME): $250 × index price per point; E-mini S&P: $50 × price. Nasdaq 100 futures: $100 × price; Nasdaq 100 E-mini: $20 × price. A "point" in trader shorthand equals 100 (e.g., "180 points" = 1.80 index points). All times are U.S. central time.

**Entry 1 — breakout-to-new-highs (Smith's favorite).** Note the futures' high during the first 50 minutes of trading (by ~9:20 a.m.). Buy if that high is exceeded any time between 9:20 and 11:00 a.m. — most reliable after a narrow-range prior day (e.g., narrowest of the preceding 13 sessions).

**Entry 2 — bounce-off-the-low.** Between 9:20 and 11:00 a.m., buy when the futures trade 180 points above their intraday low.

**Entry 3 — high/low tracking.** During the first 2.5 hours, if the futures hang at or within a few ticks of their intraday high for 45+ minutes, making marginal new highs with no real reaction, treat it as confirmed strength and a long candidate.

**Entry 4 — OOPS (Larry Williams).** Futures open above the prior day's high then trade back below it (or open below the prior low then trade back above it) — a reversal signal back toward the prior range, especially strong after a large gap that immediately reverses.

**Confirmation (required for all entries).** Market internals — the Dow, Transports, tick, and the Dow/cash-S&P/Nasdaq Composite relationship — must point the same direction as the breakout. Skip any signal contradicted by negative divergence in the cash S&P or Nasdaq relative to the Dow.

**Divergence method (Sliter-derived).** Track a rolling point-ratio between indexes (e.g., 8 Dow points ≈ 1 cash S&P point). When the cash S&P/Nasdaq Composite run well ahead of or behind what the ratio implies, confirmed by trin, trade toward the stronger index — wait until 10:00 a.m., and treat it as good for the next 1-2 hours only.

**Entry 5 — shorting setup.** Between 9:00 and 9:45 a.m., futures make new lows with little bounce, then further new lows, again with little bounce. Confirm with trin rising and ≥ 0.95; do not short if trin is below 0.90.

**Death Zone.** Between 12:30 and 1:30 p.m. (originally 1:00-1:40 p.m.), expect elevated odds of a sharp countertrend reversal, especially after a strong earlier move; downtrend reversals here are more reliable than uptrend ones.

**Nonrepeater filter.** An exact intraday pattern (e.g., a large down-gap that trends lower all session) rarely repeats identically the next day, especially in the Death Zone or final hour — lean toward the opposite outcome on a repeat setup, except across a Friday-to-Monday gap.

**Trade management.** Hold to the close, or carry overnight if the trend is intact at the close, rather than scalping for small intraday targets.

**Vehicle preference.** Nasdaq 100 futures are preferred over the S&P for more persistent, less choppy trends; confirm setups against the Semiconductor Index (SOX) and Morgan Stanley High Tech Index for divergent weakness first.

## Risk

Smith traded this as a strict one-contract system throughout its 122-month track record because his risk tolerance couldn't absorb a multi-contract drawdown — there is no explicit percent-of-equity sizing rule beyond that self-imposed one-lot discipline. No entry pattern carries a fixed dollar or percentage stop; risk is managed through the confirmation filters and by exiting quickly when expected follow-through fails to appear. Full-size S&P/Nasdaq 100 margin is substantial relative to a small account, and each contract concentrates risk in one large, leveraged position.

## Caveats

Several reference points are dated, as the book itself notes: the MidCap futures contract Smith once traded became illiquid, and the original 1985 Nasdaq 100 futures contract was discontinued and relaunched in 1996. Smith states his commercial mechanical breakout-system variants had lost much effectiveness by the late 1990s due to rising volatility, working for him only because he kept filtering signals with live tape reading rather than trading mechanically. The Death Zone window itself shifted over the years the book covers, and the trin threshold and index point-ratios need periodic recalibration as markets change.
