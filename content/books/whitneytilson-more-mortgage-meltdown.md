## Summary

Hedge fund managers Whitney Tilson and Glenn Tongue, writing in early 2009 in the depths of the financial crisis, explain what caused the U.S. housing bubble and credit crunch, argue the mortgage meltdown was in roughly its "fifth inning" (not over), and then apply value-investing and short-selling principles to six real positions they held (Berkshire Hathaway, American Express, Resource America, a distressed mortgage-loan trust, MBIA, and Wells Fargo). The book blends macro diagnosis (Part One) with applied, case-study-driven investing advice (Part Two), explicitly framed as teaching a process rather than giving stock tips.

## Key points

- Core bubble driver: from 2000–2007 the income multiple an average borrower could get on a mortgage roughly tripled, disconnecting home prices from their multi-decade real trend.
- The authors ran roughly 100% long, 55% short (net ~45% long) heading into 2008, which they credit with limiting losses to less than half of the S&P 500's decline.
- Howard Marks (Oaktree) framework cited: master both "value" (price vs. intrinsic worth) and "cycles" (how value itself shifts); a bear market runs through three stages, ending when even prudent investors capitulate.
- The authors shifted from historically buying "60-cent dollars and selling 90-cent dollars" to buying "30-cent dollars and selling 75-cent dollars" — taking profits earlier given macro uncertainty.
- Six risk-ordered opportunity categories in a beaten-down market: blue chips, out-of-favor blue chips, balance-sheet plays, turnarounds, special situations, and small high-variance "mispriced" stocks.
- Shorting is discouraged for most investors: capped upside, unlimited downside, and squeeze risk (Volkswagen/Porsche 2008: stock went from $200 to over $1,000 in hours); the authors lost money shorting for three straight years before 2008.
- Long-dated index puts are used to hedge favorite long positions instead of shorting individual names, avoiding forced early exits from winners.
- Cyclical P/E hit 12.3 in March 2009 (vs. a 16.3 long-run average since 1870); historically, buying at that level preceded roughly a doubling in stocks over the next decade.

## Actionable rules

1. Size long/short exposure by conviction and macro risk, not fixed leverage — the authors ran ~100% long/55% short as a defensive-but-invested posture.
2. In volatile regimes, buy around a 70% discount to intrinsic value and sell around a 25% discount, rather than holding for full value realization.
3. Before shorting any stock, confirm tolerance for uncapped downside and squeeze risk; consider index puts to hedge longs instead.
4. Avoid shorting a sector via a basket/ETF unless every constituent is well understood — the authors' 2003–05 QQQQ/SMH put basket failed despite a correct macro thesis.
5. When moving from cash into a bear market, average in gradually rather than trying to call the exact bottom.

## Caveats

Written in real time during the 2008–2009 crisis, so specific forward-looking calls (the "fifth inning" claim, individual theses on MBIA, Wells Fargo) are historically dated and should be read as process illustrations, not current views. Not a systematic trading manual — position sizing and exit rules vary case by case rather than following one fixed formula, and the value-investing approach relies on subjective intrinsic-value estimates that are harder to verify than a rules-based technical system.

## Who it is for

Fundamental/value-oriented investors wanting to see how experienced hedge fund managers reasoned through position sizing, hedging, and shorting decisions during an actual systemic crisis, rather than technical or short-term traders looking for chart-based setups.
