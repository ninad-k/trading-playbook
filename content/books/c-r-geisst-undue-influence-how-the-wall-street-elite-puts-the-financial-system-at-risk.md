## Summary

A financial historian's narrative of how Wall Street banks, regulators, and Congress spent roughly 70 years dismantling the Depression-era regulatory "safety net" — culminating in the 1999 repeal of the Glass-Steagall Act (via the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act) — and how that deregulation contributed to the market bubble and collapse of 2000–2001 (Enron, WorldCom, and related accounting scandals). The book traces the arc from 1920s distrust of Wall Street through the Pujo hearings, the original 1933–34 securities laws, the slow erosion of restrictions from the 1960s onward, the Reagan-era acceleration, and 1990s deregulation, closing with a postscript asking whether deregulation actually worked.

## Key points

- **Glass-Steagall (Banking Act of 1933)** — separated commercial banking (deposits, loans) from investment banking (underwriting stocks/bonds) after the 1929 crash; repealed in 1999 by the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act after decades of lobbying.
- **Securities Act of 1933 / Securities Exchange Act of 1934** — survived the 1990s deregulation wave because they targeted disclosure and industry practices rather than structurally dividing banking, but the book argues their disclosure requirements had gaps that later fraud (Enron-era) exploited.
- **Pujo hearings (1912-13)** — early congressional investigation into concentrated "money trust" power (J.P. Morgan and allied banks/trusts), establishing the historical template for later Wall Street distrust.
- **The specialist "dual capacity" conflict** — NYSE floor specialists trading for their own book while handling customer order flow, a conflict of interest documented as unresolved from the 1930s through a 2004 ~$240 million SEC settlement.
- **Long-Term Capital Management (1998)** — a highly leveraged hedge fund bailout orchestrated by the Fed, cited as an early warning sign that "modern risk management" claims did not match actual systemic risk.
- **Citigroup formation (Travelers/Citicorp merger)** — approved by regulators in anticipation of Glass-Steagall's repeal, described as the high-water mark of the deregulatory movement.
- **Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002)** — passed in direct response to Enron/WorldCom, creating the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board and new auditor/attorney disclosure obligations.
- **Recurring pattern the book argues for** — regulatory rollback driven by an alliance of Wall Street firms, allied regulators, and both major political parties; deregulation proceeds until a crisis (1929, 2001) exposes the gap, followed by a new round of reform, followed eventually by renewed deregulation pressure.

## Actionable rules

None given — this is a narrative history of regulation and institutional behavior, not a trading system. What a market participant can take from it: treat "modern risk management" claims from large institutions skeptically, watch for regulatory-rollback cycles as a macro signal of building systemic risk, and expect conflicts of interest (dual-capacity trading, revolving-door regulators, self-regulating exchanges) to persist across market eras rather than being permanently resolved.

## Caveats

Published in 2005, so it does not cover the 2008 financial crisis that many later readers will want to compare it against; its central thesis (deregulation enabled the 2001 collapse) is argued narratively through documented events and quotes rather than through statistical or econometric analysis, and the author's viewpoint is openly critical of the deregulatory consensus rather than neutral. Detailed legislative and personnel history (named congressmen, SEC chairmen, bank executives) requires background knowledge of US financial regulatory institutions to fully follow.

## Who it is for

Traders and investors who want historical context for why banking/securities regulation exists, how it has been eroded and rebuilt in cycles, and why conflicts of interest at large financial institutions are a structural feature rather than an occasional lapse.
