## Overview

The first installment (1995) of Bernstein's two-volume day-trading series, cataloged by the Library of Congress under futures/futures markets and written primarily for short-term traders of financial and commodity futures, though the indicator and system chapters apply to any liquid, actively-traded intraday instrument. The book surveys roughly twenty distinct timing tools and mechanical or semi-mechanical systems for intraday trading: moving averages, a stochastic-based "Stochastic Pop" (SP) technique, gap trading, Bernstein's own Moving Average Channel (MAC), Wilder's RSI and its first derivative, intraday momentum and rate of change, an intraday channel breakout, classical trend-line and chart-pattern reading, a proprietary open/close (O/C) oscillator, scalping, order mechanics, seasonality (including "key seasonal dates" and the Critical Time of Day, or CTOD, breakout method), intraday spread trading, a Consecutive Closes System (CCS), and a sentiment index (the Daily Sentiment Index, DSI) — closing with a chapter on day-trading psychology and "24 Keys to Success."

## Core thesis

No single indicator or system is reliable on its own; consistent day trading comes from combining a handful of objective, rule-based short-term methods — chosen to fit the instrument and time of day — with strict stop-loss and trailing-stop discipline, sound order-handling practice, and psychological control (patience, discipline, avoidance of overtrading). Bernstein is explicitly skeptical of black-box, over-optimized systems, arguing that most fail once real slippage and commissions are accounted for, and repeatedly frames day trading as requiring a cardinal, non-negotiable risk rule that must never be violated regardless of conviction.

## Key concepts

- **Stochastic Pop (SP)** — a momentum-continuation technique built on a 14-period slow stochastic: a buy signal triggers when %K reaches 75 percent or higher, on the premise that strong momentum readings often precede the sharpest continuation moves rather than an imminent reversal.
- **Opening gap** — today's open trading above yesterday's high (gap up) or below yesterday's low (gap down); the precondition for Bernstein's gap-trading systems.
- **Delayed Gap Down/Up Buy (GDO)** — a refinement of the basic gap method that waits through the first hour of trading before committing, to avoid chasing a gap that never fills.
- **Moving Average Channel (MAC)** — a support/resistance channel built from separate moving averages of highs and lows, with defined buy points (lower band, LBP), sell points (upper band, USP), and midpoints (MBP/MSP), exited on two consecutive bars closing outside the opposite band.
- **RSI first derivative** — a smoothed transform of Wilder's RSI intended to produce a less choppy, more timely timing line than the raw RSI.
- **Intraday Channel Breakout** — buying strength above a resistance level or selling weakness below a support level, on the premise that a fresh breakout tends to continue long enough to be tradable.
- **O/C oscillator** — Bernstein's own indicator comparing a moving average of 5-, 10-, or 15-minute opens against a moving average of the corresponding closes, intended to catch large intraday swings early.
- **Critical Time of Day (CTOD)** — an opening-range breakout system using the first two hours of 5-minute closing prices to define breakout levels (system detail in the sub-page).
- **Consecutive Closes System (CCS)** — a trend-following entry triggered by a defined run of consecutive closes in one direction, held with a predetermined stop.
- **Daily Sentiment Index (DSI)** — a contrarian sentiment gauge; historically, extreme low or high DSI readings preceded reversals or continuation moves with notable (though not universal) reliability in the book's S&P case log.
- **Key seasonal dates** — calendar dates on which a given market has shown a statistically strong historical tendency to close higher or lower, used as a directional filter rather than a standalone entry.

## Rules and setups

1. **Gap trading (GO / Delayed GDO)** — full rule set in the [[bernstein-jake-the-compleat-day-trader-vol-i--gap-trading-system|gap trading system sub-page]]: place a buy stop two ticks above the previous day's low after a gap-down open (or the mirror for a gap-up sell), with a delayed variant that waits through the first trading hour.
2. **Moving Average Channel (MAC) entries** — buy at the lower band (LBP) or midpoint (MBP) in an established uptrend; sell at the upper band (USP) or midpoint (MSP) in a downtrend; exit on two consecutive bars closing outside the channel on the opposite side, or trail a stop once the position shows a profit.
3. **Critical Time of Day (CTOD)** — full rule set in the [[bernstein-jake-the-compleat-day-trader-vol-i--ctod-critical-time-of-day|CTOD system sub-page]]: trade the breakout of the first two hours' 5-minute closing-price range, with a 5-minute-close-based trailing stop.
4. **Stochastic Pop (SP)** — using a 14-period slow stochastic, buy when %K is at 75 percent or higher at the end of the period; the sampled pages do not carry the mirrored sell-signal threshold or the exit rule.
5. **Scalping goals** — trade as often as reasonable within the session; exit quickly at either a profit or a loss; avoid holding a strong directional opinion, deferring instead to market fact tick-by-tick.
6. **RSI day trading** — the chapter is explicitly titled "Rules for Day Trading with the RSI," but the sampled pages end at the section heading before the numbered rules; treat the RSI first-derivative concept as documented and the specific entry/exit thresholds as missing.
7. **Seasonality** — trade in the direction indicated by a market's key seasonal date only when the statistical sample behind that date is large; no specific date list survives in the sampled pages.

## Risk and money management

Risk rules vary by system rather than following one universal formula. The MAC exits on a technical signal (two consecutive opposite-side closes) in preference to a fixed dollar stop; CTOD uses a 5-minute-closing-price trailing stop that ratchets in with each completed hour; the gap systems use a stop set in ticks from the entry level. Bernstein states no explicit percent-of-equity risk rule in the sampled pages, but repeatedly frames risk control as a "cardinal rule" that must never be violated regardless of market conditions, and recommends a trailing stop loss (rather than a static one) once any position shows a meaningful open profit, so that a stalled or reversing move gives back only part of the gain.

## Psychology and discipline

The closing chapters push a practitioner's discipline checklist: keep a detailed trading diary logging every trade, its rationale, and the emotional context around the decision, and review it regularly to reinforce winning behaviors and suppress losing ones; treat brief intraday price surges (on news or large orders) as opportunities to exit or add rather than noise to ignore, adjusting a mental or trailing stop as the move develops; and hold to a single daily profit goal, taking profits more readily once that goal is within reach rather than pressing for more. Bernstein frames violating one's own risk or system rule as a "cardinal" error regardless of how compelling the market looks in the moment.

## Strengths and caveats

The book is genuinely broad — one of the more comprehensive single-volume surveys of distinct, individually-testable short-term systems from a well-known commodity trading author — and several systems (gap trading, CTOD) are given complete, codeable rule sets even within the sampled OCR pages. Its futures orientation means examples are quoted in ticks and dollar amounts specific to 1990s contract specifications (e.g., an "$800 stop loss" on a 3- versus 54-period moving-average test), which a stock or forex trader must translate to their own instrument's tick size and volatility. The sentiment (DSI) case log spans 1982-1993, and several chapters — RSI day-trading rules, the seasonal date list, SP's sell-side threshold — reference specific numeric rules that are described as forthcoming in the text but were not present in the sampled OCR pages; these gaps are noted above rather than filled in. No aggregate, cross-system backtest or drawdown statistic is given; each system's support is a handful of illustrative chart examples plus, in a few cases, a single historical performance table.

## Who should read it

Futures and other short-term traders wanting a toolbox of several distinct, individually adoptable mechanical or semi-mechanical systems rather than a single method, and who are comfortable adapting tick-denominated rules to their own instrument. Less useful for a reader who wants one fully specified system worked in depth (see the sub-pages for the two that are), or for a stock-only trader uninterested in translating futures-contract examples.
