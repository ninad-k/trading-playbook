## Overview

The Master Swing Trader is a comprehensive manual for short-term equity trading built around Farley's "Pattern Cycle" — the idea that every market rotates through bottoms, breakouts, rallies, highs, tops, reversals, and declines, and that swing traders profit by identifying where price sits in that rotation. The book is organized in three parts: general market analysis and strategy-building tools (charting, time, volume, risk), the "7-Bells" — seven original, named setups for locating trades — and mechanics of trade execution (tape reading, order types, avoiding fakeouts). It is dense with concrete visual rules rather than formulas, aimed at active discretionary traders working multiple time frames simultaneously.

## Core thesis

Price moves in cycles, not straight lines, and every chart feature (moving averages, trendlines, Fibonacci retracements, Bollinger Bands, candlesticks) is a lens on the same underlying crowd psychology. Farley's central method is **cross-verification (CV)**: a trade is only worth taking when multiple, independently derived levels — a prior high, a 50% retracement, a moving average, a trendline — converge at the same price. The more elements that intersect, the higher the probability that price will react there. Discretionary "3D charting" (viewing three related time frames at once) replaces mechanical indicator signals as the primary tool for timing.

## Key concepts

- **Pattern Cycle** — the recurring sequence bottoms → breakouts → rallies → highs → tops → reversals → declines that every market passes through repeatedly.
- **Cross-verification (CV)** — entering only where multiple unrelated S/R signals (highs, retracements, moving averages, trendlines) converge at one price; "CV×4" marks an especially high-probability confluence.
- **3D charting** — analyzing three time frames at once (e.g., 1-minute/5-minute/60-minute for day traders, 60-minute/daily/weekly for position traders) to align entries.
- **Trend-range axis** — markets alternate between trending and rangebound phases; Farley notes trends persist only 10–20% of the time.
- **Moving average ribbons (MARs)** — layered MAs (20-50-200 for daily charts, 5-8-13 for intraday) whose alignment and crossovers reveal trend state.
- **Empty zone (EZ)** — the low-volatility interface between the end of a rangebound market and the start of a new trend.
- **Reward:risk (R:R)** — the distance to the profit target versus the distance to the failure target; Farley requires roughly 3:1 before slippage.
- **Execution target (ET) / failure target (FT)** — the pre-planned entry price and the price that proves the setup wrong.
- **%WIN, AvgWIN, AvgLOSS** — Farley's three levers for improving a trading system's profitability.
- **Fractals / trend mirrors** — price tends to repeat its own past behavior at a given level or in similar wave structures.
- **7-Bells** — Farley's seven original setups: Dip Trip, Coiled Spring, Finger Finder, Hole-in-the-Wall, Power Spike, Bear Hug, 3rd Watch (see system sub-pages).

## Rules and setups

1. **Bollinger Bands**: 20-bar, 2 standard deviations for swing/position charts; 5-8-13 combination (5-bar and 8-bar SMAs over 13-bar, 2 std-dev bands) for intraday. Consider new shorts when price extends more than 50% outside the top band, new longs on the same violation of the bottom band.
2. **Moving average ribbons**: 20-50-200 (or aggressive 18-50-150) for daily charts; 5-8-13 for 1-minute/5-minute/60-minute charts.
3. **Reward:risk filter**: only take setups where the profit target is at least 3× the distance to the failure target; discount roughly half a point for slippage.
4. **2B Top/Bottom** (classic setup, Farley's version of Sperandeo's/Connors' pattern): short when price makes a marginal new high above the last swing high, then reverses back below it; stop just above the failed high. Mirror for 2B bottoms.
5. **Channel Break**: trade breaks of parallel price channels (PPCs) with the channel width as the projected target.
6. **Liquidity filter**: avoid stocks trading under 500K–1M shares/day for 1–3 day swings; intraday traders need 2M+ shares/day (3–5M+ for shock-event stocks).
7. **Capitalization tiers**: $2,000–$5,000 requires minimal position size and lower-beta stocks; $10,000–$25,000 supports active swing trading without margin; $50,000–$100,000+ is the minimum to trade for a living.
8. **Stops**: prefer discretionary, trailing S/R-based exits over fixed physical stops when a position can be actively managed; use the 5-8-13 MA/BB grid — exit an 8-bar break in strong trends, a 13-bar break in moderate ones.

Concrete numbers for each of the 7-Bells (NR7 narrow-range triggers, volume-spike thresholds, Fibonacci zones, etc.) are detailed in the system sub-pages.

## Risk and money management

Farley treats risk management as the discipline that separates survivors from the roughly 70%+ of swing traders who fail. Core rules: never enter without knowing the exit; size the position so the pre-defined failure target produces a loss within personal risk tolerance; and target reward at least 3× risk before real-world slippage (final ratio typically closer to 2.5:1). He gives a full formula set — %WIN = winners/total trades, AvgWIN = total profit/winning trades, AvgLOSS = total loss/losing trades — and shows that a system can be profitable with a %WIN under 50% if AvgWIN sufficiently exceeds AvgLOSS (a worked table shows $10,000 profit achievable at 75%, 50%, or 25% win rates by adjusting AvgLOSS). Liquidity minimums (500K–5M+ shares/day depending on holding period) and capitalization tiers ($2K–$100K+) scale trading style to account size. Margin is described as "a powerful tool in the right hands and a dangerous weapon in the wrong hands," to be added only after a proven track record.

## Psychology and discipline

Farley frames swing trading as a psychological discipline as much as a technical one. He warns that most participants unconsciously sabotage their own success, treats "getting high" on the adrenaline of trading (win or lose) as a direct path to failure, and insists traders must "kill the thrill" and tolerate long stretches of boredom. Key behavioral rules from his 30-rules conclusion: "if you have to look, it isn't there" (the best setups are obvious); "price has memory" (levels that mattered before tend to matter again); "control risk before seeking reward"; and "expect the market to reverse as soon as you get filled." He also stresses non-market discipline (health, stress management) as a precondition for trading discipline, and cautions that a %WIN near or below 50% is normal even for professionals.

- Ch 1 — Trading the Pattern Cycle — introduces the cycle framework, trend-range axis, time, volume, and reward:risk.
- Ch 2 — Preparing for the Market Day — support/resistance, cross-verification, 3D charting, MA ribbons, candlesticks, Bollinger Bands, watch-list building.
- Ch 3 — Analyzing the Market — the Pattern Cycle phases in detail (bottoms, breakouts, rallies, highs, tops, reversals, declines) and volume behavior at each.

## Strengths and caveats

Farley's framework is unusually rich in concrete, visual rules rather than vague generalities, and the cross-verification/3D-charting method generalizes well beyond his named setups. However, the book is dated in places: it assumes decimalized but pre-high-frequency U.S. equity markets circa 2000 (ECNs, Level II, "the specialist"), and several liquidity/volume thresholds (500K shares/day, 2M+ intraday) reflect early-2000s market structure rather than today's much higher overall volumes. Many setups rely on discretionary visual pattern recognition ("the skilled eye") that is difficult to codify or backtest, and Farley is candid that several bells (Coiled Spring, Power Spike pivots) require experience to apply well. Some named patterns overlap heavily with earlier work he credits (Sperandeo's 2B, Raschke and Connors' Holy Grail and Turtle setups, Edwards and Magee's gap taxonomy), so readers familiar with those sources will recognize the lineage.

## Who should read it

Best suited to intermediate-to-advanced discretionary equity swing and day traders who already understand basic technical analysis and want a systematic vocabulary for multi-time-frame confluence trading. Less useful for pure mechanical/quant system builders, since most setups depend on visual judgment rather than fully codified rules.
