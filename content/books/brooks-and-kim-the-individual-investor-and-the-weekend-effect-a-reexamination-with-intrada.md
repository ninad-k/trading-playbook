## Summary

Brooks and Kim reexamine the weekend effect—the tendency toward negative Monday returns—and ask whether individual and institutional trading patterns help explain it. They use intraday transactions, quotes, and trade sizes for 276 randomly selected NYSE and AMEX firms during 1989. Small trades (100–500 shares) proxy for individual investors and trades of 10,000 shares or more proxy for institutions; buys and sells are classified relative to the bid-ask midpoint (pp. 2–4). The sample’s mean Monday return is −0.250% (t-statistic −3.85), and the opening hour is significantly negative across transaction, bid, and ask prices (pp. 4–6). Monday average daily dollar volume is about $8.25 million per firm versus more than $9.35 million on Tuesday–Friday. Large-trade volume falls from $4.254 million on other days to $3.490 million on Monday, while small-trade selling rises: full-day selling is 49.9% versus 48.9%, and the difference is significant at t=7.12 (pp. 8–10). After a negative Friday return, Monday selling pressure is higher than after a positive Friday (p. 11). The authors infer that individuals contribute directly through selling and institutions indirectly through reduced liquidity.

## Key points

- The study uses trade size as a proxy, not investor identities; the authors acknowledge institutions can split orders (pp. 2–3).
- Monday’s return and volume effects concentrate around the open and differ by trade size (pp. 6–10).
- The pattern appears across equity-size portfolios, especially reduced large trades and higher small-trade selling (p. 11).

## Actionable rules

1. The paper gives no trading strategy or sizing rule; it studies historical return, volume, and order-flow patterns.
2. As a research implication, separate Monday’s opening hour from the rest of the session and condition tests on the prior Friday’s return (pp. 6–11).
3. Treat trade size only as a proxy for investor type and validate the result with participant-level data before drawing behavioral conclusions (pp. 2–3, 11–12).

## Caveats

The evidence is a single 1989 US sample and is observational. Trade size imperfectly identifies investor type, midpoint classification omits midpoint trades, and stale quote depth prevents a direct order-book imbalance measure (pp. 3–4, 12). The historical anomaly may not persist.

## Who it is for

Useful for market-microstructure researchers and traders studying time-of-week execution effects.
