## Summary

A six-page essay by Malcolm Robinson (distributed via tradingforbeginners.com and Mark McRae) drawing an analogy between quitting smoking/drinking and cutting trading losses. The author argues that traders who repeatedly let losses run are not making a free choice each time — they are conditioned to believe, often unconsciously, that holding on is beneficial, much like a smoker believes smoking has benefits. He splits trading skill into "anticipation" (market analysis) and "execution" (actually managing the trade), and argues execution, not analysis, is where most traders lose money.

## Key points

- Most trading losses come from a few large uncut losses, not many small ones.
- Advice like "cut your losses early" fails because it assumes the trader has an easy choice, when in fact behavior is driven by deep-seated, often unconscious beliefs about what is "beneficial."
- Society conditions people to be persistent, decisive winners who don't quit — traits that work against cutting losses in trading.
- Trading skill splits into two parts: anticipating the market (reading charts, systems, indicators) and executing trades (order placement, discipline, follow-through).
- A floor broker's job is judged purely on execution quality, independent of trade outcome — a model the author says traders should adopt for their own conduct while a trade is live.
- Judge trading decisions only during market hours by execution quality; save evaluation of anticipation/strategy for when markets are closed.
- Common poor-execution mistakes: not placing a stop per plan, moving a stop against yourself, hesitating on a clear signal, trading out of anger, failing to trail a stop, letting winners turn into losers.

## Actionable rules

None given as numeric parameters. The author's behavioral prescription: (1) always place a predetermined stop loss and never move it further away, (2) use a trailing stop to protect profits, (3) while trading, evaluate yourself only on execution — not on whether the last trade was profitable, (4) review and adjust strategy/anticipation only when markets are closed.

## Caveats

This is a short motivational/psychological essay, not a tested system — it offers no data, statistics, or specific position-sizing numbers. The smoking/drinking analogy is illustrative rather than evidence-based. It ends with a promotional link to the author's paid course, which is not covered here.

## Who it is for

Traders who understand technical analysis but consistently fail to honor their own stops — useful as a short psychological reframe on why "just cut your losses" advice doesn't stick, and for pointing attention toward execution discipline rather than more system-building.
