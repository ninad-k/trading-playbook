## Overview

Written by futures industry veteran Philip Gotthelf, this book positions currency trading around a single organizing idea: parity, the ratio at which one form of value exchanges for another. Every price move in every market, Gotthelf argues, is a parity adjustment, and understanding cross-parity and multiple-parity relationships is the foundation for spotting arbitrage and directional opportunity in FOREX. The book walks from money and currency history, through the macro forces that move exchange rates, into the mechanics of the Interbank cash market, currency futures, and options on futures, and finishes with practical trading strategies and chart-based decision rules. It is a 2003-era primer aimed at investors deciding whether and how to trade currencies, not a specialist quant text.

## Core thesis

Currency prices are not mysterious; they are the observable record of successful parity tests between buyers and sellers, the same process that sets any market price. Because currency values are relative (a dollar cannot rise without something falling against it), profitable trading requires tracking cross-parity across multiple currencies and commodities simultaneously, choosing the trading vehicle (cash, futures, options) whose risk/exposure profile fits the trader's forecast and capital, and pairing every forecast with an explicit, consistent decision rule and money-management plan. Gotthelf stresses that a forecasting method is worthless without rules for what action to take and how much capital to risk on it.

## Key concepts

- **Parity** — the ratio at which two items of value exchange; the book's central lens for reading all price action, including non-currency markets.
- **Cross-parity / multiple parity** — when a currency or commodity is priced in more than one currency, algebraic relationships between those prices create arbitrage potential.
- **Interbank market** — the decentralized network of banks and dealers that makes the cash FOREX market; not a physical institution, and largely unregulated.
- **Spot vs. forward** — spot settles within 2 days; anything beyond 2 days is a forward transaction, carrying added interest-rate and counterparty risk.
- **Pip** — the smallest quoted increment of an exchange rate; the bid/ask difference is the spread, expressed in pips.
- **Risk vs. exposure** — risk is the probability of loss; exposure is the dollar amount at stake. A limited-loss instrument (a bought option) can still carry near-100% risk if the strike is unlikely to be reached.
- **DOMO (deep out of the money)** — an option with a strike far from the current price; cheap, but with a low probability of paying off. Roughly 80% of options expire worthless.
- **Delta** — the ratio of an option's price change to the underlying's price change; used to build delta-neutral combinations.
- **Intervention** — central banks (individually, "unilateral," or jointly via G5/G7, "collective") buying or selling currency to alter parity; historically short-lived in effect per the book's cited academic study.
- **Synthetic long/short** — combining a bought call with a sold put (or vice versa) at the same strike to replicate a futures position.
- **Straddle vs. strangle** — a straddle sells (or buys) a call and put at the same strike; a strangle uses different strikes to bracket an expected trading range.
- **Formation vs. pattern** — a single price top/bottom is a formation; a formation that repeats becomes a pattern (e.g., a double top), which the book treats as the threshold for predictive reliability.
- **Accuracy, efficiency, consistency** — the three performance measures Gotthelf applies to any forecasting/decision method: how often it's directionally right, how much of the move it captures, and how stable those two are over time.

## Rules and setups

The book does not present one proprietary system; instead it gives a framework plus worked chart examples with explicit stop placement. Key numeric rules stated in the text:

1. **Every strategy = forecast + decision rule + money management.** A forecast alone ("price will rise") is not tradable without a stated action ("buy futures, stop at X").
2. **Trading-range (envelope) strategies**, using a defined support/resistance band: sell the call at resistance and the put at support (short strangle); or reverse a synthetic short/long at each boundary; or sell futures at resistance with a buy-stop above and buy futures at support with a sell-stop below.
3. **Trend/breakout trades use a projection-and-trail method**, illustrated with the 2001–2002 euro: buy on breakout from a consolidation, place the initial stop at the base of the consolidation, move the stop to breakeven once the measured-move objective (consolidation height added to the breakout point) is hit, then ratchet the stop up to each successive support level as new objectives are reached. Example: "Buy September euro at market using an .87500 stop. Move stop to entry after .90000. Raise stop to .90000 after .91500."
4. **Measuring-gap and head-and-shoulders projections**: project price targets by adding the size of a prior gap or pattern (e.g., a 300-point head-to-neckline distance) to the breakout level.
5. **Penetration adjustment**: because traders cluster orders on round chart-grid numbers, offset stated objectives and stops by a small buffer (the book uses 5 points on the euro) rather than the exact round number.
6. **M/W pattern continuation rule**: a break of the M-top base or W-bottom top projects a first move equal to 50% of the pattern's height.
7. **Moving-average system**: buy when price crosses above the moving average, sell/short when it crosses below; shorter averages suit sharp trends with few corrections, longer averages suit volatile trends with deeper pullbacks.

## Risk and money management

- **The "$600 or 2% rule."** Through paper-trading observation, Gotthelf finds that trades that draw down more than roughly 2% of position value (or about $600 per position in his example) rarely recover; he recommends capping automatic loss exposure at that level rather than letting a position run further against you, and validating the threshold's consistency with a Chi-Squared goodness-of-fit test over a multi-year back-test.
- **Survival math**: with $10,000 of starting capital and a fixed $600 loss cap, an account can theoretically withstand about 16–17 consecutive losing trades before being wiped out (before transaction costs).
- **"Cut your losses short and let your profits ride"** is framed quantitatively: a system with a losing-trade-to-winning-trade ratio of roughly 2:3 can still be profitable if the average loss is kept small relative to the average win.
- **Initial futures margin is roughly 1–2% of contract value** — extreme leverage (a 9% currency band under 1970s margin rules translated to potential 500–5,000% swings on margin).
- **Options limit exposure to premium paid, but do not limit risk** (defined as loss probability) — a core distinction the book repeats throughout the options chapters.
- **Paper trading is recommended before committing real capital**, using three record-keeping forms (Account Balance Ledger, Daily Trading Log, Historical Trading Record) to build a statistical profile of average win/loss, maximum consecutive losses, and drawdown before sizing real positions; the author warns paper trading understates the emotional pressure of real losses and can produce false confidence.
- **Sell options rather than buy them, when practical** — the book cites that roughly 80% of options expire worthless and argues retail traders are systematically biased toward the losing side (buying) of that statistic; selling requires posting margin and accepting theoretically unlimited exposure, so stops or covering positions are advised.

## Psychology and discipline

Gotthelf frames psychology mainly through discipline in following a written plan rather than introspective trader-mindset work. He cites a survey of subscribers to a mechanical daily trading system (Commodex) showing women outperformed men, attributing the gap to greater discipline in following exact buy/sell/stop signals rather than "challenging the rules." The book's recurring caution is against three regret phrases — "I should have, I would have, I could have" — used to illustrate the cost of failing to act on a forecast. It also devotes a full chapter to interventions, scams, and rogue traders (Kohli fraud, Barings/Nick Leeson, Allfirst/John Rusnak, Princeton Economics/Martin Armstrong) as cautionary case studies in leverage, unchecked risk-taking, and the importance of verifying a manager's NFA registration before entrusting capital.

## Strengths and caveats

The book is dated in market structure specifics: it describes early-2000s retail cash FOREX minimums ($5,000–$10,000), pre-decimalization futures references, and a currency landscape still adjusting to the newly launched euro; contract sizes, minimum account thresholds, and regulatory bodies referenced (unregulated cash FOREX, limited NFA oversight) have since changed substantially with the Dodd-Frank era regulation of U.S. retail FOREX. The chart-pattern chapter is candid about its own subjectivity — Gotthelf compares chart interpretation to Rorschach testing and admits pattern recognition is "as much an art as a science" — which is a caveat worth carrying into any use of its projection rules. The intervention-effectiveness discussion leans on a single 1999 academic study (Hutchison and Fatum) covering 1982–1993; treat its conclusion (interventions are usually short-lived and often net trading opportunities) as dated and narrow in scope. There is no single named, testable trading system in this book — strategies are illustrative frameworks and worked historical examples rather than a fixed, universal rule set, so no system sub-page accompanies this note.

## Who should read it

Best suited to traders and investors who are new to FOREX and want a grounding in why currencies move (macro/parity) and how the different trading vehicles (Interbank cash, futures, options) differ in risk/exposure before committing capital. Less useful for experienced options traders (the Greeks coverage is introductory) or as a source of a rigorous, back-tested mechanical system — its "system" content is closer to guided chart-reading with example stop placement.
