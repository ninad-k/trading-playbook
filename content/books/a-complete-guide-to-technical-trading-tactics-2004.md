## Overview

John L. Person, a longtime broker and CTA at National Futures Advisory Service, wrote this as a soup-to-nuts futures and options trading manual — market mechanics, fundamentals, chart types, candlesticks, chart patterns, indicators, sentiment, order execution, psychology, tactics, and options, in roughly that order. Its centerpiece and most original contribution is Chapter 6, Pivot Point Analysis, where Person lays out the exact floor-trader formula for calculating daily, weekly, and monthly support/resistance target levels, then spends the next several chapters showing how he layers those numbers with candlestick reversal patterns and momentum oscillators to build what he names the P3T (Person's Pivot Point Trade) signal. The book's organizing principle, borrowed explicitly from Arthur Sklarew, is "verify, verify, verify" — no single technique is trusted alone; a trade idea needs confluence across at least two independent tools before Person acts on it.

## Core thesis

Markets have a mathematically derivable equilibrium point for any given session (day, week, or month) and trade within calculable support and resistance bands around it; these bands come from the pivot point formula applied to the prior period's high, low, and close. Pivot levels are a leading indicator — available before the session starts — unlike most technical tools, which are lagging. But raw pivot numbers are not signals by themselves: reliable trade entries come from the pivot target being tested at the same time an independent confirming signal fires — a candlestick reversal pattern, an oscillator divergence (stochastics, MACD), or lopsided Commitments of Traders (COT) positioning. This "rule of multiple techniques" is the book's throughline across chart patterns, candles, indicators, and sentiment.

## Key concepts

- **Pivot point (P)** — `(High + Low + Close) / 3` of the prior period; the base equilibrium level around which the next period's price is projected to trade.
- **R1 / S1 / R2 / S2** — first and second resistance/support target levels derived algebraically from P, the prior high, and the prior low (exact formulas in [[a-complete-guide-to-technical-trading-tactics-2004--p3t-pivot-candle-confluence]]).
- **P3T (Person's Pivot Point Trade) signal** — Person's named setup: a pivot target level (typically R1 or S1) being tested in confluence with a candlestick reversal pattern and, ideally, an oscillator divergence.
- **Verify, verify, verify** — Person's restatement of Sklarew's "Rule of Multiple Techniques": never act on one signal alone; the more independent, non-correlated confirming indicators that agree, the higher the confidence.
- **Oops signal** (credited to Larry Williams) — a countertrend gap-fade: buy stop a few ticks above the prior day's low after a lower open, sell stop a few ticks below the prior day's high after a higher open.
- **Opening range breakout** (credited to Larry Pesavento) — the day's open falls within roughly 10% of the day's eventual high or low about 67% of the time; day traders buy above and sell below the early opening range accordingly.
- **Three gaps / island reversal** — a sequence of a breakaway gap, a midpoint (measuring) gap, and an exhaustion gap marks the likely end of a move; an island top/bottom forms when price gaps away from a prior range and later gaps back through it, isolating a block of bars.
- **Commitments of Traders (COT) report** — CFTC's weekly breakdown of net futures positioning by commercials (hedgers, "smart money"), large speculators (funds/CTAs), and small speculators (the public); a lopsided small-speculator position at a price extreme is read as a contrarian warning.
- **Fibonacci retracement/extension** — standard ratios (0.382, 0.5, 0.618, 0.786, 1.272, 1.618) applied to a prior swing to project correction depth and extension targets; Person cross-checks these against pivot targets for confluence.
- **Elliott Wave** — a five-wave impulse/three-wave correction (A-B-C) framework used loosely as a structural overlay, with the rule that wave 3 cannot be the shortest impulse wave and wave 4 cannot overlap wave 1's territory.

## Rules and setups

1. **Pivot point formula**: `P = (H + L + C) / 3`; `R1 = (P × 2) − L`; `R2 = (P + H) − L`; `S1 = (P × 2) − H`; `S2 = (P − H) + L`. Calculate on daily, weekly, and monthly data in parallel; when two time-frame targets cluster near the same price, treat that confluence zone as a higher-confidence target.
2. **P3T entry rule**: wait for price to reach a calculated R1/S1 (or R2/S2) target, then require a confirming candlestick reversal pattern (shooting star, hammer, engulfing, harami, harami cross, dark cloud cover, or piercing) at that level before entering counter to the approach; add a confirming oscillator divergence (stochastics or MACD) where available. Only take the first test of a given pivot level — Person explicitly avoids re-entering on a second or third test of the same number.
3. **Oops trade**: on a gap-down open, place a buy stop a few ticks above the prior session's low; on a gap-up open, place a sell stop a few ticks below the prior session's high; works best as a countertrend fade in an already-extended move.
4. **Opening range breakout**: buy on a break above, or sell on a break below, the first part of the day's opening range, since the open sits near the eventual daily high or low roughly two-thirds of the time.
5. **Chart-pattern measuring rules**: head-and-shoulders — project the neckline-to-head distance from the neckline breakout; triangles/flags/pennants — project the pattern's widest dimension (flagpole or triangle height) from the breakout point; the longer a triangle takes to form, the weaker its eventual breakout tends to be (typical daily-chart triangles run 6–10 trading days, up to two weeks).
6. **COT filter**: treat a market as vulnerable to reversal when it sits at a price extreme AND large speculators are heavily net long while commercials are heavily net short (or vice versa at lows), especially when small speculators also hold a historically lopsided position.
7. **S&P Friday 10:30 a.m. rule**: the intraday trend established between 10:30 a.m. and noon (Central) on Fridays tends to persist into the week's close; day traders can wait for that window before trading with the established direction.
8. **Pyramiding money-management example**: starting with $5,000, add one contract for every 20-cent favorable move (illustrated with soybeans), sizing the add to roughly match the initial margin requirement, with stops trailed below each new entry — presented as an illustrative, not universally prescribed, technique.

## Risk and money management

Person does not prescribe a single percent-of-equity risk rule; risk management is folded into specific tactics instead. Stops are placed at technically meaningful points — below/above the pivot level that triggered the trade, below a candlestick pattern's extreme, or below/above chart-pattern boundaries — rather than at an arbitrary dollar or percentage figure. The worked pyramiding example risks 20% of a $5,000 starting stake ($1,000, a 20-cent stop) on the first soybean contract, then uses subsequent open profits (not new capital) to finance each additional contract, trailing stops upward as the position grows; a second, more aggressive "scale-back" variant is shown to risk nearly the whole account on the initial entries and is flagged as materially riskier. Person's blunt psychological risk rule, quoting an old trading adage, is "it's okay to lose your shirt in this business, just don't lose your pants" — meaning individual losses should be survivable, never account-ending.

## Psychology and discipline

The book's mental-game chapter (Ch. 11) centers on "I'll think about it" as the single most damaging trader habit — hesitation after a valid, planned setup has triggered, driven by fear of being wrong or fear of losing money, which causes missed entries and, just as often, overstayed exits on winning trades. Person's fix is procedural: "plan your trade, and trade your plan" — define entry, stop, and target before the setup arrives so execution becomes mechanical rather than a live emotional decision. He cites Mark Douglas's view (from an interview) that any single chart-pattern outcome is fundamentally a random, probabilistic event, meaning the trader's job is to execute a positive-expectancy process repeatedly, not to be right on any one trade. Paper trading is recommended specifically to rebuild confidence and test a method during a losing streak, not as a permanent substitute for live trading.

## Strengths and caveats

The pivot point chapter is the book's strongest material: the formula is stated exactly, applied consistently across daily/weekly/monthly time frames, and backed by numerous real, dated chart examples (sugar, cattle, silver, S&P, dollar index, crude oil, cotton) where the projected target and the actual high/low are compared directly, giving the reader an honest sense of the method's typical margin of error rather than only cherry-picked exact hits. The "verify, verify, verify" discipline is a genuinely useful filter against acting on any single indicator in isolation. Caveats: this is a 2004 futures/options-focused book, so contract specifications, margin figures, and brokerage/order-routing details (Chapter 10) are dated; several worked examples predate decimalization-adjusted tick sizes in some markets. The Elliott Wave and Gann sections are covered briefly and non-rigorously compared to dedicated texts on those subjects, and read as supplementary color rather than fully specified methods. As with most confluence-based systems, no formal backtest or win-rate statistic is given for the P3T signal — its edge is demonstrated anecdotally through selected historical examples rather than a systematic study.

## Who should read it

Best suited to an intermediate futures or commodities trader who wants a concrete, calculable support/resistance framework (pivot points) to replace or supplement subjective trend-line drawing, and who is willing to layer candlestick and oscillator confirmation on top rather than trade pivot levels mechanically. Less useful as a first book on candlestick patterns specifically (the coverage in Chapter 4 is solid but not as exhaustive as a dedicated candlestick text) or as an options-strategy reference (Chapter 13 is a primer, not a deep options course).
