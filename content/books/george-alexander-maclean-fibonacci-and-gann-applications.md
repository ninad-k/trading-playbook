## Overview

Written by a working fixed-income/futures technical analyst, this book is less a theory text than an experienced practitioner's log of where Fibonacci and Gann drawing tools actually work, where they fail, and why. After deriving the Fibonacci sequence and ratio mathematically, MacLean walks through retracements, extensions, fanlines, arcs, and timelines chart by chart (mostly bond yields, interest-rate futures, and agricultural/metal futures from 2001-2004), then does the same for Gann's swing charting, vertical retracements, fanlines, and squares, and closes with experimental "synthetic ratio" retracements built from other irrational numbers (√2, √5, the sphere-to-cube ratio). Throughout, the emphasis is on construction discipline (which high/low to anchor a tool to, when a pattern has "aged out" of usefulness) rather than on any single trading system.

## Core thesis

Price is the visible residue of the physical attrition between buyers and sellers, and because certain ratios recur in natural growth processes (the Fibonacci sequence, √2, √5, the golden ratio), they also recur as psychologically meaningful support/resistance levels in markets. But these tools are diagnostic overlays, not signals in themselves: their reliability depends heavily on correct construction (anchoring to a genuine historic/lifetime extreme, not an arbitrary local high/low), on the asset class (works best in interest-rate/bond instruments with a strong "interest" carry component; weakest in metals and weather-driven commodities), and on how much time/price distance has elapsed since the pattern's origin (a fanline or Gann angle "ages out" and should be redrawn or abandoned). The author is explicit that Gann's planetary/astrological material is outside the book's scope and that no drawing tool should be used alone — congestion, flag patterns, and channel context must confirm a retracement or fanline level before it is traded.

## Key concepts

- **Fibonacci retracement** — horizontal support/resistance levels at 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% (and sometimes 78.6%/14.6%) of a primary price move, measured from a significant high to a significant low (or vice versa); 38.2% and 61.8% are treated as the primary levels, 23.6% as secondary (useful mainly in range-bound rate/bond markets).
- **Fibonacci extension** — projection levels beyond 100% of a move (100%, 138.2%, 150%, 161.8%, 261.8%, 423.6%) used as price objectives after a retracement completes; the author considers these the least reliable tool in the kit and prefers pattern-based targets instead.
- **Fibonacci fanline** — diagonal trendlines drawn from an origin (significant low/high) through the vertical retracement points, extended forward in time; behaves like a trending version of the horizontal retracement, with the 38.2% fanline gaining strength the longer it goes untested.
- **Fibonacci arc** — circular retracement bands centered on the move's extreme, radius set by the retracement percentages; direction-agnostic (a break signals a move toward the next arc but not which way) and sensitive to chart-scale distortion.
- **Fibonacci timeline** — vertical lines drawn through Fibonacci day-counts (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8...) from a significant origin day, meant to forecast turning dates; the author is openly skeptical, finding them useful only on weekly-or-longer charts and unreliable for calling reversals.
- **Gann swing chart / trend indicator line (TIL)** — a simple high/low swing-charting method (line moves to the new high if today's high exceeds yesterday's, to the new low if today's low is lower; outside/inside days have specific handling) used as a standalone entry/exit/stop system.
- **Gann retracement (eighths)** — vertical retracements in eighths (1/8 through 7/8) of a move rather than Fibonacci percentages; 4/8 (50%) is the key level, with "thirds" (33%/66%/100%, tied to 120°/240°/360° of a circle) treated as less useful.
- **Gann fanline / angle** — diagonal lines drawn from a significant high or low at fixed price-to-time ratios (1x1 = the classic 45° line, plus 2x1, 4x1, 1x2, 1x4, etc.), anchored to the idea that Gann's era priced in 32nds against a 360-day interest year.
- **Synthetic ratios** — retracement/fanline levels derived from other irrational constants: √2 gives 41.4%/70.7%, √5 gives 44.7%, and the cube-to-circumscribed-sphere volume ratio gives 52.3%/91.1%; tested empirically against the same chart set as traditional Fibonacci levels, with mixed but sometimes strong results (e.g., 41.4%/44.7% acting as a congestion zone in several bond futures examples).

## Rules and setups

1. **Retracement construction**: anchor to a confirmed, significant high-to-low (or low-to-high) primary move only after the reversal is underway; plot 23.6%/38.2%/50%/61.8% (add 23.6% specifically for range-bound interest-rate/bond futures). Interpretation cascade: price slides from the extreme toward 23.6%, then 38.2%, then 50%, then 61.8%, then the 100% origin, with a break of 61.8% treated as a signal to switch to other technical tools.
2. **Extension use**: only trust an extension level (100%, 161.8%, 261.8%) as a target when there is prior price history in that zone (a previous congestion/support level); avoid targeting "blue sky" extension levels with no trading history behind them, and prefer pattern-measured objectives (flag/pennant projections) instead.
3. **Fanline construction**: draw from a historic/significant origin (never an intermediate high/low) through the vertical retracement points, extended across time; if a new extreme forms, redraw ("running fanline") rather than keep the old anchor. Bearish (down-sloping) fanlines strengthen the longer the 38.2% line goes untested; bullish fanlines behave more like ordinary retracements.
4. **Arc construction**: center the circle on the move's extreme with radius set at each retracement percentage; treat a break of one arc as signaling movement toward the next arc without directional bias, and beware zoom/scale changes distorting the visual radius.
5. **Gann Swing Chart entry/exit/stop system**: a complete, codeable trading system built on the TIL — see the companion system page [[george-alexander-maclean-fibonacci-and-gann-applications--gann-swing-chart-system]].
6. **Gann fanline construction**: anchor only to a genuine historic or lifetime high/low (an intermediate anchor "is dangerous and... completely wrong" per the author); price-time ratios follow the sequence 4x1, 3x1, 2x1, 1x1, 1x2, 1x3, 1x4; a break of one fanline projects toward the next, and conjunctions of two fanlines from different anchors mark high-conviction consolidation zones.
7. **Gann Square/Hexagon**: build a number spiral (Gann Square) or basis-point hexagon centered on a key yield/price extreme; watch the diagonals and the 90°/180°/270°/360° radii (with 120°/240° diagonals on the hexagon) as candidate turning levels, calculated via spreadsheet rather than charting software.
8. **Aging-out discipline**: any diagonal tool (Fibonacci or Gann fanline, Gann angle) loses reliability as time/price elapses from its origin; steep, short-lived fanlines complete quickly, while shallow, long fanlines can remain valid for a year or more — there is no fixed expiry, only asset-specific experience.

## Risk and money management

The book contains no position-sizing, stop-percentage, or capital-allocation rules — its only explicit risk-management content is embedded in the Gann Swing Chart system, where the stop-loss level is defined mechanically as the price of the TIL's last change of direction (i.e., a market-structure stop, not a fixed dollar or percentage amount). All other tools (retracements, fanlines, arcs, extensions) are presented purely as price-level and timing analysis; the author repeatedly cautions that a broken key level (especially a 100% retracement or a Gann fanline anchor) should trigger a reassessment of the whole analytical setup rather than a mechanical re-entry.

## Psychology and discipline

MacLean frames technical analysis explicitly as an "art" learned only through repeated chart study, not a mechanical formula — he states plainly that "no one really knows all there is to know" and that skills learned in one market regime often fail to transfer to the next. He warns against over-loading a chart with every available Fibonacci/Gann tool at once, against forcing a fanline or arc to "fit" by tweaking its scale, and against calling extension or timeline targets with confidence when there is no prior price history to support them — a discipline of admitting a tool's use has "run out" (aged out) rather than forcing continued reliance on it.

## Strengths and caveats

The book's strength is candor: it reports failures as readily as successes (e.g., steep Gann fanlines that "ran out of steam," Fibonacci timelines that were "starting to be late," extension calls that proved "overenthusiastic"), and it is explicit about which asset classes suit Gann analysis (bonds/rates, energy) versus which don't (metals, weather). Caveats: nearly all worked examples are 2001-2004 European/US bond, FX, and futures charts, so there is no equity-market or modern (post-2005, decimalized/algorithmic) validation; no win-rate, backtest, or drawdown statistics are given anywhere — every claim is a narrated chart example, not a study; the synthetic-ratio chapter is explicitly experimental ("I have not used this very often"); and Gann's price/time-in-a-360-day-year framework is acknowledged by the author himself to introduce systematic error against a five-day trading week, with no quantified correction offered beyond "experience."

## Who should read it

Intermediate-to-advanced technical analysts who already understand basic chart reading and want disciplined construction rules for Fibonacci and Gann drawing tools, particularly in interest-rate and futures markets — not a good first book on either Fibonacci or Gann for a beginner, since it assumes familiarity with candlestick/bar patterns, moving averages, and channel/flag terminology throughout.
