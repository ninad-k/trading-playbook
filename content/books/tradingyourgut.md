## Overview

Curtis Faith, one of the original Turtle traders trained by Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt, wrote this as the second book in his "Art of the Trade" trilogy (following *Way of the Turtle*). The book's argument is that "right brain" gut instinct and "left brain" logical analysis are not opposites — trading emotionally (reacting to fear and hope) is different from trading from a trained gut, and the latter is a skill that can be deliberately built. Roughly the first half of the book explains why untrained intuition misleads traders (cognitive biases, herd behavior, "wrong-brain thinking"); the second half is about training exercises and processes to build reliable, fast, trained intuition, closing with how to combine intuition with a testable trading plan.

## Core thesis

Master traders do not suppress emotion in favor of pure logic; they develop a third mode — trained intuition — through repetition and deliberate practice, the same way expert chess players or fighter pilots develop fast, accurate pattern recognition that bypasses slow conscious reasoning. The book's central claim is that most "trading psychology" advice stops at "control your emotions," when the more useful skill is training the right brain (via specific practice methods borrowed from sports, the military, and skill acquisition research) so that gut reactions become a reliable, testable input alongside a rule-based system rather than a substitute for one.

## Key concepts

- **Trading emotionally vs. trading from the gut** — the book's core distinction: emotional trading is reactive (fear/hope driving decisions); gut trading is trained pattern recognition operating faster than conscious analysis.
- **Mental inertia** — the tendency of both individual brains and, in aggregate, markets, to resist starting a new trend and then resist stopping once underway; explains why momentum persists and why traders are systematically late to enter and exit.
- **Loss aversion / sunk-cost bias** — the instinct to avoid "booking" a loss (treating it as not real until sold), which causes small losses to compound into large ones; identified as one of the most damaging inherited "prime lessons."
- **Confirmation bias / hubris** — the tendency to seek evidence supporting a position already taken and discount contrary evidence; the book's antidote is Paul Saffo's "strong opinions, weakly held."
- **Discretionary trading as intuition** — visually recognizing chart patterns (e.g., "efficient" low-noise trending stocks) is framed as one of three forms of intuition, alongside pattern-compression for handling large data volumes, and expert-level situational awareness built from deep experience.
- **Right brain / left brain framing** — a simplified (not strictly neuroscientific) shorthand used throughout: left brain = sequential, rule-based, verbal logic; right brain = holistic, fast, pattern-based judgment.
- **Simplicity as a trained outcome** — the book's account of the Turtle program: complex analytical knowledge compresses, with practice, into near-instant recognition ("I looked at the chart and knew immediately what to do").
- **Techno traders / wind-tunnel training** — an analogy to skydiving formation training in a wind tunnel: practicing a skill in a safe, repeatable, lower-stakes environment reduces fear and accelerates the shift from conscious to intuitive execution.

## Rules and setups

This is a psychology book, not a system manual; the only fully specified trading rules given are the Turtle rules Faith describes from his own training, offered as an illustration rather than a system to adopt as-is:

1. **The four Turtle rules (as taught by Richard Dennis)**: (1) trade with an edge — have a strategy with a positive expectancy; (2) manage risk — do not use leverage large enough to risk ruin; (3) be consistent — apply the strategy uniformly to capture its edge; (4) keep it simple.
2. **Entry**: buy breakouts of 10- to 12-week highs; sell short breakouts of 10- to 12-week lows (the original Turtle breakout system, described only in outline here).
3. **Stop placement (two variants offered to Turtles)**: (a) standard — stop at roughly twice the average daily trading range from entry (example given: gold entered at $400 with a $10 average range gets a stop near $380); (b) "whipsaw" variant — exit and be willing to re-enter if price reverses, producing more frequent, smaller losses but (per Dennis) better long-term performance; harder to execute psychologically.
4. **Position sizing**: the book explicitly warns against rule-of-thumb sizing (e.g., blindly risking 1% or 2% per trade) as potentially dangerous because it does not adapt to changing volatility; Faith describes using recent average daily price movement to size positions, then deliberately overriding that formula with smaller size when trained intuition senses conditions have become abnormally quiet before a potential violent move.
5. **Trade-outcome asymmetry (not a rule but a stated pattern)**: in a year Faith was up over 200% as a Turtle, he had roughly 10-12 winners against 45-50 losers; winners ranged 5-40%+, losers were capped near 0.5% each — illustrating a low-win-rate, cut-losses-short/let-winners-run profile rather than a high-win-rate approach.

No options, sector, or asset-class-specific setups are given; the book is deliberately non-technical about mechanics.

## Risk and money management

The book's risk framework is psychological rather than formulaic: master traders are said to judge success by ending account equity, not by win rate, and to expect (and tolerate) many more losing trades than winning ones if the losers are kept small and consistent. The explicit caution is against static percentage-risk rules of thumb (1-2% per trade) applied without adjustment for changing volatility, since a quiet market before an explosive, possibly overnight move can make a volatility-based position look far too large in hindsight; the recommended override is to let trained intuition scale down size when conditions "feel" abnormally compressed, in addition to (not instead of) a volatility-based baseline. Persistence is treated as double-edged: necessary to survive drawdowns, but dangerous when it keeps a trader in a position or a broken method past the point where evidence says to exit — the book urges the same trained "reversal" instinct that lets a trader cut a loss quickly to also override the well documented human bias toward stubbornness.

## Psychology and discipline

This entire book is a psychology and discipline treatment; there is no separate technical layer to distinguish it from. Its distinguishing angle versus other trading-psychology books is the emphasis on trainability: rather than treating biases (loss aversion, confirmation bias, herd conformity, anchoring) as permanent handicaps to be merely "aware of," Faith presents specific practice regimens (drawn from analogies to Formula One drivers, skydivers, and military decision-training, e.g., Gary Klein's work with Marine squad leaders) intended to convert slow, biased, conscious decision-making into fast, accurate, trained intuition. Key behavioral patterns discussed: gun-shy risk avoidance (evolutionarily adaptive, but causes traders to over-favor high-win-rate/low-payoff strategies), keeping-what's-yours loss aversion, hubris/confirmation bias, and the "strong opinions, weakly held" stance recommended as the antidote to both indecision and stubbornness. A recurring structural point: strategies that feel psychologically uncomfortable to execute (frequent small losses, letting a rare big winner run) tend to be the ones that remain profitable, precisely because their discomfort keeps most other traders from crowding into them.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — The Power of the Gut — introduces trained intuition via George Soros and the author's own Turtle background; distinguishes gut trading from emotional trading.
- Ch 2 — The Purpose of Gut Intuition — the evolutionary/functional case for intuition as a legitimate decision tool.
- Ch 3 — Wrong-Brain Thinking — catalogs cognitive biases (mental inertia, loss aversion, confirmation bias, persistence) that misfire in modern markets, illustrated with Turtle-era examples and stop-placement mechanics.
- Ch 4 — The Structure of the Markets — how market structure and participant behavior interact with the biases from Ch 3.
- Ch 5 — Training and Trusting Your Gut — methods for building confidence in intuitive judgments.
- Ch 6 — Trading Smarts — combining analytical ("smarts") and intuitive inputs.
- Ch 7 — Simplicity and Speed: Training to Be a Master — the Turtle program as a case study in compressing rule-based knowledge into fast pattern recognition; states the four Turtle rules and breakout/stop mechanics.
- Ch 8 — Techno Traders — using deliberate, repeatable practice environments (the skydiving wind-tunnel analogy) to build trading reflexes.
- Ch 9 — A Careful Balancing Act — balancing intuition against over-reliance on either brain hemisphere.
- Conclusion / Afterword — evaluating discretionary systems, reducing fear to increase usable intuition, conscious awareness of decision-making, position-sizing critique, and closing personal anecdotes on trusting gut calls outside trading.

## Strengths and caveats

The right-brain/left-brain framing is used as a plain-language metaphor, not a rigorous neuroscience claim, and should be read that way — the book cites real researchers (Kahneman, Tversky, Gary Klein) but its own brain-hemisphere language is looser than the literature it draws on. The Turtle-system details given (breakout length, stop multiple) are deliberately abbreviated summaries of a system fully described in Faith's earlier book, not a complete specification here. The core advice — that intuition can be trained and is not the same as emotional reactivity — is a reasonable, if unfalsifiable, thesis; the book offers no backtests or quantitative evidence that "trained gut" trading outperforms rule-only trading, only anecdotes (Soros, Livermore, the author's own results, and non-trading examples like Marine Corps squad leaders and Formula One drivers). Readers looking for a rule-based system should read this alongside, not instead of, a mechanical-systems book.

## Who should read it

Discretionary and semi-discretionary traders who already have some rule-based foundation and want a framework for when and how to trust pattern-recognition judgment calls, especially those who trained originally as rule-followers (systematic or Turtle-style) and are trying to understand why raw discretion sometimes outperforms rigid rules. Less useful for a trader looking for concrete entry/exit/position-sizing rules to code, since the book intentionally treats those as secondary to the psychological argument.
