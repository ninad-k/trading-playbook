## Summary

The National Futures Association booklet explains how retail off-exchange foreign currency trading works and why it carries substantial risk. OTC retail customers trade directly with a dealer rather than through a central exchange or clearing house, so the dealer is the counterparty and sets the execution price (pp. 2–4, 7). The booklet distinguishes bid and ask, spread, rollover, and settlement, and gives a profit formula: selling price minus buying price multiplied by transaction size (p. 5). Its example buys 100,000 euros at 1.2178 and sells at 1.2188 for a $100 gain (p. 5). Margin is a security deposit: at 1%, $1,217.80 controls $121,780 of euros, described as 100:1 leverage (pp. 5–6). A 1% adverse move can therefore consume the deposit, and losses may exceed it depending on the account agreement (pp. 6–7). NFA also highlights uninsured funds, dealer credit risk, system failure, fraud, and the absence of a central marketplace (p. 7). It recommends checking registration and disciplinary history, account terms, commissions, markups, and dispute procedures (pp. 4, 8–11).

## Key points

- Leverage magnifies both percentage gains and losses; it does not change the dollar P/L for a given position move (pp. 5–6).
- Dealers may charge commissions, markups, or both, so spread alone is not the full cost (p. 4).
- Regulation and protections vary by entity and activity; customers should verify the dealer’s status (pp. 8–9).

## Actionable rules

1. Use only capital whose loss would not affect your financial situation, and read the account agreement before funding an OTC account (pp. 7, 9).
2. Calculate notional exposure, security deposit, spread, rollover charges, and possible loss before opening a position (pp. 4–7).
3. Independently verify the dealer, introducing firm, and account manager with the relevant regulator and check disciplinary history (pp. 8–9).

## Caveats

This is a 2004 US investor-education document; regulatory entities, margin rules, and dealer practices may have changed. The examples are mechanics, not expected returns. “100:1” is an illustration of leverage, not a safe operating level.

## Who it is for

Useful for new FX traders performing broker and counterparty due diligence.
