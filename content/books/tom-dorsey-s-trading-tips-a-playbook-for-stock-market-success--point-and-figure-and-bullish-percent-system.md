## What it is

Dorsey, Wright & Associates' complete point-and-figure (P&F) methodology: a time-independent charting technique that plots only price (no volume, no fixed time axis) as alternating columns of Xs (demand/rising) and Os (supply/falling), combined with two derived layers — relative strength (RS) charts that compare a stock or sector to the S&P 500, and bullish percent indices that measure the percentage of stocks on a P&F buy signal within a market, sector, or index. Together these three layers answer, in sequence, whether the market favors buying or selling (bullish percent), which sectors are strongest (sector bullish percent and sector RS), and which individual stocks within those sectors have the best chart pattern (P&F chart patterns and trendlines).

## Rules

**Constructing the chart**
1. Use only the daily high and low price; plot rising moves as a column of Xs and falling moves as a column of Os. Columns always alternate X/O — never mixed within a column — and each column requires a minimum of three boxes.
2. Set the box size (price increment per box) from the standard scale: ¼ point per box from $0–$5, ½ point from $5–$20, 1 point from $20–$100, 2 points from $100–$200, 4 points above $200. Use a smaller box size for more signals/active trading, a larger box size to filter noise on volatile or high-priced stocks.
3. Require a three-box reversal to start a new column in the opposite direction (e.g., on a 1-point box, price must reverse by at least 3 points to flip from a column of Xs to a column of Os, or vice versa). This is the chart's noise filter.
4. Label columns by month using the standard convention (1–9 for January–September, A/B/C for October/November/December) to preserve a time reference despite the chart being price-driven.

**Reading buy/sell signals and patterns**
5. The simplest buy signal (double top) occurs when a column of Xs exceeds the high of the immediately preceding X column. The simplest sell signal (double bottom) occurs when a column of Os exceeds the low of the immediately preceding O column.
6. A triple top/bottom breaks two prior column extremes instead of one; a catapult combines a triple top (or bottom) followed immediately by a double top (or bottom) breakout, a stronger continuation signal.
7. A shakeout pattern requires a minimum of five columns of alternating lower tops and higher bottoms; act only on the eventual breakout in either direction, not before. A triangle pattern requires a minimum of seven such columns.
8. A low pole warning (bullish) occurs when a decline exceeds the prior bottom by three or more boxes and then reverses up, retracing more than 50% of the decline. A high pole warning (bearish) is the mirror-image signal on a rally that fails and retraces over 50%.
9. Draw the bullish support line at a 45-degree angle upward from the box directly below the lowest O in the chart's base; a stock trading above this line is in a positive trend. Draw the bearish resistance line at 45 degrees downward from the box directly above the highest X at the chart's top; a stock trading below this line is in a negative trend. A break of the relevant line is a warning to reassess the position.

**Timing with relative strength**
10. Calculate relative strength (RS) as (stock or sector index price ÷ S&P 500 price) × 100, plotted as its own P&F chart using the same X/O and three-box-reversal rules. A column of Xs on the RS chart means the issue is outperforming the S&P 500, independent of its own absolute price trend.
11. Favor stocks whose own RS chart and peer-group RS chart are both in Xs; RS column changes (X-to-O or O-to-X) tend to persist for roughly six to eight months, while a full RS signal (buy or sell) tends to persist roughly two years on average — treat an RS reversal as a longer-horizon signal, not a short-term timing trigger.

**Timing with bullish percent**
12. Calculate a bullish percent reading as (number of stocks in the universe currently on a P&F buy signal ÷ total stocks in that universe) × 100. Plot it on its own P&F chart on a 0–100% grid, using the same three-box-reversal convention (a 6-percentage-point move on the standard 2%-per-box scale).
13. Treat readings above 70% as overbought/high-risk (favor defense — reduce new buying, consider hedges) and readings below 30% as oversold/low-risk (favor offense). The strongest buy signals occur when the bullish percent reverses up from below 30%; the strongest sell/defense signals occur when it reverses down through 70%.
14. Apply the same bullish percent read at multiple levels — overall market (NYSE, OTC), individual sector, and options universe — and require the sector's bullish percent to be in a column of Xs (ideally still below 50%, i.e., early in the up-move) before selecting individual stocks within it.
15. Sequence the analysis top-down: market bullish percent (offense or defense) → sector bullish percent and sector RS (which sectors) → fundamentals (candidate list) → individual stock P&F pattern, trend line, and RS (final entry timing).

## Risk

The methodology is a timing and selection filter, not a position-sizing or stop-loss system by itself — it must be paired with an explicit stop (commonly a bullish-support-line violation or a double-bottom break below entry) and a risk/reward check (the source's own rule of thumb requires at least 2 points of reward — measured to a P&F vertical/horizontal count price objective — for every 1 point of risk to the stop). Three-box reversals mean a chart-based stop can require a meaningful adverse move (three boxes' worth) before triggering, which is larger on wider box sizes or higher-priced stocks; using too large a box size on a volatile stock understates real drawdown risk before a chart signal changes, while too small a box size on a slow-moving stock generates excessive whipsaw signals (documented in the source with a YHOO example: 18 signals on a 2-point box vs. 9 on a 5-point box vs. 3 on a 10-point box over the same period). Bullish percent extremes (below 30%, above 70%) are described as risk zones, not automatic reversal triggers — a reading can remain pinned in "overbought" or "oversold" territory for extended periods without reversing.

## Caveats

The three core numeric building blocks (box size table, three-box reversal rule, bullish percent 30/70 thresholds) are stated precisely, but the price-objective mechanics referenced throughout (the "vertical count" and "horizontal count" used to set upside/downside targets) are used in worked examples without the underlying counting formula being spelled out in the sections sampled — treat target-setting as a documented but incompletely specified part of the method here, and consult a dedicated point-and-figure charting reference for the exact count arithmetic. Signal persistence statistics (RS column changes lasting six to eight months, full RS signals roughly two years) are stated as house-research averages rather than backtested with confidence intervals. All example charts (YHOO, Boeing, XYZ Technology, the Dow Jones 20 Bond Average through 2000) are drawn from the late-1990s/2000 market and are illustrative rather than a systematic historical study of the method's edge.
