## What it is

A sequential screening checklist Hagstrom distills from Buffett's actual purchase decisions: twelve criteria, grouped into four categories, that a business must satisfy (not necessarily all twelve, but enough to build conviction) before a purchase is justified. It is a qualitative/quantitative business-selection method, not a price-timing system — it answers "is this a buy candidate, and at what price," not "when this week should I enter."

## Rules

**Business tenets** (three basic characteristics of the business itself):
1. Is the business simple and understandable? You should be able to explain how it makes money without excessive uncertainty about its future earnings.
2. Does it have a consistent operating history — the same core business, not a company in the middle of a turnaround or a major strategic pivot? Buffett treats "severe change and exceptional returns" as rarely compatible.
3. Does it have favorable long-term prospects — is it a "franchise" (product needed/desired, no close substitute, unregulated pricing) rather than a commodity business competing mainly on price?

**Management tenets** (three qualities senior managers must display):
4. Is management rational — particularly in capital allocation decisions (reinvestment vs. dividends vs. share buybacks), always with the goal of maximizing per-share intrinsic value rather than growing the business for its own sake?
5. Is management candid with shareholders — does it report fully and honestly, including failures and the metrics that actually matter (not just favorable ones)?
6. Does management resist the institutional imperative — the reflex to imitate peer companies' acquisitions, expansions, or compensation practices regardless of whether they make business sense?

**Financial tenets** (four financial criteria, evaluated over four- or five-year averages, not single years):
7. Focus on return on equity, not earnings-per-share growth; adjust the ratio by holding marketable securities at cost and excluding capital gains/extraordinary items, and prefer businesses that earn good ROE without heavy leverage.
8. Calculate "owner earnings" (net income + depreciation/depletion/amortization − required capital expenditures) rather than relying on GAAP earnings or naive cash flow.
9. Look for high profit margins, and evidence that management works actively to control costs.
10. Check the "one-dollar premise": has the company created at least one dollar of market value for every dollar of retained (undistributed) earnings, over time?

**Value tenets** (two interrelated purchase-price criteria):
11. Determine the business's intrinsic value: forecast owner earnings over the business's useful life and discount them back at an appropriate rate (Buffett has used the long-term risk-free government bond rate, raised — e.g., to about 10% — when prevailing rates are unusually low, effectively widening the margin of safety).
12. Buy only when the current market price sits at a significant discount to that calculated intrinsic value — the margin of safety. Buffett has cited discounts on the order of 25% off intrinsic value as the kind of cushion he looks for; the exact discount is a judgment call scaled to the certainty of the earnings forecast.

**Focus-portfolio construction rule (applied after the checklist)**: limit the portfolio to companies you can genuinely track and understand — roughly ten to twenty positions ("more than twenty is asking for trouble"); size positions by conviction rather than equally, putting the largest weights (Buffett has suggested at least 10% of net worth for a top-conviction idea) behind the best few names; plan to hold five to ten years minimum, targeting an annual portfolio turnover rate of roughly 10–20%; and treat interim price volatility as noise to be tolerated, not a signal to act on.

## Risk

Risk control in this system is entirely upstream of position sizing: it comes from (a) restricting purchases to well-understood, predictable-earnings businesses (tenets 1–10), and (b) requiring a margin of safety between price and calculated intrinsic value before buying (tenets 11–12). There are no stops, and drawdown tolerance is managed through concentration discipline and multi-year holding periods rather than trade-level risk limits — the framework explicitly rejects broad diversification as a substitute for understanding what you own.

## Caveats

The checklist depends on subjective judgment at several points — how wide a discount counts as an adequate "margin of safety," what discount rate to use, and how far out to forecast owner earnings — Hagstrom does not supply a mechanical formula that removes analyst judgment from steps 11–12. Not every Buffett purchase satisfies all twelve tenets simultaneously (the book notes this explicitly); treat it as a weighting framework, not a strict pass/fail gate. The framework was built by reverse-engineering historical decisions and is therefore subject to hindsight/survivorship bias — it explains what Buffett's winning purchases had in common, not a tested, forward-looking selection rule.
