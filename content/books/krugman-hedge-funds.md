## Summary

Krugman discusses whether hedge funds coordinated speculative attacks during the 1998 crisis. He accepts that large funds shorted currencies and other assets, but treats claims of a single controlling conspiracy cautiously. The proposed cascade is a leveraged sequence: funds borrow yen, short Asian or commodity-linked currencies and stocks, invest in higher-yielding assets, then face margin calls and withdrawn credit lines when positions move against them (pp. 1-2). Forced unwinding can reinforce the original move, as yen-short covering raises the yen and causes more losses, while assets bought with leverage fall. Krugman’s emphasis is on balance-sheet mechanics and uncertainty about whether hedge funds were dominant or collusive actors.

## Key points

- The article cites Soros’s 1992 pound trade and other historical market episodes (p. 1).
- Australia’s dollar fell from nearly 80 U.S. cents in early 1997 to the low 60s by summer 1998 in the historical account (p. 1).
- The hypothesized cascade combines borrowing, short sales, crowded positions, and margin calls (pp. 1-2).
- Hong Kong’s defense and Russia’s collapse are presented as events that disrupted the proposed play (p. 2).
- The author says collusion and dominance are difficult to establish from available evidence.

## Actionable rules

1. Stress leveraged trades for funding withdrawal, margin calls, and forced-unwind feedback, not only for the initial price thesis.
2. Track cross-asset financing and crowded short exposure when assessing currency-crisis contagion.
3. Treat crisis narratives as hypotheses requiring data; this article does not provide a trading trigger.

## Caveats

This is a contemporary 1998 commentary, not a causal empirical study. Its crisis examples are historical and the proposed coordination is explicitly uncertain.

## Who it is for

Readers studying leverage, currency crises, and how forced liquidation can amplify market moves.
