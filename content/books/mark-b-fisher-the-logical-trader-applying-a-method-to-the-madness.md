## Overview

Mark Fisher, an independent floor trader and founder of MBF Clearing Corp. (the largest clearing firm on the NYMEX), wrote this book to formalize a method he had taught to roughly 4,000 traders over 15+ years, including floor brokers who later worked for Paul Tudor Jones. The book presents ACD (Point A, Point C, Point D), a rules-based framework for finding low-risk entries and hard price-defined exits, built around the day's opening range and a calculated pivot range. It is aimed at futures, stock, and commodity traders working intraday to multi-week time frames, and is organized around a single system rather than a survey of techniques.

## Core thesis

Profitable trading comes from always knowing, before entering a trade, the exact price at which you will admit you are wrong and get out — and from using objective, pre-calculated price levels (rather than opinion or news) to decide when the market has shown enough conviction to warrant a position. Fisher argues good reward/risk trades, not correct market predictions, are what separate the traders who survive from those who don't; the ACD method is his attempt to make that discipline "moron-proof" by turning it into a repeatable set of price rules.

## Key concepts

- **Opening range** — the high/low band established in the first few minutes (5–20 minutes for futures, longer for less liquid names) or first portion of the trading session; the baseline all other ACD levels are measured from.
- **Point A (A up / A down)** — a price level a fixed number of ticks above or below the opening range; the market must trade there for at least half the opening-range time frame to "confirm" an A. Only one A can be established per day (an A up rules out an A down and vice versa).
- **A value** — the instrument-specific tick distance used to plot Point A, calibrated to that market's typical volatility (given in an appendix table for various futures/stocks).
- **Point B** — the stop-loss level for an ACD trade taken off Point A: the opposite end of the opening range.
- **Pivot range** — a support/resistance band calculated from the prior period's high, low, and close ("the meat of the market"); price reaching, bouncing off, or slicing through it generates trade signals.
- **Point C (C up / C down)** — a price target measured a fixed tick distance beyond the opening range, usable only after an opposite-direction A has already been confirmed that day (e.g., a C down requires a prior A up); a valid Point C reversal signals a shift in intraday bias.
- **Point D** — the stop/exit level once Point C has been hit; once D is hit the trader is expected to stand aside for the rest of the session.
- **C value** — the tick distance used for Point C, distinct from the A value (in commodities; equal to it in stocks).
- **Rolling pivot (3-day, and longer)** — a pivot range recalculated on a rolling basis from the highest high, lowest low, and latest close of the last three (or more) trading days; used by swing traders for entries and trailing stops.
- **Longer-term pivot** — the same pivot-range formula applied to a multi-week window (e.g., the first two weeks of the year, or of July) to define support/resistance for the following half-year.
- **Rubber band trade** — a fade taken when price stretches toward but fails to reach an A/C target and snaps back, taken with a tight stop and small size.
- **Macro ACD / number line** — a daily score from −4 to +4 assigned to each session based on which ACD events occurred (A up/down, C up/down, close location); summed cumulatively over a rolling ~30-day window to gauge broader trend bias.
- **Pivot moving averages** — moving averages (e.g., 14-, 30-, 50-day) built on the daily pivot rather than the close, used mainly for their slope to judge whether the market's bias is trending, confused, or reversing (including a "moving-average fake-out" setup).
- **Plus day** — a day where price opens below the pivot range and closes above it (or the mirror case), used as a bullish/bearish tell for the next session.

## Rules and setups

The book's full opening-range/pivot-range mechanics and stop placement are detailed on the system sub-page [[mark-b-fisher-the-logical-trader-applying-a-method-to-the-madness--acd-method]]. In summary:

1. **A-up/A-down breakout**: go long above a confirmed A up, short below a confirmed A down; stop is Point B (opposite side of the opening range).
2. **A through the pivot**: an A up/down that also clears the daily pivot range is a higher-confidence entry; stop can be tightened to the far side of the pivot range instead of Point B, cutting risk substantially (book example: 40 ticks of risk via Point B versus 10 ticks via the pivot range).
3. **C-reversal trades**: after an opposite A is confirmed, a Point C hit signals a sentiment shift; entries are taken with Point D as the stop.
4. **Pivot-range fade / breakout**: trade a bounce off the pivot range with a stop on the other side of the range, or trade a "sliced through" break of the range in the breakout direction.
5. **Rubber-band trade**: fade a failed approach to an A/C level that snaps back, with a stop at the A/C price and small size (book example: risked 4 ticks to make 126).
6. **Rolling pivot / longer-term pivot**: for multi-day to multi-month holds, substitute a 3-day rolling pivot or a multi-week pivot range for the daily version, with wider A/C tick values scaled to the longer time frame.
7. **Time stop**: if price reaches a target level but the anticipated move doesn't develop, exit after a defined time window rather than waiting for the price stop to be hit.

No fixed percent-of-equity sizing formula is given; all stops in the base system are price levels (B, D, or the far side of the pivot range), not a percentage of account.

## Risk and money management

Fisher describes position sizing as performance-adaptive rather than fixed: at his firm, a trader on a hot streak may be allowed to increase size to "doubles and triples," while a trader in a slump has size cut by roughly a third to as much as half or two-thirds. Position limits reset to baseline at the start of each month regardless of the prior month's adjustments. Counter to instinct, the book recommends trading larger when markets are busy and volatile (where the edge is best expressed) and smaller when markets are quiet — the opposite of what Fisher says most traders do out of fear. Per-trade risk is always defined by a specific price level (Point B, Point D, or the pivot range boundary) established before entry, never by a fixed dollar or percentage rule; tighter stops (e.g., using the pivot range instead of Point B) are preferred when the setup allows it, letting size increase for the same dollar risk.

## Psychology and discipline

Discipline is treated as the deciding factor between traders who succeed with ACD and those who don't — several trader interviews in the book (Chapter 8) describe applying the system mechanically specifically to override emotional decision-making (e.g., holding losers too long, exiting winners too early). A recurring theme is that a trader must always know the exit price before entering, illustrated by a cautionary trading-pit anecdote (a trader who kept averaging into a losing position with no predefined stop and lost control). The book also stresses that the ACD system is meant to be used consistently rather than abandoned during emotionally charged periods, and that pattern-following without knowing "where you're wrong" is the single most common failure mode described by the interviewed traders.

## Chapter map

- Ch 1 — Know Your ACDs — opening range, Point A/B mechanics, A values, rubber-band trades.
- Ch 2 — The Pivot Concept — daily pivot range formula, pivot support/resistance strategies, three-day rolling pivot, trailing stops.
- Ch 3 — Putting It Together: ACD and Pivot Ranges — combining A/C signals with the pivot range for higher-confidence, lower-risk entries.
- Ch 4 — Macro ACD — the daily −4/+4 scoring system and the cumulative number line for gauging broader trend bias.
- "Logical Trader: Midterm" — a self-test recap of Chapters 1–4 before moving to intermediate/advanced material.
- Ch 5 — Pivot Moving Averages — moving averages built on the pivot price, slope analysis, moving-average fake-out setup.
- Ch 6 — The Advanced Trader — extending ACD to longer-term pivots and less common scenarios.
- Ch 7 — The ACD Version of "Ripley's Believe It or Not!" — real trading-floor anecdotes illustrating discipline, position sizing, and market shocks (e.g., the MG and crack-spread stories).
- Ch 8 — Trader Interviews — first-person accounts from traders who used ACD, focused on psychology and risk management.
- Appendix / Glossary — A and C value tables by market, formula reference, terminology.

## Strengths and caveats

The method is concrete and mechanical, with every entry paired to an explicit stop, which is unusual for a discretionary-trading book. It draws its evidence mostly from anecdote and worked historical chart examples rather than a formal backtest; no win rate, expectancy, or drawdown statistics are given for the system as a whole. The book is rooted in open-outcry pit trading (COMEX/NYMEX) in commodities and index futures circa the late 1990s/2000–2001, and its A/C tick values, opening-range lengths, and market examples are specific to that era's tick sizes and liquidity — they would need to be recalibrated for modern decimalized, electronic markets. The book does not address transaction-cost sensitivity for smaller/retail traders reproducing the higher-turnover setups.

## Who should read it

Futures and active-stock day traders and short-swing traders who want a fully price-defined intraday framework (entries, stops, and a bias filter) rather than pattern-based discretion, and who are willing to calibrate the A/C tick values to their own market before use.
