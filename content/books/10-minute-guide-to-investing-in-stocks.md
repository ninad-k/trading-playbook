## Summary

A 15-lesson introductory course aimed at complete newcomers to the stock market, structured as short "10 minute" chapters. It walks from confronting fear of investing and comparing stocks to bonds/cash/mutual funds, through the mechanics of what a stock is, the major exchanges, brokers and account types, order types, basic valuation, and simple long-term strategies, ending with how to read stock tables and market indices. No trading systems or technical analysis are covered; the focus is entirely on foundational mechanics and vocabulary for a first-time investor.

## Key points

- Compares stocks against bonds, cash, and mutual funds as competing places to park savings, favoring stocks for long-term growth.
- Explains company valuation basics, share classes (common vs. preferred), and the "two main issues" of stock (par value and market value).
- Covers derivatives briefly: subscription rights, warrants, and options, as instruments layered on top of stock ownership.
- Describes the major US markets (NYSE, AMEX, regional exchanges, OTC/NASDAQ) and international exchanges.
- Distinguishes full-service, discount, and e-brokers, and explains the SEC's regulatory role.
- Explains cash accounts vs. margin accounts, including how margin amplifies both gains and losses.
- Defines round lots (typically 100 shares) vs. odd lots, and the price/execution tradeoffs of each.
- Introduces the standard, trailing, and forward P/E ratios and how to interpret "high" (growth) vs. "low" (value) P/E stocks.
- Presents buy-and-hold, dollar cost averaging, and constant dollar averaging as low-maintenance strategies for smaller investors.
- Explains how to read stock tables, the ticker tape, and the major indices (Dow, NASDAQ Composite, S&P 500, AMEX, NYSE Composite, Russell, Wilshire 5000).

## Actionable rules

1. Round lots trade in blocks of 100 shares (10 for some issues); buying in round lots typically gets a better price per share than odd lots.
2. A limit order to buy is placed below the current price; a limit order to sell is placed above it — the trade only executes if the market reaches that price.
3. A sell stop order is placed below the current price to protect a gain or cap a loss; the book illustrates trailing a sell stop upward as a stock rises (e.g., from $9 to $11 as the stock climbs from $10 to $12).
4. P/E ratios of roughly 10–20 are described as "normal"; under 10 is a "low" (often value/mature) P/E, and high-growth names can trade up to 100x earnings.
5. Dollar cost averaging: invest a fixed dollar amount at fixed intervals regardless of price, which mechanically buys more shares when price is low and fewer when price is high.

## Caveats

Written in 2000/2001 for a US retail audience; brokerage examples (full-service vs. discount vs. e-broker), commission structures, and some market-structure details (specialist system, regional exchanges) are dated relative to the modern all-electronic, near-zero-commission market. No coverage of technical analysis, short selling mechanics beyond a passing mention, or risk management beyond general diversification advice — this is a mechanics-and-vocabulary primer, not a trading strategy guide.

## Who it is for

First-time stock investors who need to learn the vocabulary and account mechanics (order types, margin, P/E, indices) before opening a brokerage account, not traders looking for entry/exit rules.
