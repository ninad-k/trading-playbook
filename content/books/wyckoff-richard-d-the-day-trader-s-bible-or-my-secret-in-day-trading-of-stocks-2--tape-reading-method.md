## What it is

A discretionary, transaction-level trading method built entirely from reading the sequence of prices and volumes printed on the tape (in modern terms, time-and-sales / order-flow data), rather than from charts, news, or fundamentals. The trader identifies which of several related "leader" stocks is showing the clearest, most decisive trend, trades only in that trend's direction, and manages risk through tight, price-derived stops that trail as "points of resistance" form.

## Rules

**Pre-trade selection**: identify the current leader stock(s) in a related group (e.g., a sector or a small set of actively traded issues) — the one showing the clearest directional force and largest volume relative to its normal activity. Avoid stocks/markets confined to a narrow range with no clear trend; a market swinging within only a couple of points cannot pay its own costs.

**Reading the tape for direction**:
- Rising prices on increasing/large volume with only fractional resistance = demand in control; look to go long or stay long.
- A stock that fails to rally on good news, or breaks decisively on a "thrust" of large-volume selling after a period of narrow, repeated trading at the same fractions, signals supply overwhelming demand; look to go short.
- Large volume absorbed within a narrow price band (little net price change despite heavy transactions) signals a point where accumulation or distribution is occurring, and often precedes a change in trend once the absorption is complete.
- A related "leader" turning first (e.g., a related stock breaking a support level while your position's stock has not yet moved) is treated as an early warning to close or reverse, even before your own stock's tape confirms it.

**Entry**: enter in the direction of the immediate trend once the tape gives a clear indication (a decisive break through a repeatedly-tested point of resistance, on volume, in the direction of the group's leaders). Do not enter, or average into, a position against the prevailing tape direction ("averaging... is groping for the top or bottom").

**Initial stop**: place the stop just beyond the most recent, most-tested point of resistance (e.g., 1/8 to 1/4 point beyond a level the price has turned at multiple times) rather than an arbitrary fixed distance. If no clear point of resistance is available (e.g., away from the tape, carrying overnight, or a data disruption), fall back to an arbitrary stop of roughly two points maximum loss, or start an automatic stop one point from entry.

**Automatic trailing variant**: for every 1/4 point the trade moves favorably, tighten the stop so it is never more than one point from the current extreme price. Once the stop reaches breakeven plus commissions, switch off the mechanical trailing and let the market's own points of resistance define the stop going forward (mechanical trailing is described as interfering with judgment once the trade is no longer in its early, most dangerous phase).

**Adding to the position**: only in the same direction as an already-profitable trade, and only when the tape reconfirms strength (a new point of resistance forming higher, on continued volume) — never to lower the average cost of a losing position.

**Exit (close the trade) when any of these occur**:
1. The tape itself signals a trend change (the same kind of volume/price evidence used for entry, in reverse) — this is the primary, most important signal.
2. The trailing stop is hit.
3. The position/trend becomes unclear or indecisive.
4. A satisfactory profit exists and capital would be better redeployed into a clearer opportunity elsewhere.

**Reward-to-risk filter**: because round-trip costs (commissions plus the bid/ask "invisible eighth") run close to half a point, only take setups with a probable payoff on the order of two to five points or more; skip anything narrower.

## Risk

Cap the loss on any single trade at a small, fixed amount relative to account size — Wyckoff's own consensus figure is roughly two points maximum gross loss per trade as a fallback, tightened whenever a closer, tape-derived stop (a recent point of resistance) is available. Never widen a stop once set; it may only be moved to reduce risk or lock in profit. Reduce position size (down to as little as ten shares in Wyckoff's example) after a losing streak to restore judgment quality, rather than trading through it at full size. Keep a stop working at all times when not actively watching the tape, including overnight — Wyckoff explicitly warns against leaving a position unprotected even briefly (his cautionary anecdote: a trader who left for lunch without a stop and returned to a $5,000 loss on a ten-point adverse move).

## Caveats

This is a fully discretionary method with no fixed indicator formulas — success depends on the trader's trained judgment in reading volume/price patterns, which the book itself says takes months to years of full-time practice to develop ("he should have no other business or profession"). It assumes access to real-time, transaction-level tape and the ability to act on it in seconds, which does not map cleanly onto modern electronic markets with vastly higher message rates, decimalized pricing, and algorithmic order flow; the specific price increments (eighths) and floor-trading mechanics referenced throughout are obsolete. No formal backtest or win-rate statistic is provided — the compounding-growth illustration in the parent book is arithmetic demonstration, not audited results.
