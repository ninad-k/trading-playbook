## What you will be able to do

You will be able to name the common chart patterns — double tops, head and shoulders, triangles, flags,
wedges, cup and handle — and, more usefully, describe each one in terms of the swing highs, swing lows,
and breaks of structure you learned to spot in stage 3. You will also be able to state, for any pattern
you point at on a chart, the exact price level that would invalidate it.

## The lesson

Every named chart pattern is something you already know how to see. It is not a new skill sitting on
top of structure — it is structure, described with a picture instead of a sentence. The catalogue of
patterns exists because certain arrangements of swings recur often enough that traders gave them names.
Learn to translate the name back into the structure underneath it, and you stop needing the catalogue.

### Double tops and double bottoms

A double top is nothing more than a failed higher high. Price makes a swing high, pulls back to a swing
low, rallies again, and fails to clear the first high by any meaningful margin. In stage 3 terms: an
uptrend that stops making higher highs. The "top" is just the second swing high sitting at roughly the
same level as the first, and the pattern completes — in the conventional reading — when price breaks
below the swing low between the two tops, which is the same break-of-structure event you already know
how to spot, just to the downside. A double bottom is the mirror image: a failed lower low. Nothing about
either pattern requires new vocabulary. What makes it nameable rather than just "a failure to extend the
trend" is that it happened twice at a similar level, which is what makes the level look like resistance
or support rather than coincidence.

### Head and shoulders

A head and shoulders top is three swing highs, where the middle one is higher than the two either side of
it, sitting on top of a trend that is losing the ability to make higher highs and higher lows. Read as
structure: the left shoulder is a normal swing high in an uptrend. The head is one more higher high — the
trend still working. The right shoulder is where the trend fails to make a higher high at all, and the
low between the head and the right shoulder — conventionally called the neckline — is the swing low that,
if broken, completes the same kind of break of structure you learned in stage 3, just with two shoulders
of context either side of it instead of one. An inverse head and shoulders is the same read upside down,
built from a downtrend that stops making lower lows.

### The triangle family

A triangle is a range whose swings are converging. You already know a range as a market bouncing between
a swing high and a swing low with no new extremes; a triangle is that same range, except the highs are
getting lower, or the lows are getting higher, or both. A symmetrical triangle is a range compressing
from both sides — indecision, in the sense that neither buyers nor sellers are extending the range. An
ascending triangle has a flat top and rising lows: buyers are stepping in earlier each time, willing to
pay more, while sellers keep defending the same ceiling — read as mildly bullish because one side of the
range is visibly losing ground. A descending triangle is the same logic reversed: a flat floor and
falling highs, sellers pressing while buyers keep pulling back. In every case, the pattern resolves the
same way a range always resolves: with a break of structure through one side.

### Flags, pennants, and the pullback

A flag is a pullback that has gone sideways or slightly against the trend for long enough to look like
its own small range, after a sharp directional move. If you can already tell a pullback from a reversal —
which is a stage 3 skill — a flag is simply a pullback with visible, roughly parallel structure to it. A
pennant is the same pullback compressing into a small symmetrical triangle instead of a parallel channel.
Neither is a different phenomenon from the pullback you already know how to read; they are the same
event with enough bars in it to draw lines around.

### Wedges and cup and handle

A wedge looks like a triangle but slants against the direction it eventually breaks: a rising wedge
grinds higher on both trendlines while momentum quietly fades, and is read as bearish because the range
is narrowing even as price appears to climb; a falling wedge is the same compression grinding lower and
is read as bullish. A cup and handle is a rounded low — a gradual, curved swing low rather than a sharp
one — followed by a small pullback (the handle) that behaves exactly like a flag. All of it is still
swings, ranges, and pullbacks. The names describe the shape; the structure underneath is what you already
learned to read.

### The measured move

Most of these patterns come with a conventional price target, and the logic behind it is the same in
every case: take the height of the move that built the pattern, and project that same distance from the
point where the pattern breaks. A head and shoulders target subtracts the head-to-neckline distance from
the neckline break. A triangle target adds (or subtracts) the height of the triangle's widest point from
the breakout. A flag target adds the length of the "flagpole" — the sharp move before the flag — to the
breakout of the flag. This is called a measured move, and the idea behind it is simply that a move often
repeats its own scale once it resumes. That is a reasonable starting assumption for where to take profit
or where to expect resistance — it is not a law of price, and there is no mechanism that forces a market
to travel exactly as far the second time as the first.

### The honest part

Pattern recognition is subjective. Give the same chart to five traders and you will get more than one
answer for what pattern is on it, where it starts, and whether it is even finished — because a pattern is
a story fitted to a shape, and shapes are ambiguous until price has already moved past them. This is also
why patterns look far more obvious in a book than they do on the right edge of a live chart: a textbook
head and shoulders is chosen after the fact, from the small number of times the shape appeared and worked.
On the right edge, in real time, you cannot know yet whether the right shoulder will fail to make a
higher high or whether it is about to blow past the head. The published "reliability" percentages you
will see attached to these patterns in books and articles — this pattern works 70% of the time, that one
80% — are not the result of anything you should treat as robust evidence. They typically come from a
small, hand-picked, hindsight-selected sample of chart examples, not from a controlled test across a
large number of markets and time periods with a precise, repeatable rule for what counts as the pattern
in the first place. Treat every such number as marketing, not as measurement.

None of that means patterns are useless. It means the pattern itself is not the edge — the discipline you
bring to using it is. The single most useful question you can ask about any pattern you think you see is:
what would have to happen for this to be wrong? For a double top, that is a clean break above the second
high. For a triangle, it is a break the wrong way with the swing structure violated, not just a poke
through a trendline. If you cannot answer that question with a specific price, you have not identified a
pattern — you have described a picture.

## Worked example

Suppose a stock has been climbing for three weeks, making higher highs and higher lows the way you
learned to track in stage 3. It makes a swing high at 110, pulls back to a swing low at 104, and rallies
again — but this time stalls at 111 instead of pushing meaningfully higher, then turns down. You now have
two highs at roughly the same level with a swing low between them. That is a double top by name. Read as
structure: the uptrend has, for the first time in three weeks, failed to make a clear higher high. The
level that would invalidate the pattern is 111 — a close back above it and the "failure" reading is wrong,
the uptrend is intact, and you were looking at a pause, not a top. The level that would confirm it is 104
— a break of that swing low completes the same break-of-structure event from stage 3, now with the extra
context that it followed a failed higher high. The conventional measured-move target subtracts the
110-to-104 range (6 points) from the break at 104, projecting to roughly 98. Notice that every part of
this reasoning — the swing high, the swing low, the break, the projection — was available to you before
you ever heard the term "double top." The name organized the observation; it did not add information you
did not already have.

### Drill: Name it, then break it

Pull up 15 charts — any instrument, any timeframe, your choice — and for each one, find one classical
pattern forming or recently completed (a double top, a triangle, a flag, whatever you can find). For each
pattern, write down two things only: the exact price level that would invalidate it, and the swing
structure underneath it in one sentence, the way this lesson described the double top. Do not write down
a trade idea. The point of the drill is proving you can translate a named pattern back into structure and
name its failure level, not picking winners.

## Self-check

1. In your own words, what swing-structure event is a double top actually a failure to produce?
2. Why is an ascending triangle read differently from a descending triangle, given that both are ranges
   with converging swings?
3. Where does a measured-move target come from, and what is it actually assuming about the market?
4. Why are published pattern "reliability" percentages not something you should treat as robust, and what
   question should you ask instead of trusting one?
